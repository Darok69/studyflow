Who is who in the textbook examples

Clara owner · Hector thief · Leander bona fide buyer · Midas depositary · Rusticus landowner · Lea lessee. The same names come back across chapters, so link the cases in your head.

Ius controversum

Jurists often disagree on the same case (Trebatius vs Labeo, Sabinians vs Proculians, Julian vs Ulpian). In an exegesis, naming the opposing opinion and the interests behind each view earns points.

Persons in power

Slaves and children in patria potestas own nothing themselves but acquire for the dominus / pater familias, through an order (iussum) or a peculium.

Possession I — concept and types

book pp. 1–24
class: Property; possession

The formula

animus possidendi
intent to control
+
corpus
control: the thing is within your sphere
=
POSSESSIO
a fact, not a right
  • corpus needs no touching: oculis et affectu suffices (D 41.2.1.21). But it is never purely symbolic.
  • Correct Latin: possession is acquired animo et corpore.
  • Why it matters: the possessor is stronger in a dispute (the other side must prove its right), and possession is the basis of ownership.

Types of holding — the tree

factual control of a thing
Proprietary possession
animus rem sibi habendi (for oneself). Owner, thief, bona fide buyer.
Detention = naturalis possessio
animus rem alteri habendi (for another). Tenant, lessee, depositary, precarist. A holder sui iuris.
Possessor's agent
a person alieni iuris (slave Achilles taking sheep to market) = the master's "extended arm".
Immediate: control yourself or through persons in your power.
Mediate: through a detentor sui iuris.
Clara → Midas → Hector: Clara deposits her vase with Midas = Clara mediate possessor, Midas detentor. Hector steals it → both lose it, Hector becomes possessor (never owner).

Two qualities of possession

iusta possessio

Unflawed towards the opponent: nec vi nec clam nec precario. Judged only relationally (alter ab altero).

→ interdict protection

possessio ex iusta causa

Civil possession: based on a valid title (sale, gift, loan, dowry).

→ ownership (authorised transferor) or usucapio (unauthorised) p. 10

Leander (bona fide buyer) has both qualities. Hector (thief) has neither towards Clara, but he is protected against a robber who robs him.

Possessory interdicts

  • Praetorian orders that stop dispossession and escalating conflict; protection is only temporary. The right itself needs an actio in rem.
  • Prohibitory: vim fieri veto · restitutory: restituas
  • exceptio vitiosae possessionis: no protection for someone who got the thing vi, clam, precario from the opponent.
  • vim vi repellere licet: a rare case of permitted self-help.
  • Some detentors are protected too: emphyteuta, precarist, pledgee, sequester.

Excursus: furtum

  • Taking another's movable with intent to enrich oneself (includes embezzlement and keeping found property). No theft of land.
  • Intent alone is not enough: an external act is needed (the depositary steals only once he hides the thing).
  • The thief is a possessor (original acquisition), but has no civil possession and no ownership; res furtivae cannot be usucapted (lex Atinia) p. 10
  • actio furti (penalty ×4 / ×2) + condictio furtiva (thing or value). The condictio furtiva and the rei vindicatio cannot be combined: choose one.

Possession II — how possession is acquired

book pp. 25–54
stricter for acquiring than for retaining

Who can acquire?

  • Persons sui iuris with enough understanding; furiosi cannot.
  • pupillus: yes, once he has intellectus (Paulus D 41.2.1.3)
  • Persons in power acquire only for the dominus.

Original vs derivative

  • Original: by your own act (occupatio of a res nullius, theft).
  • Derivative: handed over (traditio).
  • Derivative needs less corpus than original.

How much corpus? Three factors

  • the mode of acquisition (original is stricter)
  • the type of thing (land is easier than movables; small handy things are easy)
  • the social environment (high risk from third parties → a clearly visible act is needed)

Tradition surrogates (change without handover)

Brevi manu: a borrower or lessee buys the thing (Gaius D 41.1.9.5). Constitutum: a woman gives away land and leases it back (Ulpian D 6.1.77). They mirror each other.

Wine barrel case · Ulpian D 18.6.1.2

Buyer seals barrels in the seller's cellar. Labeo/Ulpian: no possession, because sealing only prevents swapping and shows no animus possidendi. Trebatius: yes. Contrast: marking timber does give possession.

Lesson: intent to buy ≠ intent to possess. Contract ≠ conveyance p. 9. Also a textbook example of ius controversum.

Acquiring through others

  • Persons in power (alieni iuris): animo nostro, corpore alieno (Paulus D 41.2.3.12). The master's animus comes from an order (iussum) or a peculium; later approval (ratihabitio) also works.
  • Persons sui iuris: classical law has only indirect agency (the agent acquires, then hands over). Exceptions: procurator, tutor, curator. Justinian later allows direct agency in general.
  • nemo sibi ipse causam possessionis mutare potest: you cannot change your own title by yourself. Surrogates are fine because both parties agree.

Ch. IV · 14 Oct

Possession III — retaining and losing possession

book pp. 55–67
tendency: keep existing possession

Decision tree: is possession still there?

Has the possessor given up his animus? → if yes: possession ends. If no:
Movable
Test = custodia: can he lay hands on it at any time?
  • mislaid in your own house → kept (D 41.2.3.13)
  • lost outside your sphere → lost (D 41.2.25 pr)
  • forgotten buried money → kept while nobody interferes
Fugitive slave (servus fugitivus)
Exception: the master keeps possession solo animo until a third party takes the slave; he even keeps usucapting. Things the slave takes along and acquires go to the master too.
Land
Can be kept solo animo (at first only seasonal pastures; Proculus: all land), but only with a view to returning.
  • through detentors: kept even if they die or leave; lost at once if they are driven out by force (D 41.2.44.2)
Stones in the Tiber · Ulpian D 41.2.13 pr — stones sink in a shipwreck and are later recovered. Possession lost (no control at all), ownership kept. Shows the custodia test. Contrast the servus fugitivus. (model exegesis for 14 Oct)
Going to market · Ulpian (Labeo) D 41.2.6.1 — B leaves his estate empty, A occupies it. Ulpian (prevailing view): B loses possession only if he cannot drive A out when he returns. Labeo: A possesses as soon as he enters.

Possession lost ≠ ownership lost

  • Goods thrown overboard in an emergency: possession lost, ownership kept. In doubt, there is no dereliction.
  • A finder who keeps obvious lost property commits furtum.
  • Exception: a wild animal that regains its freedom → ownership ends too p. 11

The intruder rule

A occupies B's land intending to possess. A acquires only if B does not try to regain it, or fails. If B regains control, B was the possessor all along. Afterwards B has: interdicts uti possidetis / unde vi, the actio publiciana and the rei vindicatio p. 13

Other rules worth one sentence

  • A change counts only if it is likely to be permanent (Iavolenus D 41.2.22).
  • pupilli and furiosi cannot give up possession by intent alone, without tutor or curator.
  • A detentor's mental illness, or a sublease, has no effect.

Exegesis: possession fragments — ask

  • Who has which kind of holding: proprietary possession, detention, possessor's agent? Immediate or mediate?
  • animus and corpus present at the decisive moment? Original or derivative? Capacity?
  • Which corpus factors: type of thing, oculis et affectu, keys, surroundings? A surrogate? Acquisition through others?
  • Retention: custodia, movable or land, servus fugitivus, intruder, expulsion by force?
  • iusta towards the opponent? Civil? Which interdict?

Ownership — dominium

book pp. 68–76
the full right in rem

What the owner may do

  • use the thing, exclude everyone else, take all fruits
  • change or destroy it; grant limited rights (pledge, servitude)
  • alienate it, authorise others to dispose, abandon it (dereliction → res derelicta), free slaves (manumissio)

Limits

  • ius cogens: mandatory law, e.g. no gifts between spouses; Augustus' 70-foot building height limit
  • cautio damni infecti: security a neighbour can demand when a crumbling building threatens his
  • neighbour restrictions = "legal servitudes" p. 15

Fruits (first look)

Periodic yields from normal use. fructus naturales (milk, wool, crops) and fructus civiles (rent). They belong to the owner in principle. Who acquires them, and when p. 11

The ladder of rights

dominium
full right · rei vindicatio
iura in re aliena (limited rights in another's thing)
servitude · usufruct · pledge · emphyteusis · superficies
possessio (no right at all, a fact)
interdicts only · but it can grow into ownership

Interdicts protect possession temporarily. The rei vindicatio decides ownership for good.

Two kinds of ownership

quiritebonitarian
sourceius civilepraetor (ius honorarium)
typical casemancipatio, usucapiores mancipi by mere traditio with a causa

becomes quirite—after 1 year (movables) / 2 years (land)

Joint ownership

consortium ercto non cito

The old community of heirs after the pater familias dies. Property undivided, no shares; one member can alienate for all. Ended by the actio familiae erciscundae.

condominium

Classical form (communio pro indiviso). Each owner has an ideal share (quota) and may dispose of it freely; disposing of the whole needs everyone.

Leo + Irene co-own slave Pamphilus. Leo mancipates him to Crassus → Crassus gets only Leo's half. He can free Pamphilus only with Irene's consent or after buying her half.

End: actio communi dividundo → divide in kind, or one co-owner takes all and pays the others, or sell and split the proceeds.

Exegesis: ownership fragments — ask

  • Is the issue ownership or mere possession?
  • Full ownership or a limited right? Quirite or bonitarian?
  • Which power is exercised; does ius cogens limit it?
  • Joint owners? Remedy: rei vindicatio or interdict?

How ownership is acquired — the overview tree

book pp. 77–81
three categories, two directions
ACQUIRING OWNERSHIP

① By conveyance → derivative

The owner (or an authorised person) transfers; the acquirer steps into the owner's position.

  • mancipatio: formal, abstract, res mancipi
  • in iure cessio: formal, abstract, any thing
  • traditio: informal, causal

nemo plus iuris transferre potest quam ipse habet (Ulpian D 41.1.20 pr): encumbrances pass on (a usufruct stays); a non-owner transfers nothing.

p. 9

② By usucapio → original

Qualified possession held for the time set by law. Cures transfers that failed:

  • form defect (res mancipi only handed over)
  • transferor not owner, lacks capacity, or no valid causa
  • dormant inheritance (usucapio pro herede)

The former owner loses his right with no claim to compensation.

p. 10

③ "Natural" modes → original

Gaius bases them on naturalis ratio (natural reason):

  • occupatio: take a res nullius (wild boar killed → owner)
  • fruits: the owner of the mother thing, with four exceptions
  • accessio: inseparable connection → owner of the main thing
  • confusio/commixtio: nothing changes, each keeps his share
  • specificatio: a new thing → Sabinians vs Proculians → media sententia

pp. 11–12

Why the distinction matters

  • Derivative: you get only what the transferor had → check his right first.
  • Original: a new right arises; the old one dies. The former owner is left with claims in personam (compensation, condictio).

How the three link up

A traditio by a non-owner fails as a derivative transfer → the buyer gets civil possession → after 1 or 2 years, usucapio makes him owner → meanwhile the actio publiciana protects him p. 14

Two Latin anchors

  • nemo plus iuris… → derivative limit
  • per traditionem quoque iure naturali res nobis adquiruntur (Inst 2.1.40) → under Justinian traditio works for every thing

The mancipatio formula

Hunc ego hominem ex iure Quiritium meum esse aio isque mihi emptus esto hoc aere aeneaque libra — the buyer grasps the slave, strikes the scales with a coin; the seller is silent.

The causa dispute — Julian vs Ulpian

Julian D 41.1.36
(1) A gives land thinking he owes it under a will, B thinks under a stipulation → owner.
(2) C gives money as a gift, D takes it as a loan → owner: both want ownership to pass.
Ulpian D 12.1.18 pr
Same facts (gift vs loan) → no derivative acquisition, because there is no agreed causa. D becomes owner only by spending or mixing the coins (original). Before that C can vindicate, afterwards condictio; but exceptio doli, since C wanted to give.

Julian's view → the abstract model of § 929 BGB.

Traditio — the three gates (all must be passed)

1

Authority

Owner, or someone authorised to dispose. Otherwise nemo plus iuris → only possession → usucapio.

2

Possession passes

Physically, brevi manu, constitutum possessorium, oculis et affectu. No symbolic transfer p. 4

3

iusta causa

A ground the law recognises (titulus): sale, loan for consumption (mutuum), gift (donatio), dowry.

Pass all three → owner (for a res mancipi: bonitarian). Fail gate 1 → usucapient. Fail gate 3 → possibly the condictio.

Iavolenus D 39.5.25 — T is told to give E's thing to Titius in E's name, but gives it in his own name. Titius is not owner (gate 1 fails), but keeps the thing: benignius est → exceptio doli against E's rei vindicatio. Related maxim: venire contra factum proprium.

  • Gate 1: owner, agent, or someone exceeding his authority?
  • Gate 2: how was possession transferred? Is it a res mancipi?
  • Gate 3: agreement on the causa (Julian vs Ulpian)?
  • Fallbacks: original acquisition, condictio, exceptio doli, usucapio.

Usucapio — ownership through time

book pp. 99–125
original acquisition of civil ownership

Why it exists

  • stops possession and ownership drifting apart for ever
  • rewards the good-faith possessor once the time is up
  • original: the former owner loses his right

What it cures

  • form defect: a res mancipi only handed over → bonitarian owner → quirite after the time
  • defect in the transferor: (a) no ownership or authority (nemo plus iuris), (b) no capacity (pupillus, furiosus, prodigus), (c) no valid causa

Bonitarian owner ≠ every usucapient

Only someone who got a res mancipi from an authorised transferor (mere form defect) is a bonitarian owner. Someone who bought from a non-owner is a mere usucapient. Both use the actio publiciana p. 14

The five requirements — res habilis · titulus · bona fides · possessio · tempus

1

res habilis

The thing can be usucapted. Not: things outside commerce (market square, temple); stolen things (lex Atinia); things taken by force, land included (leges Iulia et Plautia).

2

titulus

A causa that would have given ownership if the transferor had been authorised (= iusta causa traditionis). Putative title (the buyer only believes there is one) is disputed: strict Ulpian · middle view · liberal: tolerabilis error.

3

bona fides

An error of fact at the moment of acquisition. An error of law excludes good faith. Only concrete knowledge harms; mere doubts do not. mala fides superveniens non nocet

4

possessio

Unflawed (not vi, clam, precario) and uninterrupted. Possession through a detentor is enough. If it is lost, the period starts again.

5

tempus

1 year movables · 2 years land. A buyer starts again from zero (adding the seller's time is post-classical); an heir continues the deceased's period.

Paulus D 41.4.2.15 — two buy from a minor without his tutor. Buyer 1 thinks he is of age (fact) → can usucapt. Buyer 2 thinks minors may sell (law) → cannot.

Lex Atinia and reversio in potestatem

Stolen things cannot be usucapted by anyone, even in good faith, until they return into the owner's power (he regains direct custody and knows it is his). No effect on land (land cannot be stolen). Pledgor steals his own pledge back: disputed (Cassius/Paulus vs Labeo).

Exegesis: usucapio — ask

  • First: why did the derivative acquisition fail?
  • Then the 5 gates: lex Atinia? title or putative title? error of fact or of law? flawless, uninterrupted possession? time complete?
  • Meanwhile: actio publiciana.

Modern contrast (detail): bona fide acquisition gives ownership at once, with no waiting period, mostly for movables (§ 367 ABGB, § 935 BGB). Roman law instead treats stolen things as impossible to usucapt.

Natural modes I — occupatio and fruits

book pp. 126–131
original acquisition

Occupatio = taking a res nullius into possession

Originally ownerless

wild animals, pearls from the seabed

res derelictae

abandoned with intent to give up ownership. A jettisoned cargo is not abandoned.

thesaurus (treasure)

Sabinians: res nullius. Hadrian: half to the finder, half to the landowner.

Wild animals

  • Ownership needs open and secure possession (a strict corpus test, see D 41.1.5.1 p. 4).
  • The animal regains its natural freedom → possession and ownership are lost.
  • Enclosure or fish pond: owned · fenced forest or open lake: not.
  • consuetudo revertendi: a tamed animal stays owned while it keeps coming back (bees, pigeons). Pets that run away are not ownerless.

Lost ≠ ownerless

A finder can neither occupy nor usucapt lost property: it is not ownerless, he has no iusta causa and usually no good faith. He should announce the find publicly, or he risks furtum.

A thief gets no wool from stolen sheep; a bona fide buyer does. A slave woman's child (partus ancillae) is not a fruit; the young of animals are.

Disputed: the usucapient who later learns the truth

Pomponius/Paulus: he keeps usucapting (mala fides superveniens non nocet) but gets no more fruits. Julian: good faith at acquisition covers both, until the owner sues.

Link: fruits come back in the rei vindicatio

After litis contestatio every defendant must restore fruits taken and fruits he failed to take p. 14

Natural modes II — accessio, mixing, specificatio

book pp. 132–151
class: acquisition through accessio

Step 1 — classify the case

what happenedmodeeffect
two things joined inseparablyaccessioowner of the main thing
joined but can be separated—nothing changes
liquids blended / solids mixedconfusio/commixtionothing changes
a new kind of thing madespecificatiomedia sententia
just cleaned or processed—nothing changes

Titus' wool: made into clothes = specificatio · cleaned = processing · dyed with someone's purple = accessio (still Titus').

accessio cedit principali

  • Inseparable = separating would cause considerable damage or effort. Welding (ferruminatio) is inseparable; soldering (adplumbatio) is not.
  • Main thing = the one that can be vindicated on its own; value and labour do not count (purple on wool).
  • Good or bad faith of the person joining does not matter for ownership.

Land always wins — superficies solo cedit

  • implantatio (once it takes root) · satio (once sown) · alluvio (deposits) · aedificatio (on a foundation; a movable granary stays the builder's)
  • Building is special: the materials are only latently joined. dominium dormiens revives if the house is demolished.
  • Building on another's land knowingly → only ius tollendi (right to remove).
  • Building on your own land with another's materials → actio de tigno iuncto (double value).
Gai. 2.78 — painting on another's panel (tabula picta): the painter owns it, an exception for artistic work. But writing on another's parchment belongs to the owner of the parchment. Panel owner: exceptio doli, actio utilis. Gaius himself doubts the reasoning. (model exegesis for 28 Oct)
Paulus D 6.1.23.5 — arm joined to a statue: soldered → the arm's owner keeps it (actio ad exhibendum → rei vindicatio). Welded → the statue's owner acquires it; the arm's owner gets an actio in factum.
Celsus D 6.1.38 — bona fide builder: the owner vindicates the land but must reimburse expenses, up to the increase in value. A poor owner may let him remove the building instead. neque malitiis indulgendum est

Specificatio — the school dispute

Sabinians

owner of the material: sine materia nulla species effici possit

Proculians

the maker: a new res nullius, quod factum est, antea nullius fuerat

⇣
media sententia (Gaius D 41.1.7.7)

Reversible (metal vessel → can be melted) → owner of the material. Irreversible (ship, clothes, wine, oil, mead) → the maker, who must compensate. Threshing grain creates nothing new.

Mixing

  • Without consent: each owner keeps his share → vindicatio pro parte (2 t + 4 t of wheat).
  • With consent: co-ownership → actio communi dividundo.
  • Money is the exception: whoever mixes coins becomes sole owner, even a thief. The loser has the condictio (condictio furtiva against a thief).

Who lost his thing — what now?

Good faith → actio in factum · theft → condictio furtiva · still possessing → exceptio doli until paid p. 13

Protection I — procedure and the rei vindicatio

book pp. 152–167
ubi rem meam invenio, ibi vindico

Formulary procedure — two stages

IN IURE — before the praetor

He grants an action from his edict, creates one (in factum, utilis), or refuses (denegatio actionis). He does not check the facts.

➜
litis contestatio

The formula is issued: action + any exceptiones = a conditional judgment: "if it is proven, condemn; if not, acquit".

➜
APUD IUDICEM — before the iudex

A private citizen establishes the facts and condemns or acquits. omnis condemnatio pecuniaria est: every condemnation is in money.

Anatomy of the rei vindicatio formula

partcontent
intentiosi paret rem … ex iure Quiritium Auli Agerii esse — the basis of the claim; the plaintiff must prove it
(exceptio)inserted right after the intentio; the defendant must prove it
clausula arbitrarianeque ea res restituetur — the order to restore pushes the defendant to give the thing back
condemnatioquanti ea res erit — pay the value. If he refuses out of malice → the plaintiff fixes the value on oath (iusiurandum in litem)

Who sues whom?

  • Plaintiff: only the quirite owner, who must prove it. A chain of owners back to an original acquisition is the probatio diabolica. Bonitarian owner → actio publiciana.
  • Defendant: any possessor; Ulpian: any natural possessor who is able to restore.
  • Fictitious possessors: pro possessione dolus est, i.e. someone who defends while not possessing, or gives up possession after litis contestatio.
  • Preparation: interdicts decide who is defendant (uti possidetis for land, utrubi for movables); actio ad exhibendum.

After litis contestatio: what must be restored?

Principle of restituere: the plaintiff is put where he would be if the thing had been returned at litis contestatio.

issuerule
fruits after LCfruits taken + fruits that should have been taken; good or bad faith does not matter
fruits before LCbona fide possessor keeps the separated fruits; a bad-faith possessor commits furtum
thing destroyed after LC3 models: possession-based (not liable unless dolus) · Proculians: always liable (even casus) · Sabinians: liable for dolus + culpa
expensesbona fide possessor: value-increasing expenses → retains the thing via exceptio doli; builds after learning the truth → only ius tollendi
Paulus D 6.1.16.1 — the ship: after LC the defendant sends the ship to sea in the sailing season and it sinks → not liable (no fault). He would be liable if he chose an unsuitable crew (culpa in eligendo).

Defences — the exceptio

  • exceptio doli: si in ea re nihil dolo malo Auli Agerii factum sit neque fiat — past fraud (praeteriti) or suing now is itself bad faith (praesentis).
  • exceptio rei venditae et traditae: the seller who is still quirite owner sues the buyer he sold and delivered to p. 14

Protection II — publiciana, pleas, negatoria

book pp. 168–177

Three actions protecting ownership

actionwhoagainst
rei vindicatioquirite owner without possessionpossessor
actio publicianausucapient, bonitarian owner (also a quirite owner, to avoid the probatio diabolica)possessor
actio negatoriaowneranyone who claims a servitude or otherwise interferes (smell, steam, stone chips)

Without a claimed right → interdictum quod vi aut clam.

Actio publiciana — the fiction

The plaintiff is treated as if the usucapion period had run (1 or 2 years). Only the intentio differs from the rei vindicatio. He must prove traditio on a iusta causa; the model formula is purchase.

All requirements of usucapio except the time p. 10

Pomponius (Julian) D 21.3.2: T buys S's land from Titius. Titius then inherits from S and sells and delivers the land to M. → T's actio publiciana against M succeeds: the defect is cured, T is bonitarian owner, Titius keeps only nudum ius Quiritium.

➜

➜
Buyer: exceptio rei venditae et traditae (or doli)
➜
Buyer wins

Causa other than sale → exceptio / replicatio doli.

Double sale by unauthorised sellers

Neratius (D 19.1.31.2): the buyer who got delivery first wins (by analogy with the owner's double sale). Julian/Ulpian (D 6.2.9.4, different sellers): whoever possesses wins (melior est causa possidentis).

Exegesis: rei vindicatio fragments — ask

  • Who claims what from whom, and on which basis? Is the plaintiff a quirite owner (otherwise publiciana)? Is the defendant a possessor (or a fictitious possessor)?
  • Which exceptiones and replicationes?
  • Restitution: fruits, liability for destruction, expenses and retention by the bona fide possessor.

Servitudes, usufruct, emphyteusis

book pp. 178–188
limited rights in another's thing
SERVITUDES — the owner must tolerate or refrain; never act: servitus in faciendo consistere nequit

Praedial (landed) servitudes

A right of whoever owns the dominant land (praedium dominans) over the servient land (praedium serviens). It goes with the land when it is sold. Modern term: easement.

Rural
  • passage: iter (walking) · actus (driving cattle) · via (vehicles)
  • water: aquae ductus, aquae haustus
  • pasture: ius pascendi
Urban
  • ius altius non tollendi, ius luminis
  • support: ius tigni immittendi, ius oneris ferendi (the one exception: duty to keep in repair)
  • drainage: stillicidium, flumen, cloaca

Personal servitudes

ususfructus (Paulus D 7.1.1): ius alienis rebus utendi fruendi salva rerum substantia, the right to use another's thing and take its fruits while keeping its substance. Ends with the holder's death.

  • fruits incl. rent; the young of animals yes, partus ancillae no; a herd must be kept up with young animals
  • cautio usufructuaria: a promise to use it as a decent man would
  • others: usus (use only, no fruits), habitatio, operae servorum
  • ≠ loan or lease: those are contracts (in personam)

Hereditary rights

emphyteusis: use of land for rent, alienable and hereditary; the emphyteuta is almost an owner → interdicts and rei vindicatio utilis.

superficies: a hereditary right to a building on another's land → interdictum de superficiebus, actio de superficie.

Limits

  • vicinitas: the estates are neighbours
  • utilitas: it must benefit the land, not personal pleasure (walks, meals)
  • servitus civiliter exercenda est: exercise it considerately
  • no extension by one side (20 sheep ≠ 40)

Protection

  • vindicatio servitutis = actio confessoria: "the servitude exists"
  • owner's mirror image: actio negatoria p. 14
  • interdicts modelled on possession

End

  • nulli res sua servit: holder becomes owner → ends (confusio)
  • usucapio libertatis: not used (1 / 2 years); for buildings plus a contrary state (building higher)
  • usufruct: holder's death

Exegesis: servitudes — ask

  • Which type, and what does it allow?
  • Limits: no duty to act, utilitas, considerate use, no extension, substance kept
  • confessoria vs negatoria; end by non-use, confusio, death

"Legal servitudes" = restrictions that come straight from the law, e.g. the neighbour may collect fallen fruit every other day (interdictum de glande legenda, from the Twelve Tables).

Pledge I — pignus: what it is and how it arises

book pp. 189–205
a security right in rem

The life of a pledge

1 · Creation
  • a secured money claim (accessory: no debt, no pledge)
  • pledgor is owner or authorised (bonitarian ownership is enough)
  • conventio pignoris: no delivery needed
➜
2 · Existence
  • the thing is sold with consent → the pledge ends (waiver), unless it was reserved
  • sold without consent → the pledge survives (a movable sold knowingly = furtum)
➜
3a · Enforcement
  • default → sale, usually at auction (a tacit pactum de vendendo, after notice)
  • surplus → the pledgor; any shortfall is still owed
  • lex commissoria (creditor keeps the thing) banned in 320 AD
➜
3b · End without sale
  • debt paid (indivisa est pignoris causa: until it is paid in full)
  • waiver · confusio · destruction or specificatio
  • usucapio does not end it

Three meanings of pignus

  1. the pledged thing itself
  2. the right in rem (arises through conventio pignoris)
  3. the real contract of pledge (arises on delivery)

Types

  • origin: by contract, by court order, by law
  • possessory pledge: extra security and visible to all; the pledgee is a detentor with interdicts
  • non-possessory pledge (hypothec): the pledgor keeps using the thing to earn the money to repay

Other securities

  • fideiussio: a personal guarantor, an obligation and not a right in rem
  • fiducia cum creditore: ownership transferred as security, returned on payment

Remedies

actionwho → for what
actio pigneraticia in rem
(vindicatio pignoris, Serviana, hypothecaria)		pledgee → the thing, from any possessor, the pledgor included. A third party who does not restore pays full value; the surplus goes to the pledgor.
exceptio pigneraticiapossessing pledgee against the pledgor's rei vindicatio
actio pigneraticia directapledgor → return of the thing / the surplus (hyperocha)
actio pigneraticia contrariapledgee → damages and expenses (a sick cow infects his calf)

Pledging someone else's thing (res aliena pignori data)

No valid pledge. If the pledgor later becomes owner, the pledge becomes valid with retroactive effect (convalescence) → actio pigneraticia in rem utilis.

The owner becomes the pledgor's heir: Paulus D 13.7.41 no (the owner never wanted the burden) vs Modestinus D 20.1.22 yes (protect the creditor; the heir also inherits the debt).
Paulus D 20.1.29.2 — the burnt house: a pledged house burns down; L. Titius buys the land not knowing of the pledge and builds. The pledge survives in the land and the new house (superficies solo cedit; a sale without consent is no waiver). The creditors bring the vindicatio pignoris; Titius has the exceptio doli and keeps the house until he is paid the increase in value. (model exegesis in the textbook, ch. XV)

Blanket liens

A lien over all present and future property; an aggregate such as a shop's stock can be pledged as one thing. Household goods, clothing and close personal slaves are excluded. Scaevola D 20.1.34: the goods in the shop at the time of death are covered; goods sold before are free.

Liens without an explicit agreement

wherewhat is pledgedhow enforced
residential leasethe tenant's invecta illata (movables brought in to stay); secures rent and damageslandlord may seize them himself; tenant: interdictum de migrando
agricultural leasethe fruitsvindicatio pignoris; the lessee's own movables only if expressly pledged → interdictum Salvianum
statutory liensloan for repairing a building; the treasury; the ward against the guardian; the wife for her dowryoften blanket liens

A tacit lien (pignus tacitum) is ius dispositivum, not ius cogens: the parties may exclude it.

Rank in court — the pleas

  • Senior pledgee in possession vs junior's vindicatio pignoris → exceptio rei sibi ante pigneratae ("pledged to me earlier")
  • Same rank → melior est causa possidentis → exceptio rei sibi quoque pigneratae
  • Junior possessor pleads quoque → senior replies with the replicatio rei sibi ante pigneratae
Africanus D 20.4.9 pr — the bath: A leases a bath from 1 July and pledges the slave Eros to the lessor. Before 1 July he pledges Eros to lender C. → the lessor ranks first: the date of the contract counts, not the start of the lease. A pledge for a conditional claim also ranks from its creation.

Priority — the waterfall (Example 38)

Nike pledges her silver plate (worth 1,000) first to Europa (owed 500), then to Pluto (owed 400). The higher rank is paid in full before the next gets anything; what is left (superfluum) goes back to Nike.

The most senior creditor decides when to sell. A sale ends all pledges; lower ranks claim the surplus.

Ius offerendi ac succedendi

A junior creditor may pay off the senior and take his rank: Pluto pays Europa 500 → he now ranks first for 500 + his own 400.

Exceptions to priority

  • privileged statutory liens: ward, wife (dowry), treasury
  • a loan that preserved or improved the thing ranks first, because it raised the value (Ulpian D 20.4.5; Ex 39)
  • refinancing: the new creditor takes over the old rank; juniors are no worse off

Exegesis: pledge fragments — ask

  • Created? Secured claim, pledgor's ownership or authority, conventio and its scope
  • Still there? Sale with or without consent, destruction, specificatio, convalescence
  • Who enforces, and against whom? vindicatio pignoris, exceptio doli, rank, ius offerendi

The one-sentence story

You possess by animus + corpus; with a iusta causa from the owner you become owner by traditio; from a non-owner you usucapt in 1 or 2 years, protected meanwhile by the publiciana; once owner you use the rei vindicatio, and you can burden the thing with servitudes and pledges.

The three classic tensions

  • owner vs bona fide acquirer: lex Atinia, publiciana, exceptio iusti dominii
  • material vs labour: accessio, specificatio, building
  • creditor's security vs owner's autonomy: convalescence (Paulus vs Modestinus), D 20.1.29.2
