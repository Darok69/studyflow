Introduction I — persons: status and capacity

book pp. 1–8
who can hold rights · who can change them

The law of things attributes legal objects (res) to legal subjects = anyone capable of rights and duties ("legal personality"). Today all humans are equal (§ 16 ABGB); in Rome status depended on liberty, citizenship, family position and gender.

1. Legal status — the tree

Who is a legal subject in Rome?
status libertatis
homines liberi (ingenui · liberti) vs servi. Slaves = things (sold, pledged); no rights of their own, but can act for their domini. Mostly born to slave women or prisoners of war.
status familiae
patria potestas of the pater familias over filii/filiae (also adoptees, even adults unless emancipated) and the uxor in manu. Free but own nothing: acquire for the pater. Imperial era: manus-free marriage is the norm.
status civitatis
Reserved to citizens: political rights, dominium ex iure Quiritium → Ch. VIII, mancipatio → Ch. VII. Non-Romans could get the commercium (equal rights in transactions).

Beginning of personality

At birth — born alive and of human shape. The unborn: nasciturus pro iam nato habetur, quotiens de commodis eius agitur (Paulus D 1.5.7; § 22 ABGB) → a child born after the father's death may inherit.

d. Legal entities

universitates, collegia: res publica, municipia, associations, burial societies, churches, piae causae, societates publicanorum. Own and act via representatives, independent of members.
si quid universitati debetur, singulis non debentur (Ulpian D 3.4.7.1)
other limits on capacity
minores < 25lex (P)laetoria (c. 200 BC): exploiting a minor is punishable; praetor grants exceptio legis Laetoriae or restitutio in integrum; curator minoris (consent binding only from late Diocletian)
women sui iuristutor mulieris for formal acts (mancipatio) — a mere formality by the mid-2nd c.; Gaius (Inst 1.190) calls its justification a myth
furiosino capacity to act, no criminal capacity → curator furiosi
prodigussquanders family estate → praetor's interdictio: acts alone only to his exclusive benefit

2. Personality ≠ capacity

A. Property right vs possession

Possession tells us nothing about a right: Hector (thief) and Leander acquire possession, not ownership. Everyday "possessions" = owned things — not the legal term.

B. Why possession matters

1. Dispute: the possessor is stronger — he hands over only once the claimant proves his right.
2. Basis for acquiring a real right:
  • handed over (traditio) by an authorised person on a valid transaction → ownership → Ch. VII
  • taking an ownerless thing (occupatio) → ownership → Ch. IX
  • received from an unauthorised person → ownership by qualified possession over time (usucapio) → Ch. VIII

D. Proprietary and natural possession — who holds for whom?

someone controls a thing — for himself or for another?
Proprietary possession (animus rem sibi habendi)
What the sources mean by possessio. Owner, thief, buyer. Immediate if exercised himself or through persons in his power; mediate if through a detentor.
Detention = naturalis possessio of a holder sui iuris (animus rem alteri habendi)
Tenants, leaseholders, lessees, depositaries, precarists. Sources: tenere, habere. Detentor = immediate natural possession; possessor = mediate.
Possessor's agent = holder alieni iuris (slave, child, uxor in manu)
Not a detentor: the "extended arm" of the dominus → the master has immediate possession.
Ex. 4 Rusticus → Lea: he leases his field to Lea. Rusticus: animus + corpus through Lea = mediate proprietary possessor; Lea = detentor.
Ex. 5 slave Achilles takes Rusticus' sheep to market → Achilles is possessor's agent; Rusticus = immediate proprietary possessor.
Ex. 1 Clara → Midas: Midas = immediate natural possession (holds for Clara); Clara keeps her animus = mediate proprietary possessor.
Ex. 1 Hector's theft ends both Clara's possessio and Midas' detention; Hector (control + intent to enrich himself) = possessor.

Warning: naturalis possessio is not proprietary possession despite the word possessio — it is holding for another (by a free person or a slave).

Possession II — interdicts and civil possession

book pp. 16–20
two qualifications that strengthen the possessor

1. Iusta possessio (= possessio ad interdicta)

  • Interdicts = decrees of the praetor in individual cases: prevent dispossession and stop the escalation of conflict.
  • Requirement: possession nec vi nec clam nec precario — not by force, not secretly, not by revocable grant.
  • precarium = gratuitous grant of use, revocable at will; a factual relation (unlike commodatum, which gives actiones in personam).
  • The clause denying protection = exceptio vitiosae possessionis.
  • Relational: judged only towards the opponent (alter ab altero), never objectively → only a temporary measure; the right needs an actio in rem → Ch. X
prohibitoryrestitutory
vim fieri veto — "I forbid the use of force"restituas — "you shall restitute"
both blocked by the exceptio vitiosae possessionis: no one can demand back what he himself got vi, clam, precario from the person now holding it

  • Civil possession: which iusta causa? From an authorised person (→ ownership) or not (→ usucapio, unless res furtiva)?
  • Furtum: movable, external act, dolus? Which actions, which can be cumulated, who has standing?

A. Acquisition is judged more strictly than retention

acquiring may change established conditions of ownership
➜
to prevent conflict the rules must be clear, predictable, evident to all
➜
high standard for animus and corpus; once held, possession needs no continuous proof (even solo animo) → Ch. IV

Corpus is never purely symbolic: no traditio per cartam (documents), no symbols, no traditio ficta. Even the so-called solo animo modes (brevi manu, constitutum) need corpus.

C. Original vs derivative

originalderivative
by one's own act, without the previous possessorwith the previous possessor's help: he transfers control
occupatio of a res nullius (possession + ownership); theft (possession only)traditio: transferor delivers, transferee receives

→ derivative needs less corpus (factor 1 below).

B. Capacity — who can form a relevant animus?

Only persons with sufficient understanding. pupilli and furiosi are largely excluded. A problem only for persons sui iuris — those in power cannot acquire for themselves at all.

Case 22 · Paulus (Ofilius, Nerva) D 41.2.1.3 capacity
Q: who can acquire possession by himself? Held: not furiosi, and pupilli not without the tutor — no relevant intent. Ofilius and Nerva the Younger: a pupillus can without auctoritas — possession is a matter of fact. Paulus follows this if the child has intellectus.
⇒ maturity, not age alone. Same line: Marcianus D 41.1.11 (Case 42). Persons in power also need intellectus possidendi to acquire for the dominus.

D. Animus: contract ≠ conveyance

animus possidendi may be said (buyer: "I take possession now") or inferred from conduct (hunter, thief: goal-oriented acts).

Contract (e.g. emptio venditio)
consensus on thing + price → claims to dare, facere, praestare. Buyer gets neither possession nor ownership.
➜
Conveyance (usually traditio)
alters a right's scope or attribution; parties agree that delivery transfers possession = transferee's animus possidendi.
Ex. 10: Leo buys a cart from Melitta on 1 March (contract); delivery on 15 March (transfer of possession). Intent to buy ≠ intent to possess — the buyer may want delivery later, e.g. for storekeeping.

F. How much corpus? — four factors

Always real factual control; the degree depends on:

1 · mode

derivative < original

the successor steps into a position already established and accepted; an original acquirer must create it anew
2 · type of thing

land < movables

land cannot disappear; movables may vanish from view. Immovable = land + things firmly connected (solidly founded buildings, rooted plants). Heavy, bulky things are still movable. No theft of land.
3 · appearance

small and handy = easy

jewellery can be pocketed; a large stock of wares in an inaccessible place makes corpus much harder
4 · social environment

risk from third parties

high risk → a manifest act (grasping it); orderly, low-conflict setting → a realistic chance of taking control may suffice. ("Third party" = anyone who disturbs the position, even if really the second person.)

These four factors are the key to every case on the next two pages — name them in the exegesis.

E. Typical cases on animus — the sources rarely discuss it

Case 11 · Ulpian (Trebatius, Labeo) D 18.6.1.2 ius controversum
Facts: a buyer of wine seals a particular barrel, evidently in the seller's cellar.
Held: Trebatius (late Republic): possession acquired — sealing shows animus possidendi. Labeo (early classical) and Ulpian (late classical): no possession — sealing only prevents a later substitution, it does not show intent to possess now.
Corpus: no problem — the buyer is close and even touches the barrel.
Book's explanation: not deliberate casuistry — trade usage may have changed (in Trebatius' time sealing = taking possession; later = selecting goods). Labeo may also have wanted ownership not to pass yet (transfer of possession + causa + authorised seller = ownership).
Case 7 · Paulus (Alfenus) D 18.6.15(14).1
Facts: buyer marks wooden planks in the seller's warehouse (larger store).
Held: possession acquired — Alfenus (Republic) and Paulus (late classical) agree; apparently universally accepted.
Why: marking = clear expression of animus possidendi; probably a lasting custom among wood traders.

Ius controversum: opposing opinions are equally part of Roman law. A true controversy exists only for opposing views on the same case or type — not where the facts differ, nor where a later jurist criticises an outdated view.

G. Cases on corpus — land and delivery

Case 1 · Paulus D 41.2.3.1
Facts: someone wants to acquire a piece of land.
Held: he need not walk its whole perimeter — setting foot on it with intent suffices.
⇒ land is easy to control (factor 2).
Case 2 · Celsus D 41.2.18.2, 2nd sentence — the tower
Facts: the seller, intending to transfer possession, shows the buyer the land from the buyer's own tower on a neighbouring estate the buyer already possesses.
Held: buyer acquires possession.
Why: a borderline case (normally you set foot on it); decisive: from his own property the buyer extends his sphere of influence onto the neighbouring land.
Case 2 · Celsus D 41.2.18.2, 1st sentence — goods left in the house
Facts: A bought goods; A and B agree that B will place them in A's house. B leaves them there; nobody from A's household is present.
Held: A has corpus upon delivery; animus inferred from his arrangement.
Note: not oculis et affectu — A wanted possession only on arrival, and the deal was not made in the goods' presence.
Case 3 · Iavolenus D 46.3.79
Facts: C owes D money / goods. On D's order, C lays them down before D's eyes.
Held: D acquires possession the moment C follows the order — although D has not touched them.
⇒ corpus = the possibility of physical control: quodammodo manu longa tradita ("by a long hand").
Longa manu traditio is a label for all transfers without a physical act of taking — not a clearly defined legal concept, because the cases differ.

Case 4 · Paulus D 41.2.1.21 — four cases of acquisition oculis et affectu (not corpore et tactu)

Rule: if buyer and seller in the presence of the goods agree on the immediate transfer, the buyer acquires possession without further action — the thing has entered his field of vision and sphere of control.

① to the steward
In front of the goods they agree that the seller will deliver to the buyer's procurator. → possession now; consistently, the steward fetches later and need not even be present.
② coins to a third party
Debtor and creditor agree, with the coins present, that the debtor will give them to a third party. → creditor possesses, debtor is freed; the debtor then holds the money in the creditor's name.
③ heavy object
In front of an extraordinarily heavy object, buyer and seller agree on transfer → possession without moving it.
④ cellar keys
Seller hands the wine buyer the keys of the cellar → fits oculis et affectu only if the keys are handed over at the cellar.

Keys to a warehouse

Case 5 · Papinian D 18.1.74
Held: the buyer of goods in a warehouse acquires possession as soon as he gets the keys, provided the handover takes place at the warehouse.
Case 6 · Gaius D 41.1.9.6 two readings
Facts: possession (and ownership) of warehouse goods by delivery of the keys; the text does not say where.
Reading A: handover near the goods is presupposed → Gaius = Papinian (Case 5).
Reading B: the keys themselves give enough corpus — even elsewhere, if the warehouse is securely locked and the keys are the only access (short of brute force).

Wild animals — original acquisition, strict corpus

Case 8 · Gaius (Trebatius) D 41.1.5.1 controversy
Facts: a hunter has wounded a wild animal so badly that it can be captured. When does he become possessor (and owner)?
Trebatius: at once — as long as he pursues it.
Gaius: only when he actually captures it, quia multa accidere possunt, ut eam non capiamus.
Case 9 · Proculus D 41.1.55
Facts: a wild boar is caught in a hunter's trap; a passer-by sets it free.
Held: no exact criterion — possession when in meam potestatem pervenit. Two aspects: (1) does the trap give sustained control? If the boar can free itself after a long struggle → no corpus (factor 3). (2) third-party risk: higher on public ground or on another's land against his will; lower with the owner's consent or on one's own land (factor 4).
Why strict here? Both are original acquisitions (factor 1): a new position must be created against the world → real, sustained control → Ch. IX occupatio
Contrast the delivery cases 2–6 (derivative, traditio): the previous possessor co-operates → presence + agreement or a realistic chance of control is enough.

The two tradition surrogates — mirror images

3. Nemo sibi ipse causam possessionis mutare potest

Nobody can change the title of his possession on his own. The test for civil possession → Ch. II

  • Who declares himself possessor on his own lacks an iusta causa possessionis → no civil possession, no usucapio → Ch. VIII
  • Hits the thief and the detentor who decides to hold for himself.
  • Exceptions: occupatio of ownerless things; usucapio pro herede (before the heir enters, anyone may take estate things — but up to classical times not a detentor who just keeps the thing).
  • Rationale: justification shows in the manifest act of traditio; without it, the claimed possession is suspect.
  • Basis of § 319 ABGB.
Do the surrogates break the rule? No — brevi manu and constitutum rest on the agreement of both parties, not on one side's decision.

Acquiring possession V — through persons in power

book pp. 45–48 · J.
Cases 21, 25 (+ 23, 24, 27, 30)

animo nostro, corpore alieno — Paulus D 41.2.3.12 (Case 24)

Sons, daughters, uxores in manu and slaves cannot hold property (Papinian D 41.2.49.1, Case 23) → they cannot possess in their own name, but they can acquire for the dominus. The dominus supplies the animus; the body is the other's.

1. The dominus' corpus = two elements

  • the potestas relation — the person in power as "extended arm";
  • the actual control the person in power exerts over the thing.
  • Through a slave: the dominus must possess the slave himself.
  • Exception — usufructuary: only detentor of the servus usufructuarius, yet acquires what the slave gets by his own work or with the usufructuary's means (Gaius Inst 2.94, Case 27).
  • Children etc. are not possessed: the basis is the patria potestas (Paulus D 41.2.1.8, Case 30).

2. The dominus' animus — three ways

  • iussum: order for one particular transaction.
  • peculium: assets the person manages independently = a branch operation; general, abstract intent for all his transactions, not only typical ones. Concretised by the holder per deal → acquisition animo servorum (Case 24).
  • ratihabitio: approval afterwards = as if a prior iussum (rati enim habitio mandato comparatur, Ulpian D 46.3.12.4).
  • Without iussum / peculium → no acquisition.

Case 36 · Ulpian D 41.1.20.2 — two buyers, one present analogy
Facts: Titius (T) and Ego (E) buy together. Only T is present at delivery; he accepts the goods also on E's behalf.
Held: T and E acquire possession simultaneously. T acts for E like a procurator and acquires E's share in E's name: Titio et quasi meo procuratori tradita.
Why: E did not make T his procurator — Ulpian reasons by analogy and cautiously (puto — "I believe").
Text criticism: the reason quia placet per liberam personam… fits neither tone nor content → probably a Justinianic addition.
Analogy = solving a problem that does not show the exact elements of any recognised case, but is accepted to resemble known cases.

4.–5. Stabilising rules — no hasty changes

(a) traditio is a legal transaction controversy
The transferee cannot acquire (e.g. mental illness).
Ulpian D 41.2.34 pr (Case 44): the transferor remains possessor despite his wish and the factual handover — he gives up possession only on condition that the other acquires it.
Celsus D 41.2.18.1 (Case 43): the transferor gives it up anyway → the thing has no possessor.
(b) imminent involuntary loss: possession is upheld as long as possible; land is kept while third-party control looks transitory, not permanent (Iavolenus D 41.2.22, Case 55). An intruder becomes possessor only if the possessor does not try, or fails, to regain control — or if he expels detentors by force.
(c) pupilli and furiosi acting alone cannot give up possession animo (prevailing view: Ulpian D 41.2.29, Case 41; Marcianus D 41.1.11, Case 42; Proculus D 41.2.27, Case 49) — their inner will is irrelevant, for their protection. Only with auctoritas tutoris / curatoris. Where retention needs corpus, its loss ends their possession like anyone's.

Possession lost ≠ ownership lost

  • Who loses possession unintentionally usually stays owner: stolen movables and land seized by force cannot be usucapted — not even by later good-faith acquirers (again possible after reversio ad dominum) → Ch. VIII
  • A finder who keeps an obviously lost thing is a fur → Ch. II; a mere possessor could become owner only by irreversible transformation (specificatio) → Ch. IX
  • Exception: a wild animal regaining its natural freedom → ownership is lost along with possession.

Retaining possession II — movables, the fugitive slave

book pp. 57–61 · case page

B. Movables — the custodia test

custodia = control such that the person can at any time lay hands on the thing or bring it into his immediate sphere. (In the law of obligations custodia means something else: liability for loss, esp. by theft.) Slaves are movables but need no custodia.

Case 46 · Paulus D 41.2.3.13 — mislaid at home
Facts: things are merely mislaid in the possessor's sphere; he cannot find them for now.
Held: custodia and possession retained.
⇒ the model of the custodia definition.
Case 45 · Pomponius D 41.2.25 pr — lost outside
Held: who has lost a thing outside his sphere and does not know where it is → possession lost.
Case 47 · Ulpian D 41.2.13 pr — Tiber stones
Facts: stones transported on the Tiber sink in a shipwreck; after a time they are recovered from the river bed.
Held: possession lost, ownership kept.
Why: the owner lost all control. The text contrasts the servus fugitivus (possession kept) → shows why slaves are special.
Case 47a · Papinian D 41.2.44 pr — forgotten buried money
Facts: before a journey A buries money for safekeeping; on return he cannot remember where.
Held: possession retained as long as nobody intervenes — even on someone else's land. animus is clearly there; only control was in question, and forgetfulness does not end it.

C. Servus fugitivus — Paulus D 41.2.1.14 (Case 31)

questionanswer
1. Who possesses the fugitive slave? Who usucapts?The master keeps possession solo animo and continues to usucapt — until a third party takes possession of the slave. Reasons: custodia does not apply to slaves (Case 46) — strict guarding would waste their labour; and a valuable slave should not escape control by his own intelligence (Case 47).
2. Things he took with him?Still the master's possessory agent → the master keeps possession of them.
3. Things he "acquires" in flight?The master acquires through him. Problem: no iussum (out of reach) and the peculium ends with flight (Paulus D 15.1.48 pr) → probably a fiction: treated as if within a valid peculium.

Why so generous? The fugitive is likely to alienate his master's goods (esp. money); in return every acquisition by legal transaction benefits the master, who may take all items found on the slave once recaptured.

Case 28 · Paulus D 41.2.1.6 — capture by a third party
Held: the master loses possession once a third party captures the slave.
Good-faith captor (thinks the slave was abandoned = derelinquished) → the slave acquires for the new master from then on.
Bad-faith captor → cannot acquire through the slave.

D. Land without helpers — solo animo

first only seasonal land — summer and winter pastures, saltus (Cases 48, 49)
early cl. Proculus: all types of land (Cases 50, 51)
limit only with a view to re-establishing corpus; probably lost if the possessor or his people fail to return as planned (Case 51)

2. The intruder rule

A occupies B's land without permission, intending to possess: animus + control — yet no possession at once
⇣ does B regain control?
yes → no change at all: B was possessor throughout
B does not try / fails → A becomes possessor; B can sue: interdicts uti possidetis, unde vi; actio publiciana, rei vindicatio → Ch. X

A change counts only when likely to be permanent (Iavolenus D 41.2.22, Case 55).

Case 52 · Ulpian (Labeo) D 41.2.6.1 — market controversy
Facts: B goes to market leaving no one to guard his estate; A occupies it.
Labeo: A possesses as soon as he enters — did not prevail (similar: Celsus D 41.2.18.4, Case 54: an armed troop possesses at once, but only the area it controls).
Ulpian (prevailing): B loses possession only if he cannot evict A on return. A temporary absence does not end corpus.
Case 53 · Pomponius D 41.2.25.2 — long absence
The possessor is away longer → he holds solo animo. The intruder acquires only if B makes no attempt to regain control, or fails.
