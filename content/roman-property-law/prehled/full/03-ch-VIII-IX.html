Ch. VIII

Usucapio — three functions, and what it cures

book pp. 99–103
A. overview · B. form defect · C.1 typology
Usucapio = acquiring ownership through time (original acquisition)

1 · Defect in form

A res mancipi acquired by means other than mancipatio / in iure cessio → acquirer is first only bonitarian owner → after the period: civil-law (quirite) owner.

2 · Legal defect relating to the transferor

Derivative acquisition fails → mere usucapient (not bonitarian owner). Prevents a permanent separation of possession and ownership: the lawful possessor who got the thing in good faith becomes owner after a time (Neratius D 41.10.5 pr, Case 87).

3 · Usucapio pro herede

(Parts of) a dormant inheritance, no sui heredes: overt act of taking possession + 1 year undisturbed possession. Root: cult of the dead, urge the heir to enter fast. SC under Hadrian lets the heir reverse it (Gai 2.57). A detentor cannot: nemo sibi ipse causam possessionis mutare potest → Ch. III

Every bonitarian owner is a usucapient — not every usucapient is a bonitarian owner.

C.1 · Why did derivative acquisition by traditio fail? — three case groups

a. Transferor lacked ownership / authority of disposal

nemo plus iuris transferre potest quam ipse habet → transferee gets possession, not ownership.

b. Transferor lacked transactional capacity

pupillus (minor), furiosus (mentally disabled), prodigus (spendthrift under a praetorian ban) acting without auctoritas of tutor/curator. They are sui iuris and own the things, but cannot validly dispose → no binding obligation → no iusta causa traditionis.

Negotium claudicans: the part that confers rights on him is valid — if a pupillus buys, the sale is a valid causa for his acquisition (Marcianus D 41.1.11, Case 42) → Ch. VII

c. No valid causa although both are capable

e.g. the parties fail to reach consensus. Some jurists accept an apparent causa — a putative title — because the acquirer is in good faith. Likely meant for acquisition from unauthorised and authorised vendors.

Modern contrast → VIII.E

What Rome solves by usucapio, modern systems often solve by bona fide acquisition: from an unauthorised person you may acquire original ownership at once, with no waiting period.

Gate 3 — bona fides = an error of fact at the moment of acquisition

You are bona fide if you acquire …

  • from a capable person, not knowing he lacks ownership / authority (Modestinus D 50.16.109, Case 80);
  • from a pupillus · furiosus · prodigus, unaware of the lack of capacity (Cases 86, 82);
  • from an unauthorised person without valid title, knowing of neither defect (putative-title cases).

error of fact → can usucapt

C thinks the minor D is of age (Case 86). H thinks G owns the slave.

error of law → cannot

E thinks minors may sell without tutor (Case 86). H thinks slaves cannot be usucapted (Case 84).

How much knowledge harms?

  • Mala fides = concrete knowledge, at acquisition, of the defect preventing transfer.
  • Mere doubts (e.g. about capacity) still count as bona fides; apparently no duty to investigate. Modern law: only bona fide if the mistake is not due to negligence.
  • Needed only at acquisition: mala fides superveniens non nocet.
  • Irrelevant in form-defect cases; until Hadrian also in usucapio pro herede.

Gate 4 — possessio

  • Usucapio begins when possession is acquired — all requirements are tested at that moment.
  • Unflawed: not vi, clam, precario (iusta possessio). Paulus D 41.2.5: B sold but has not delivered; A, thinking B owner, seizes the thing without B's consent → no usucapio.
  • Uninterrupted for the whole period — but mediate possession through a detentor or agent suffices.
  • Possession lost → usucapio ends. Regained → period starts anew, and only if he is bona fide at re-acquisition.
  • Lost possession? → actio publiciana (+ possessory interdicts) → Ch. X

was there a reversio in potestatem? yes → B's usucapio stands

Rule

Stolen goods cannot be usucapted until they have returned into the owner's potestas. The reversio removes the blemish of furtivity.

When? Only once the owner himself regains iusta possessio: immediate custody + he recognises it as his own (Cases 72, 73, 77, 78).

Not a reversio

  • Owner regains possession only through his steward (Neratius D 41.3.41, Case 73).
  • Stolen from a detentor and returned to him, not to the owner (Paulus D 41.3.4.6, Case 72).

Why so strict?

  • Intolerable that a theft victim loses ownership because a bona fide third party usucapts.
  • After return the owner's interest in tracing his property is fully satisfied. If the thing later — without a new theft — reaches a bona fide acquirer ex iusta causa, he can usucapt; the owner keeps his rei vindicatio until the period runs out.

Is the stolen thing usucaptable again?

⇣ no
Q2 Owner sued by rei vindicatio and received the money value instead of the thing? → cleansed (Case 79)
⇣ no

⇣ no
still res furtiva — no usucapio for anyone, good faith or not.

Lex Atinia: no effect (land cannot be stolen). Force: leges Iuliae et Plautiae, real estate included.

Modern bona fide acquisition

acquire from unauthorised vendor in good faith
➜

Even stronger protection of trust in honest dealings, at the expense of established ownership.

E.1 — the building blocks a legal system can choose

1.1

movables

mainly. Austria, land: only by trusting an erroneous land-register entry (§ 1500 ABGB) or by usucaption.

1.2

good faith

belief to acquire from an authorised person; ignorance not due to negligence ("purchaser without notice", § 326 ABGB). Lower bar: slight negligence harmless (§ 932 II BGB); trust in authority of disposal enough (§ 368 I 2 ABGB). Counterweight: registered movables excluded (Art 1156 CC it.).

1.3

possession + title

unflawed: nec vi, nec clam, nec precario (§ 345). Usually valid iusta causa (§ 316 ABGB; Art 1153 CC it.). DE + FR: no valid title needed.

1.4

consideration

A gives B a thing of C: B needs less protection than C. Required in AT (§ 367) and Anglo-American law ("bona fide purchaser for value"); not in DE, CH, FR, IT.

1.5

commercial dealings

lean forms, speed, no research into ownership chains, less eviction risk, no limbo. AT: businessman in ordinary course + public auction (§ 367 I case 1, 2). Money and bearer bonds privileged (§ 935 II BGB; cf § 371 ABGB).

1.6

entrusted goods

owner trusted someone (e.g. loan) who sells → buyer acquires at once. AT § 367 I case 3 (no business / auction needed); CH Art 933 ZGB.

1.7

stolen · lost

Rome: res inhabiles. AT: possible in cases 1–2 of § 367. DE: only auction, money, bearer bonds. CH: reclaim within 5 yrs (Art 934 I). FR: within 3 yrs; fair / market / auction / merchant → only against the price (Art 2277; cf Art 934 II ZGB).

Land register vs usucaption (AT)

  • § 4 GBG: rights in land are acquired, transferred, limited, revoked only by entry in the main register (intabulation).
  • Usucaption of land = a departure from this principle. Stolen goods are not excluded from usucaption.
  • Causal tradition: acquisition via the register also needs a valid title (§ 26 II GBG with § 431 ABGB).
  • Publicity (§§ 62 ff GBG): a wrongful entry may become incontestable if no cancellation action is filed in time (no more than 3 years).

Reading the table

  • Stolen / lost goods: excluded as in Rome under § 935 I BGB; in CH and FR the owner gets a time-limited claim instead — Art 934 ZGB (5 yrs), Art 2276 CC (3 yrs).

  • Only Austria demands consideration; AT and IT demand a title, DE and FR do not. A gift from a non-owner in AT → only usucaption (3 / 30 yrs).

Wild animals — how much control?

  • Occupatio needs open and secure possession — surely met with direct physical control (Gaius D 41.1.5.1, Cases 8, 94; Proculus D 41.1.55, Case 9) → Ch. III G
  • Animal regains natural freedom → possession and ownership lost (Gaius D 41.1.3.2; D 41.1.5 pr, Case 94).
Case 95 · Paulus D 41.2.3.14 (full)
Wild animals in an enclosure, fish in a basin → possessed and owned by the captor who keeps them.
A fenced-in forest, a fish pond → no possession of the animals living there: they are in natural liberty; given the size of the habitat the owner has no control.

Tamed animals — consuetudo revertendi

  • Owned while tame and habitually returning — the habit gives the owner control (Florentinus D 41.1.4, Case 94; Paulus D 41.2.3.15, 16, Case 95).
  • Habit lost → wild again → belongs to nobody.
  • Single bee returning to the hive, pigeon to the pigeon house: owned. A swarm following its queen out to found a colony: wild, anybody may capture it (Gaius D 41.1.5.2, Case 94).
  • Pets: the habit plays no role — a runaway pet is not ownerless.

Derelictio

  • Abandon with intent to give up possession and ownership → ownerless; anyone may take it (cf § 386 1st sentence ABGB).
  • Jettison at sea in an emergency: at first only possession is lost; ownership only when the owner considers the wares abandoned (Iavolenus D 41.1.58, Case 97).
  • In doubt: no dereliction (cf § 386 2nd sentence ABGB).
  • Regardless of intent: ownership lost if the thing is so far from human reach that this equals destruction (Proculus D 41.1.55, Case 9).
  • Classical law: occupying an abandoned res mancipi → bonitarian ownership → civil-law after the usucapio period → Ch. VIII B

Lost ≠ abandoned

  • Lost, not abandoned → no occupatio.
  • The finder must give public notice to escape the suspicion of furtum (Ulpianus D 47.2.43.8, Case 98).
  • Mere finding gives no usucapio: no iusta causa, mostly no bona fides.

Acquiring fruits — separatio or perceptio?

book pp. 128–131
B. acquiring fruits

What is a fruit?

Fructus = recurring goods or income from property without diminishing its substance.

  • fructus naturales: milk, wool, eggs
  • fructus civiles: rent a tenant pays for land
  • young of animals: fruit · child of a slave woman (partus ancillae): not a fruit

Before separation

No independent legal object — a dependent part of the fruit-bearing thing, sharing its legal fate. The question "whose?" only arises after separation.

Default: the owner of the fruit-bearing thing acquires on separatio.

Apple tree test

Bona fide possessor: owns the apples as they fall. Usufructuary: owns them only when he collects the fallen ones or picks one from the bough.

Pomponius / Paulus (D 41.1.48.1, Case 103): once he learns the thing is not his → no more fruits; usucapio continues (mala fides superveniens non nocet).
Julian (D 22.1.25.2, Case 102): extends the maxim to both: possession acquired in good faith → bona fide also for fruits until evicted by the owner.

Accessio — accessio cedit principali

book pp. 132–135
C.1 general introduction

Decision tree — two things of different owners are joined

Q1 Did the owners agree on ownership of the composite?
➜
yes: as agreed — either of them or jointly (communio, societas)
⇣ no
Q2 Can they be separated without considerable damage or disproportionate effort? (soldering, screws)
➜
yes: no change of ownership → actio ad exhibendum (separation) → each: rei vindicatio
⇣ no — inseparable (welding, rooted tree)
Q3 Joined to land?
➜
yes: landowner — superficies solo cedit; building = special → C.4
⇣ no — movable to movable
Q4 Which is the main thing? = the one that can be vindicated on its own; value and labour irrelevant
➜
owner of the main thing acquires — irrevocably. Exceptions: tabula picta (opinion Gaius reports); equal pieces (Case 114)
⇣ compensation for the loser
new owner possesses → loser sues: actio in factum (good faith) · condictio furtiva (larcenous joining; actio in factum alternatively)
loser possesses the composite → defends the owner's rei vindicatio with exceptio doli: keeps it until compensated

The rule

  • Main thing (res principalis) + addition firmly joined → the addition loses its own legal fate.
  • Owner of the main thing acquires as soon as the addition is inseparably joined; must recompense the former owner.
  • Good or bad faith of the joiner (thief / believes he owns both) is irrelevant for rights in rem.
  • Origin: superficies solo cedit.

1 · Inseparable?

Separation = considerable damage or disproportionate technical effort. Aim: preserve the economic value of the composite. Key: interpenetration.

ferruminatio (welding): metals interpenetrate → inseparable
adplumbatio (soldering): solder separates the pieces → separable

Tree: inseparable once it draws nutrients from the soil.

2 · Main thing? — a procedural test

  • Only what can be brought before the court on its own can be vindicated → only that can be owned.
  • Writing cannot exist without the parchment → mere addition (Paulus D 6.1.23.3, Case 112). Ink as liquid can be owned — letters on paper cannot.
  • Language: "a statue with an arm" — nobody sues for "an arm with a statue attached" (Case 113).
  • Purple dye on wool: dyed wool stays the wool-owner's, however expensive the dye.
  • Labour value does not decide; a joiner who owned the addition may add his labour to his compensation.

Actio in factum?

Praetor sees a need for relief but has no standard action: formula opens with the concrete facts. Umbrella term — also for damages or business agreements → Ch. X B

Case 110 · Gaius D 41.1.9.1 (full)
A writes a story, poem or speech on B's paper / parchment — even in gold letters.
B owns it: the paper is the main thing ("paper covered with writing"). Only the materials count, not the intellectual input.

Case 111 · Gaius Inst 2.78 (full) — tabula picta
C paints on D's wooden panel. Opinion current in Gaius' time (high classical): the painter owns — artistic contribution beats the vindication test.
Panel owner possesses → exceptio doli vs painter's rei vindicatio; otherwise actio utilis (analogy) — the painter meets it with exceptio doli for the cost of the painting, so the claim is for handover.
Gaius disagrees: painting = writing.
Case 112 · Paulus D 6.1.23.3 (cited) — late classical: the uniform view based on rei vindicatio wins; writing is a mere addition.

Metal: soldered or welded?

Case 113 · Paulus D 6.1.23.5 (full)
F's metal arm on E's statue.
Soldered (adplumbatio) → no acquisition: separable; actio ad exhibendum, then each rei vindicatio.
Welded (ferruminatio) → E acquires the arm; F: actio in factum.
Arm later breaks off → still E's: F's right was lost irrevocably.
Case 114 · Pomponius (Cassius, Proculus, Pegasus) D 41.1.27.2 (full)
Two similar pieces of metal welded together — the vindication test gives no main thing.
Cassius: bigger / more valuable piece wins. Identical → co-ownership (cf mixing by consent) or owner is the one in whose name it was done (cf specificatio).
Proculus, Pegasus: each keeps his part despite the accession (cf confusio).

Exception — building a house (aedificatio)

book pp. 138–141
C.4 · separable and inseparable at once

Level 1 — house + ground

Permanent building on a foundation = one entity with the ground: superficies solo cedit. One rei vindicatio for both; none for the building alone.

Not permanent (granary, Case 106) → builder's.

Level 2 — materials inside

Severable accession of building materials, but: no actio ad exhibendum to separate them. Ownership of others' materials is not lost — it sleeps: dominium dormiens (Gaius D 41.1.7.10, Case 108).

Level 3 — demolition

House collapses / is torn down → ownership in the materials revives and can be asserted in court (Iulianus D 6.1.59, Case 109).

Case 107 · Celsus D 6.1.38 (full)
F bought land in good faith from an unauthorised vendor, sowed and built on it. Owner E brings the rei vindicatio. What should the judge decide?

Celsus' solution

1 · rule

E must recompense F's expenditure — but only up to the increase in value if that is lower.

⇣ E is a poor man
2 · ius tollendi

F may demolish and take his materials. E can stop this by paying what F would gain by removal.

⇣ limit
no chicanery

No scraping off plaster or murals just to destroy: neque malitiis indulgendum est — malice need not be tolerated.

⇣ E wants to sell soon but refuses to pay
3 · F keeps the land

and pays E its estimated value minus what E would have had to pay F for the building.

Ch. IX

Mixing — confusio (liquids), commixtio (solids)

book pp. 141–143
D. blending and mixing · money
liquids or solids of different owners blended / mixed

Without consent

No effect on ownership: each claims his share at once by vindicatio pro parte — quantity or value of his contribution (Pomponius in Ulpianus D 6.1.3.2, Case 116; D 6.1.5.1, Case 118). No actio ad exhibendum, no compensation remedies needed.

With mutual consent

Mixture is jointly owned (communio) → actio communi dividundo: accounts, division, allocation; then rei vindicatio for the allotted share (Gaius D 41.1.7.8, Case 117; Pomponius in Ulpianus D 6.1.5 pr, Case 115).

Money — the exception

A mixes B's coins with his own indistinguishably → A sole owner of all, original acquisition — even a thief (Iavolenus (Cassius) D 46.3.78, Case 119).

How big is each share?

2 cartloads of wheat, similar quality → share = quantity contributed
gold + copper alloy → share = value: gold-owner gets more alloy than the gold he put in; copper-owner correspondingly less

A practical fiction

Ownership normally needs the object's identity. A's share after a vindicatio pro parte contains his own grains and B's — the law disregards this for practicality.

Why the money exception?

  • Businessmen need flexible cash management, free of third-party ownership claims.
  • Specific masses of coins are hard to trace when used as currency.

Octavenus in Paulus D 6.1.6

B might still bring a vindicatio pro parte against A while all coins are still with A. It fails as soon as some of B's coins have left A's possession.

B's remedies

A acted …B's claim
in good faithunjust enrichment, e.g. condictio indebiti (paid A in the belief he owed it)
as a thiefcondictio furtiva

For ownership itself good / bad faith is irrelevant.

Specificatio — who owns the new thing?

book pp. 143–151
E. transformation · exercises

The school dispute — material or form?

Sabinians

identity = material, which survives (materia manens) → owner of the material. sine materia nulla species effici possit (Sabinus, Cassius)

Proculians

identity = form: old thing and its ownership perish; new res nullius, taken by the maker as occupatio. quod factum est, antea nullius fuerat (Nerva, Proculus)

⇣
media sententia (Gaius D 41.1.7.7, Case 120) — later prevailing

reversible → Sabinian: material owner · irreversible → Proculian: the maker (or the one in whose name it was made, alieno nomine) acquires original ownership; earlier rights are lost.

Reversibility is abstract

Enough that the product could theoretically return to its former state; whether it actually does is irrelevant. Roman example: metal.

Decision tree

Q1 Done with the owners' consent? e.g. locatio conductio operis → stays with whoever supplied the material and ordered the work
⇣ no
Q2 A thing of a different nature — looks different, has another name? no → mere processing (cleaning wool, threshing): no change
⇣ yes
Q3 Reversible? yes → material owner; maker claims his labour's added value (actio in factum / exceptio doli)
⇣ no
maker owns. Loser: actio in factum (maker in good faith) · condictio furtiva (bad faith) · possesses the product → exceptio doli until paid the material's value

What is a specificatio?

Creative effort turns another's object into a thing of a different nature — it looks different, usually has another name.

clothes from Titus' wool = transformation
cleaning it = processing: no effect
dyeing it with own purple = accessio
