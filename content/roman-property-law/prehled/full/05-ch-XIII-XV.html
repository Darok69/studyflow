Solving a case — the method step by step

book pp. 221–225
structure · formulation · Example 40 (Agatha)

Two exam formats — and why cases

  • Case → find the solution yourself (Ch. XIV) · exegesis → explain a given decision (Ch. XV)
  • aim: recognise and analyse the problems, think in alternatives, subsume facts under a rule
  • practice texts: Hausmaninger/Gamauf, Casebook on Roman Property Law (2012)

Typical property questions

  • acquisition / loss of possession or ownership — also over a chain of events
  • owner vs possessor / holder of a limited right in rem · does a pledge exist?
  • "What is the legal situation?" → positions of all parties in all things

Three tips from the book's footnotes

  • Start with possession: it is a requisite of traditio and usucapio.
  • Assume things are non-furtive unless the facts clearly say otherwise.
  • If acquisition fails on one requirement: comment briefly on all, then argue the failure.

Anatomy of a case solution — six steps (XIII.C)

1Facts
the relevant events as they happened or are supposed to have happened
➜
2Legal question
determines all further steps — answer only what is asked
➜
3Legal rule
find the rule that answers it, with its elements (requirements)
➜
4Rule applied
formulate the elements with reference to the concrete case
➜
5Subsumption = the core
is each requirement met? Short on the obvious, long on the problematic
➜
6Result
consequences only if all requirements are met; answer the question

The four rule-schemas the book spells out

rulerequirements (all must be met)see
possessioncorpus + animus possidendi (animo et corpore)→ Ch. III
traditio (derivative)alienor's power of disposition (ownership / authorisation) + traditio + iusta causa→ Ch. VII

Formulating it (XIII.D)

  • write each step so examiners can follow your reasoning; a bare result could be a lucky guess
  • full sentences, not key words; clear, simple sentence structure
  • concise but well-reasoned: do not just claim a requirement is met, argue it
  • quote the facts as proof: arg (argumento) "…"
  • learn the general structure, not model results by heart

Model solution: Example 40 — Agatha finds Bellona's gold coin

Facts: Agatha sees on the Forum a gold coin Bellona lost half an hour ago; "Today's my lucky day," she thinks, and pockets it. Q: Does Agatha acquire possession? (not ownership — the question limits the scope)
1 Rule
corpus (factual control) + animus (will to possess in one's own name)
➜
2 Applied
Agatha needs control over the coin + intent to possess it for herself
➜
3 Subsume
corpus: pockets it = immediate physical control ✓
animus: arg "Today's my lucky day!" → she wants to keep it ✓
➜
4 Result
she acquires possession: immediate proprietary possessor

Pocketing alone could also fit a detentor; the "lucky" remark tips it to animus rem sibi habendi (everyday experience).

Why the reasons count

  • Only reasoned decisions are rationally comprehensible (cf appeal review, book fn 1). Legal studies teach the method, not memorised results.

Exercise 1 (acquiring possession) — Helen's horse → Ch. II–IV

Facts: Helen lends her horse to Drusus for a day; he stables it; Lunius steals it at night; next day Lunius sells it to Klio; a week later Klio's slave fetches it from Lunius on his mistress' command. Q: Who has possession at the various stages? issues to check

Book's guide: name the elements of possession → identify the stages → go through them in chronological order.

① Before the loanHelen = possessor (corpus + animus)
② Loan to DrususHelen keeps possession, now mediate; Drusus = detentor (naturalis possessio, holds for Helen)
③ Theft by Luniusfurtum: Lunius acquires possession by his own act (original); Helen and Drusus lose theirs
④ Sale to Kliono change: a contract is not a conveyance; Lunius still possesses
⑤ Slave fetches itKlio acquires (derivatively from Lunius) animo nostro, corpore alieno: corpus via her slave, animus via her command (iussum)

Beyond the question (do not write it unless asked): Klio does not become owner (Lunius had nothing to transfer, nemo plus iuris) and cannot usucapt a res furtiva (lex Atinia) → Ch. VIII

Exercise 3 (rights of pledge) — Niobe's silver plate → Ch. XII

Facts: 1 Jan: Niobe borrows 100 from Thoas and gives a non-possessory pledge in her silver plate. 1 May: borrows 200 from Crassus and hands the plate to him as a pledge. She falls behind with her instalments; Thoas wants to enforce. Q: What can Thoas do? issues to check
Who → what → from whom
Thoas wants the plate from Crassus, who possesses it
➜
Action
actio pigneraticia in rem = vindicatio pignoris, against any possessor once the debt is due and unpaid
➜
Requirements
✓ claim 100 · ✓ Niobe owner · ✓ conventio pignoris 1 Jan (no delivery needed) → rank 1. Crassus' pledge (1 May) is valid too → rank 2
➜
Crassus' defence
exceptio rei sibi quoque pigneratae ("pledged to me too")
➜
Thoas' counter-plea
replicatio rei sibi ante pigneratae ("pledged to me earlier") — prior tempore, potior iure
➜
Result
Thoas prevails, gets possession (now also the real contract of pledge) and may sell
Crassus can stop the sale: ius offerendi ac succedendi — pay off what Niobe still owes Thoas and step into rank 1; then he decides when to sell.
Enforcement: notice to the pledger, then sale, usually at public auction (tacit pactum de vendendo); the sale ends all pledges in the plate.
Proceeds: Thoas first, in full → then Crassus (claims his share by actio utilis) → superfluum to Niobe (actio pigneraticia in personam directa). A shortfall stays a personal claim.

Exercise 2 — Turia's grain

book pp. 227–232 · traditio, usucaption, transformation
guiding questions only — path built from Ch. III, VII–IX
Facts: Dionysos' slave Lukas has a peculium. 1 March: he buys a ton of grain from Verres, who wrongly thinks it is his; it belongs to Turia. Same day Verres hands Lukas the keys of the locked granary in Ostia. 1 April: Lukas takes the grain to his master's mill, where it is ground. Two weeks later Turia demands her grain back from Lukas. Q: What is the legal situation (property law) concerning the grain? issues to check

"Legal situation" means…

possession, ownership (and limited rights in rem) of everyone involved, stage by stage. Start with possession (book fn 3).

Who wants what?
Turia wants her grain (now flour) back
➜
From whom?
she asks Lukas — but a slave holds nothing for himself; the possessor is Dionysos
➜
Action
rei vindicatio against the possessor → Ch. X
➜
Decisive question
is Turia still owner? Test every mode by which Dionysos could have acquired: derivative → usucapio → specificatio

Step 1 · Possession → Ch. III

  • Verres possesses at first: control + intent (he believes it is his).
  • Lukas, a slave, cannot possess for himself, but acquires for Dionysos: the peculium = general animus, concretised by the slave (animo servorum); Dionysos need not know.
  • When? Keys (Gaius D 41.1.9.6): handed over away from the granary. If the granary is securely locked and the keys are the only access, they give corpus on 1 March; otherwise at the latest with the transport on 1 April.
  • Not a "symbolic" traditio: corpus is never purely symbolic.

Step 2 · Derivative? → Ch. VII

  • The parties intended a conveyance → test traditio first.
  • traditio ✓ (keys / transport) · iusta causa ✓ sale (slaves contract within the peculium)
  • power of disposition ✗: Verres is neither owner nor authorised
  • nemo plus iuris transferre potest quam ipse habet → no ownership for Dionysos; Turia stays owner (so far)

Step 3 · Usucapio? → Ch. VIII

  • res habilis ✓ Verres acts in good faith → no furtum
  • possessio ✓ Dionysos (through Lukas)
  • bona fides ? issue: whose good faith counts? Lukas does not know Verres is not owner; Dionysos need not know of the purchase
  • causa ✓ sale within the peculium
  • tempus ✗ movables 1 year; only ≈6 weeks have passed
  • → fails on time alone (still mention all five)

Step 4 · Specificatio? → Ch. IX

  • Threshing only "brings to light" the grain (Gaius). Milling changes the form of each grain → a new thing, flour.
  • Irreversible: flour cannot become grain again.
  • Done in Dionysos' mill = in his name (alieno nomine).
  • No consent of Turia → the school rules apply (table below).
viewwho owns the flour?Turia's remedy
Sabiniansmaterial stays (materia manens; sine materia nulla species effici possit) → Turiarei vindicatio against Dionysos as possessor; Dionysos may claim for his labour (exceptio doli)
Proculiansnew form = new thing, ownerless, taken by the maker (quod factum est, antea nullius fuerat) → Dionysosno vindication; compensation: actio in factum (good faith), condictio furtiva only for bad faith
media sententiareversible → Sabinian; irreversible → Proculian → flour = Dionysos' (original acquisition)actio in factum against Dionysos (he acted in good faith)

Additional question (book): how does the new product affect usucaption? Issue to check: once Dionysos owns the flour by specificatio, usucaption of the grain is pointless (the grain no longer exists as such). On the Sabinian view the material survives, but the year has not run anyway.

1Felix's cow — pledge, heir, sausages → Ch. XII → Ch. IX

Facts: Felix pledges and hands his cow to Gaia (debt 1,000). At default Gaia defers for a year and returns the cow for Felix to use. Felix dies; his unsuspecting heir Hermes sells and delivers it to bona fide Japyx, who slaughters it and makes sausages.

Q: (a) when and how does the pledge arise? (b) effect of returning the cow? (c) of the inheritance? (d) of slaughter and processing? issues to check

  • (a) at the conventio pignoris: claim 1,000 + Felix owner + agreement. Delivery is not needed for the right in rem; it creates the real contract (possessory pledge).
  • (b) the right does not depend on possession → continues as a non-possessory pledge. Waiver is assumed only on consent to alienation, not to use.
  • (c) Hermes takes over debt and encumbered ownership. He sells without consent but in good faith → no furtum → Japyx presumably becomes owner, burdened with the pledge (cow = res mancipi: bonitarian).
  • (d) sausages = a new thing; the cow ceases to exist → pledge ends (cf Cassius D 13.7.18.3: ship from pledged wood). Gaia keeps only the personal claim against the heir.

2Diana's ship and cart → Ch. X

Facts: Melitta, unauthorised, sells Diana's ship (10,000; earns 70/month) and cart (5,000; 50/month) to Leo. Diana's rei vindicatio: LC 1 May. Leo uses the ship until lightning burns it on 1 July; leaves the cart idle and saws it into firewood (600) on 1 Aug. Condemnation 1 Sept.

Q: Diana's claims against Leo (a) for the ship, (b) for the cart. issues to check

  • Diana still owner (nemo plus iuris); Leo possessor. Scope = restituere: as if returned at LC.
  • (a) fruits 1 May–1 July: 2 × 70 = 140. Loss by casus: Proculians (wording neque ea res restituetur) → value 10,000; Sabinians / Paulus D 6.1.16.1 → only dolus/culpa; using a ship is not negligent → no value.
  • (b) fruits also those he could have taken (fructus percipiendi): 1 May–1 Sept = 4 × 50 = 200. Destroyed with dolus → liable on every view (pro possessione dolus est) → quanti ea res erit ≈ 5,000; if he refuses maliciously, value set by Diana's oath (iusiurandum in litem).
  • Firewood: if sawing counts as irreversible specificatio, it is Leo's. Fruits before LC: kept if Leo was bona fide.

3Cato's bed and silver table → Ch. III → Ch. VIII

Facts: Cato inherits a manor from his aunt; in January he sells Nike a thuya-wood bed and a silver table. Nike has her slave Ganymed (peculium: furniture shop) collect them. In March of the next year Agamemnon wants back the table he had deposited with the aunt.

Q: Who owns (a) the bed, (b) the silver table? issues to check

  • Possession: Nike acquires through Ganymed within the peculium (animo nostro, corpore alieno; no separate iussum needed).
  • (a) bed: Cato owner as heir + traditio + sale → Nike owner (res nec mancipi → quirite).
  • (b) table: the aunt was only depositary; the heir has no more than she had → derivative fails.
  • usucapio: res habilis (Cato sells in good faith, no furtum) · sale · bona fides · possession since January · 1 year → by March next year (≈14 months) complete → Nike owner; Agamemnon's rei vindicatio fails.

4Kreon's horse and the sealed sack → Ch. XII → Ch. IX

Facts: Kreon owes Hekuba 500 and pledges his horse (agreement only). With her consent he sells it to Merops for 600; the 600 are to be kept in a closed sack pledged to her; Kreon keeps the sack.

Q: (a) creation, action? (b) effect of the sale? (c) Kreon dies; his heir Nastes, unaware, spends the coins on bills? (d) sale without consent? issues to check

  • (a) conventio pignoris (claim, ownership, agreement; no delivery) → non-possessory pledge; when due: vindicatio pignoris.
  • (b) consent to the sale without reservation = waiver → Merops takes the horse unburdened; a new pledge arises in the coins by the new agreement.
  • (c) issue: the book has no rule on pledged coins. Payees who receive coins in good faith / mix them acquire (nummos consumere) → the coins can no longer be traced or vindicated → in practice the pledge is lost; Hekuba keeps her claim of 500 against the heir.
  • (d) knowingly selling a pledged movable without consent = furtum → Merops likely acquires no ownership; the pledge survives → vindicatio pignoris against Merops; actio furti (pledgee may bring it).

5Apples to schnapps → Ch. IX → Ch. II

Facts: Merops leases Felix's orchard. Ripe apples fall to the ground; Nastes secretly gathers them with larcenous intent and distils schnapps, which he sells and delivers to unsuspecting Leo.

Q: (a) whose apples does Nastes take? (b) does Leo become owner of the schnapps? (c) what can the victim do, which actions, against whom? issues to check

  • (a) on separatio fruits go to the owner of the mother thing → Felix's. The lessee (colonus) would acquire only by perceptio (gathering).
  • (b) distilling = irreversible specificatio → Nastes acquires original ownership (Proculians / media sententia; bad faith does not matter in rem) → sells as owner → Leo owner (derivative).
  • (c) no vindication left (apples gone, schnapps Leo's). Felix vs Nastes: condictio furtiva (value) + actio furti (penalty). Merops can force Felix to act by the actio conducti (cf Example 26).

6Elektra's silk dress → Ch. VIII → Ch. IX

Facts: Dec: seamstress Juno makes a dress from Elektra's silk (cost 500). Feb: Juno sells it to Livia for 1,500 (pretends to Elektra the silk was stolen). Apr: Livia dies; May: heiress Cleo gets it, keeps it over a year. Aug next year: sells and delivers it to Melitta for 250; Oct: Melitta cuts it into strips for a rag rug.

Q: possessor and owner in (a) Jan, (b) Mar, (c) July next year (owner), (d) Oct. issues to check

  • (a) work contract (locatio conductio operis): ownership stays with Elektra, who supplied the material → owner Elektra; Juno holds for her (detentor), Elektra mediate possessor.
  • (b) selling an entrusted thing to enrich oneself = furtum (embezzlement) → res furtiva. Livia possessor; owner Elektra.
  • (c) Cleo continues as heir, but lex Atinia bars usucaption of stolen goods for every later acquirer → Elektra still owner.
  • (d) Melitta possessor; rug = irreversible specificatio → Melitta owner, even of stolen material. Elektra: actio in factum vs Melitta (good faith); condictio furtiva + actio furti vs Juno. (Book misprint: "satisfy Juno" = Elektra.)

7Ago's seeds → Ch. III → Ch. VII

Facts: Ago gives Brontes a sack of his own seeds for safekeeping. Later, in Ago's apartment, Brontes asks to use them and promises to return a sack of the same quality after the harvest; Ago agrees.

Q: Analyse possession and ownership in the course of the case. issues to check

8Borrowed horses pledged to a money-lender → Ch. X → Ch. XII

Facts: Felix lends Titius a team of horses for a few days. Titius, short of cash, borrows from the money-lender Nastes and gives him the horses as a pledge.

Q: (a) possessory position of all parties before and after the pledging? (b) Felix's action against Nastes, and Nastes' defences? issues to check

  • (a) before: Felix possessor (mediate), Titius detentor. A mere change of intent changes nothing; the external act of pledging with intent to enrich = furtum → Felix loses possession; Titius becomes (proprietary) possessor as pledger, Nastes holds natural possession, protected by interdicts.
  • (b) rei vindicatio vs Nastes: suable on both views — Pegasus (interdict-protected holder) and Ulpian (facultas restituendi, D 6.1.9).
  • Defence none: Titius was neither owner nor authorised → no valid pledge (res aliena pignori data) → no exceptio pigneraticia. Nastes' remedy: actio pigneraticia in personam contraria against Titius.

9Secundus (12) sells his gold ring → Ch. I → Ch. VIII

Facts: Twelve-year-old Secundus sells and delivers his gold ring to Piso for a fair price paid on the spot, without auctoritas tutoris. Piso takes him (he looks older) for fifteen. 14 months later the tutor demands the ring.

Q: Must Piso return it? Variant: Secundus is 15, Piso only 13 — does Piso become owner? issues to check

  • Derivative ✗: a pupillus (boy 7–14) needs his tutor to transfer ownership → no valid causa.
  • Usucapio: from a person of limited capacity, the acquirer's good faith replaces the valid causa. Piso errs about a fact (age) → bona fides (Paulus D 41.4.2.15; an error of law would bar it). Ring non-furtive; 14 months > 1 year → Piso owner; no return.
  • Variant: Secundus (15) has reached pubertas → can transfer. Piso (13) is a pupillus, but the sale gives him a valid causa for his acquisition (negotium claudicans); he can possess himself if he has intellectus (Paulus D 41.2.1.3) → owner. Issue: his coins cannot pass to Secundus without the tutor.

10A right of way used for cattle → Ch. XI

Facts: Felix grants Tiro a right of way (iter). Tiro then sells and conveys his land to Crassus, who, pointing to the right of way, drives cattle over Felix's field.

Q: (a) legal nature and criteria of a right of way? (b) what may Crassus do on Felix's land, and why? (c) how can Felix defend himself? issues to check

  • (a) rural praedial servitude: a limited right in rem of the current owner of the dominant estate over the servient estate; tied to the land, transferable only with it. Criteria in the book: vicinitas and utilitas; servitus civiliter exercenda est; servitus in faciendo consistere nequit.
  • (b) the servitude passed with the land to Crassus (cf Ex 33). iter = walking or riding only; driving cattle would be actus → the scope may not be widened unilaterally (cf Ex 34).
  • (c) actio negatoria: declaration that no right to drive cattle exists + restoration; he may also lawfully block the excess use.

11Bellona swaps Ago's horse → Ch. III → Ch. VII

Facts: Ago commissions Bellona to exchange his horse for Carus' cow and gives her the horse; she stables it. Next day Carus agrees and hands her the cow; as to the horse, they agree that Bellona rents it from Carus for four weeks.

Q: Analyse (a) possession, (b) ownership of the horse. issues to check

after handover to Bellonaafter the rent agreement possessionAgo (mediate) Bellona detentorCarus (mediate) Bellona detentor
  • (a) constitutum possessorium: Carus forms animus; Bellona, authorised by Ago, now holds for Carus → Ago loses possession.
  • (b) Bellona has authority of disposal + transfer of possession by a surrogate + causa: exchange (issue: the book lists sale, loan, gift, dowry as the main causae; exchange also aims at transfer). Horse = res mancipi → Carus bonitarian owner; Ago keeps nudum ius Quiritium until Carus' year has run.

12Euridike's statue → Ch. VIII → Ch. III

Facts: Start of the year: Victor sells Euridike's statue to bona fide Gaius; next day Gaius' slave Stilo fetches it on Gaius' order. Gaius deposits it with Tiziana. Mid-year Gaius dies; heir Maevius. A year later Maevius fetches it from Tiziana; Euridike then finds it and demands it.

Q: Does Maevius have to return the statue? issues to check

  • Derivative ✗ (Victor not owner/authorised).
  • usucapio: possession via Stilo (iussum) · mediate possession via the depositary Tiziana suffices · sale · Gaius bona fide · heir continues (successio in possessionem, own good faith not required) · start of year to 1½ years later > 1 year → Maevius owner, no return.
  • Issue — res habilis: assume non-furtive (book). If Victor knowingly sold another's statue to enrich himself (e.g. as its holder) → res furtiva → no usucapio; Euridike's rei vindicatio succeeds.

14Ramses fetches the beams early → Ch. VII → Ch. VIII

Facts: 1 June: Ramses buys ten beams from the trader Iokaste for 500; they agree to meet in her yard on 15 June to choose ten beams, which he will take. On 7 June he passes by, enters the yard, picks ten beams and takes them home.

Q: Analyse the legal situation. issues to check

  • 1 June: the sale only creates obligations (titulus without modus); ownership passes only by traditio.
  • 7 June: Iokaste hands over nothing (handover agreed for 15 June) → no traditio → Iokaste stays owner.
  • Possession: Ramses takes control with intent → possessor, but by his own act: no civil possession (nemo sibi ipse causam possessionis mutare potest); flawed (clam) towards Iokaste.
  • usucapio ✗: Paulus D 41.2.5 — a buyer who seizes sold, undelivered goods without the seller's consent cannot usucapt.
  • Iokaste: rei vindicatio (or interdictum utrubi); Ramses: only his claim from the sale. Issue: furtum? needs intent to enrich himself — doubtful, he believes himself entitled.

15Spartacus sold twice; the wrong mare → Ch. VII → Ch. X

Facts: Breeder Irene sells Brontes the stallion Spartacus and the mare Junia; delivery in two weeks. Brontes then takes Spartacus and the mare Hera, who looks like Junia. Lucius shows he had bought and paid for Spartacus months earlier, without delivery.

Q: Positions of all parties at the end as to (a) Spartacus, (b) Hera, (c) Junia. issues to check

  • (a) owner sells twice → ownership goes to whom it is first delivered → Brontes (sale + traditio). Horse = res mancipi → bonitarian owner; Irene keeps nudum ius Quiritium. Lucius: only a claim against Irene.
  • (b) Hera was never sold → no causa → Irene owner, Brontes possessor in good faith. Usucaption at most on a putative title (disputed) and only after a year → Irene: rei vindicatio.
  • (c) Junia: Irene owner and possessor; Brontes keeps his claim for delivery.

16Irene's estate — Ares moves in → Ch. IV → Ch. VIII

Facts: Daphne inherits from Felix a house and land that really belong to Leo. 1 May: sells and conveys it to Irene. July: Irene and servants leave for her summer house. Early Aug: neighbour Ares breaks in and moves in; two months later Irene and burly friends drive him out. 1 June of the year after next: Leo demands it.

Q: Legal situation on (a) 2 May, (b) 15 Aug, (c) 1 June of the year after next. issues to check

  • (a) owner Leo (nemo plus iuris); Irene possessor and usucapient: land cannot be stolen → res habilis, sale, good faith; 2 years.
  • (b) absent, Irene keeps possession solo animo. Prevailing view (Ulpian D 41.2.6.1, Pomponius D 41.2.25.2): the intruder acquires only if she does not try, or fails, to retake → Irene still possessor. Labeo: Ares at once.
  • (c) she retakes successfully → possession never lost (force against an intruder is permitted: vim vi repellere licet) → 1 May to 1 June after next > 2 years → Irene owner; Leo fails. On Labeo's view usucaption restarts in October and Leo would win.

17Leo's two marble statues → Ch. XII

Facts: For a loan of 1,000 Leo pledges Titius two statues (small 600, large 800); they stay with Leo. (a) Titius allows a sale — small for at least 700, large for at least 900; Leo sells both to Helena for 800 each and delivers. (b) Leo's slave, a mason, by mistake turns the large statue into a column (400); Leo does not pay.

Q: (a) legal situation? (b) what can Titius do? issues to check

  • (a) small: 800 ≥ 700 → condition met → waiver, Helena unburdened owner. Large: 800 < 900 → no waiver (Marcianus D 20.6.8.14: sold too cheap) → pledge survives → vindicatio pignoris against Helena. Issue: whether Helena became (burdened) owner depends on whether Leo committed furtum.
  • (b) read as a variant without the sale. The column is a new thing → pledge in the large statue ends (Cassius D 13.7.18.3), even though made by the owner's side, unless the agreement covered products.
  • indivisa est pignoris causa: the small statue secures the whole 1,000 → vindicatio pignoris vs Leo, sale; any shortfall = personal claim.

18Ruby and glass necklaces → Ch. VIII → Ch. X

Facts: Feb 130: Irene borrows Julia's ruby necklace, then buys a look-alike glass one. June 130: by mistake she returns the glass one; nobody notices. Oct 131: she gives the ruby necklace to unsuspecting Pius, who loses it. Summer 132: Hermes has it, willing to return it but not knowing to whom.

Q: Situation and actions at the end as to (a) the ruby necklace (Hermes), (b) the glass necklace (Julia). issues to check

  • (a) Julia stays owner. Irene acts by mistake → no dolus, no furtum → res habilis. Pius: gift + good faith → usucapient from Oct 131, but loses possession before a year → no ownership. Hermes does not take it for himself → no thief.
  • Julia: rei vindicatio vs Hermes (Ulpian: whoever can restore). Pius: actio publiciana — wins vs Hermes, loses vs Julia (exceptio iusti dominii).
  • (b) Irene stays owner: handing back a loan is no causa for transfer. Julia (since June 130 > 1 year) could have usucapted only on a putative title (pro suo) — disputed among jurists → otherwise Irene: rei vindicatio.

19Wheat and barley taken from the lessee → Ch. IX → Ch. XII

Facts: Ago leases two fields from Vibia, sows wheat and barley. In summer he cuts the wheat and stores it in a makeshift wooden hut he built on the field. Days later barley and wheat are gone: Marcus, Vibia's divorced husband, took the whole crop.

Q: Which claims exist as to (a) the wheat, (b) the barley? issues to check

  • While growing, the crop belonged to the landowner (satio); a lessee acquires fruits only by perceptio.
  • (a) wheat cut and stored → Ago owner → rei vindicatio or condictio furtiva vs Marcus (not both) + actio furti. Issue: the lessee's fruits are tacitly pledged to the lessor → Vibia also has a lien.
  • (b) barley separated by Marcus → Vibia's → only she sues Marcus (condictio furtiva / rei vindicatio + actio furti); Ago can force her with the actio conducti (= Example 26).
  • The hut has no foundation → movable, stays Ago's (cf Scaevola's granary, D 41.1.60).

20Carus' pear trees and tent → Ch. X → Ch. IX

Facts: Helena, unauthorised, sells and conveys Dexter's land to bona fide Carus. Before winter he plants 40 pear trees (cost 10 each); in April five have withered; in June he puts up a tent to sleep there. In summer Dexter demands the land; Carus joins issue.

Q: (a) Dexter's position vs Carus: remedies, scope? (b) Carus' position vs Dexter: remedies, scope? issues to check

  • (a) Dexter still owner: land needs 2 years, Carus has < 1 → rei vindicatio vs the possessor: land with the rooted trees (implantatio, superficies solo cedit) + fruits after LC (taken and neglected); else quanti ea res erit.
  • (b) Carus bona fide → exceptio doli = retention until expenses are paid, but only up to the increase in value (Celsus D 6.1.38): withered trees add nothing → at most 35 × 10 = 350. Fruits before LC are his (separatio).
  • Tent: not fixed to the ground → stays Carus'. His publiciana would fail vs Dexter (exceptio iusti dominii).

21Publius the spendthrift → Ch. VIII → Ch. X

Facts: Publius, put under curatorship as a prodigus, sells and conveys land to Gorgias, who learns of the incapacity two days after the conveyance. Gorgias then builds a villa (cost 100,000; land value +80,000). 13 months after the conveyance the curator learns of it and demands the land.

Q: Will the curator prevail? issues to check

  • Derivative ✗: a prodigus cannot validly dispose without his curator.
  • usucapio: good faith replaces the valid causa; Gorgias was bona fide at acquisition (mala fides superveniens non nocet) — but land needs 2 years; 13 months → not complete.
  • → curator prevails with the rei vindicatio.
  • The villa: built after he knew → no exceptio doli for expenses (Julian in Ulpian D 6.1.37), only a ius tollendi — as far as removal does not merely harm the owner (neque malitiis indulgendum est).

22Pegasus sold and leased back → Ch. III → Ch. VII

Facts: In Brontes' house Ago sells him the horse Pegasus (looked over together a week earlier on Ago's estate) for 100,000; at the same time they agree that Ago keeps the horse for another year, paying 20,000 rent.

Q: Analyse possession and ownership of the horse. issues to check

  • Possession: constitutum possessorium — Brontes' animus + Ago now holds as tenant for him. The viewing a week ago gave nothing (no agreement in the horse's presence, so no oculis et affectu).
  • Ownership: owner + surrogate traditio + sale. Horse = res mancipi → bonitarian; quirite after 1 year — mediate possession through Ago suffices.

23Seius' ship repaired by Maevia → Ch. XII

Facts: Seius promises his ship to Cato as security for a loan of 100,000 without delivering it. After storm damage Maevia repairs it, insisting on a pledge for her fee; Seius brings the ship into her dock. He cannot pay her 50,000 nor repay Cato when due.

Q: Can Cato demand the ship from Maevia and enforce? How can Maevia retain it? issues to check

  • Cato: valid non-possessory pledge (no delivery needed), earlier → rank 1 on the time rule. vindicatio pignoris vs Maevia → her exceptio rei sibi quoque pigneratae → his replicatio rei sibi ante pigneratae.
  • But: a pledge securing what maintained or restored the pledged thing ranks first (Ulpian D 20.4.5; Example 39: loan to make a ship seaworthy). Issue: Maevia secures a repair fee, not a loan — argue the same rationale (the repair raised the value). If so, Maevia keeps the ship and may sell.
  • Cato's way out: ius offerendi ac succedendi (pay Maevia, take her rank).

24Kalypso and two pledgees → Ch. X → Ch. XII

Facts: 1 Feb: Ago gives the horse Kalypso to Curio. 1 Mar: Curio pledges it to Kassandra (rent owed 10,000); 1 May to Sophia (loan 20,000). Curio pays Kassandra 8,000, Sophia nothing though due.

Q: (a) Ago dies; heir Agathon demands the horse from Curio — will he win? (b) how can Sophia get (natural) possession, and what if Kassandra then sues for it? issues to check

  • (a) gift + traditio of a res mancipi by the owner → Curio bonitarian; Ago (heir) only nudum ius Quiritium, year not run. Agathon's rei vindicatio → Curio's exceptio doli (rei venditae et traditae only after a sale) → Agathon fails.
  • Both pledges valid: bonitarian ownership suffices to pledge.
  • (b) Sophia: vindicatio pignoris vs Curio (debt due). Then Kassandra (rank 1, still owed 2,000; pledge lasts until paid in full) sues Sophia → exceptio rei sibi quoque pigneratae → replicatio rei sibi ante pigneratae → Kassandra wins. Sophia may pay her 2,000 and succeed to rank 1.

Writing an exegesis — outline and interpretation

book pp. 259–264
for written tests and exams (not a seminar paper)

What an exegesis is

  • interpretation and explanation of a text (cf "exegetics" in theology)
  • in Roman law: classical texts, mostly decisions of classical jurists from the Digest = Digest exegesis; other sources too (FIRA, Sententiae Syriacae)

Its function

  • interpret a legal text
  • explain and give reasons for a specific, pre-existing decision
  • apply and demonstrate your knowledge of Roman law

Exam frame (as the book states it)

Vienna FÜM 1: 180 min in all; Roman law part 120 min for 120 points; the exegesis usually worth 28 points. Ask what your examiners expect.

The outline — four parts (XV.C)

1Facts of the case
What happened? Who did what? Any variants? Identify the conflict the jurist resolved.
➜
2Legal question
the problem the jurist had to solve — explicit, or implicit in the decision
➜
3Decision
the jurist's answer (not yours!); report several opinions if given
➜
4Interpretation = the main part
start from the decision: explain and give reasons for it. Parts 1–3 only prepare the ground — keep them brief.
A responsum already follows facts → question → answer, so 1–3 can often simply be read off (as in D 20.1.29.2).
Fill in tacit facts from the decision: if usucaption is discussed, derivative acquisition failed (usually an unauthorised alienor); if traditio succeeded, authorisation and iusta causa were present.
Scholarly variant (fn 3): I text (edition) · II translation · III inscriptio · IV interpretation; plus author and genre, palingenetic context, textual criticism (glosses, truncations) — in the background for exams.

Functions of dogmatics (Mayer-Maly, book fn 6)

  • review decisions for consistency with accepted legal principles
  • discover overarching principles
  • locate the decision within the system of principles

Attitude

Write like a teacher or legal adviser making the decision understandable and plausible to a lay person within the Roman legal system: go into the central problems at some length, without digressing. No one-size-fits-all recipe — practise (casebooks with model exegeses; study groups).

What goes into the interpretation (XV.D) — work down the list

Institution Which legal institution? Explain the concepts and terms used in the text.
Claim Who wants to get what from whom?
Basis On what legal foundation, and when is it given? Know the elements and subsume. A claim for restitution can rest on a right in rem (ownership, pledge), a contract (sale, mandate, lease, deposit) or a delict (furtum).
Remedy Which actio / interdictum? Who can sue (claimant), who be sued (defendant)?
Defences exceptiones of the defendant? replicationes of the claimant?
Parallels Identical or related cases? Opposing views of other jurists (legal controversies)?
Dogmatics Deduce the decision from the system of rules.
Interests How far does the decision serve the interests of the parties concerned?

Model exegesis — D 20.1.29.2, the burnt pledged house

book pp. 264–269 (Case 170)
pledge → Ch. XII · building → Ch. IX
D 20.1.29.2 (Paulus libro quinto responsorum) Domus pignori data exusta est eamque aream emit Lucius Titius et exstruxit: quaesitum est de iure pignoris. Paulus respondit pignoris persecutionem perseverare et ideo ius soli superficiem secutam videri, id est cum iure pignoris: sed bona fide possessores non aliter cogendos creditoribus aedificium restituere, quam sumptus in exstructione erogatos, quatenus pretiosior res facta est, reciperent. In short: the pledged house burned; L. Titius bought the plot and built. Paulus: the pledge persists and the new building follows the ground, with the pledge; bona fide possessors must hand it over only once repaid their building costs as far as the property gained in value.
Facts (1st sentence)
S pledges his house to G; it burns down; S sells the land to Lucius Titius, who builds on it. Variants: Titius in good / bad faith.
➜
Question (2nd)
Does G still have a pledge? Can G demand the property from Titius?
➜
Decision (3rd–4th)
the pledge remains and the building follows it; a bona fide possessor need restore only against reimbursement up to the increase in value.

Reading the inscriptio

D 20.1.29.2 = Digest book 20, title 1, fragment 29, paragraph 2 · Paulus libro quinto responsorum = from book 5 of the collected legal opinions (responsa) of Paulus, a late classical jurist.

Point 7 — what Titius can retain (book's figures)

land
100k
building costs 800k
value now
600k
retain until paid
500k

Only the increase in value (600k − 100k), not the costs: quatenus pretiosior res facta est (cf Celsus D 6.1.38).

The interpretation in nine steps (book's numbering)

1Nature: arises by agreement; limited right in rem to be satisfied from the proceeds if unpaid; preferential, against everyone (actio pigneraticia in rem).
2Creation: pledger at least bonitarian owner · a claim to be secured (accessoriality) · conventio pignoris, which fixes the pledged assets.
3Interpreting the agreement: "the house" — a house is an addition to the land (superficies solo cedit), so it can be pledged only with the land → read as land + house.
4Destroyed? The pledge covers the whole plot → survives the fire; lost value does not matter. Only destruction of the entire object ends it.
5Sale without consent: consent without reservation = waiver. Here none (arg: the pledge "remains"; "bona fide possessor" = unaware buyer) → the pledge survives.
6Action: creditor's actio pigneraticia in rem = vindicatio pignoris (also Serviana, hypothecaria) against the buyer, suable as possessor.
7Defence: the bona fide builder raises the exceptio doli and retains until reimbursed up to the increase in value (figures above).
8Malae fidei possessor (knew of the pledge): presumably no reimbursement, but a ius tollendi — e contrario from Paulus and the owner-vs-possessor cases (Ulpian D 6.1.37).
9Interests: creditor's preferential satisfaction vs the builder who raised the value. Paying him leaves the creditor no worse off: without the sale the value would not have risen.

Point 5 excursus — did the buyer become owner? (not expected in an exam)

A (Schlichting; Kunkel/Mayer-Maly): pledger lacks authority of disposal → no derivative acquisition; arg: "bona fide possessors", not owners.

B (Kaser): possible, but usually fails by furtum; none for land or by mistake → buyer owner, burdened (nemo plus iuris).

Either way the creditor keeps his pledge.

Opposing view to cite — Africanus D 39.2.44.1

No right of retention: the buyer is referred to his contractual claims against the seller (the pledger) → he carries the risk of the pledger's insolvency for his expenses. Naming this ius controversum shows you can place Paulus' solution.

Book's tip: number your interpretation points and add subheadings; make a short outline first.
