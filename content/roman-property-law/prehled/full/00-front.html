Read left to right. Possession is a fact, ownership is a right. Possession leads to ownership (by traditio, occupatio or usucapio). The law then protects both, and the owner can grant limited rights to others. Pills (e.g. Ch. VIII) point to the chapter in this complete edition.

Foundations Ch. I

  • Persons: status (libertatis, civitatis, familiae), capacity (infantes, pupilli, furiosi, prodigi)
  • Things: only res corporales — quae tangi possunt
  • Rights in rem: absolute, against everyone (actiones in rem) vs rights in personam

DOMINIUM = right Ch. V

  • nihil commune habet proprietas cum possessione

Acquiring ownership Ch. VI

Derivative (from the former owner's right — nemo plus iuris):

  • mancipatio, in iure cessio (formal, abstract) Ch. VII
  • traditio (informal, causal) Ch. VII

Original (no former owner's help):

  • usucapio — cures failed transfers Ch. VIII
  • natural modes: occupatio, fruits, accessio, confusio/commixtio, specificatio Ch. IX

Who is who in the textbook examples

Clara owner · Hector thief · Leander bona fide buyer · Midas depositary · Rusticus landowner · Lea lessee. The same names come back across chapters, so link the cases in your head.

Ius controversum

Jurists often disagree on the same case (Trebatius vs Labeo, Sabinians vs Proculians, Julian vs Ulpian). In an exegesis, naming the opposing opinion and the interests behind each view earns points.

Persons in power

Slaves and children in patria potestas own nothing themselves but acquire for the dominus / pater familias, through an order (iussum) or a peculium.
