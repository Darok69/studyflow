B. The two stages of Roman civil procedure (formula procedure)

1 · IN IURE — before the praetor
  • both parties appear; plaintiff states his case, defendant contests it = enters the proceedings
  • praetor asks: is there an actio that fits the facts as stated? He does not verify them
➜
litis contestatio ("bearing witness to the dispute")
  • praetor appoints the iudex and issues the formula = programme of the trial: actio + any exceptiones
  • a hypothetical judgment: "if the claim is found true, condemn; if not, acquit"
➜
2 · APUD IUDICEM — before the iudex
  • a private citizen, not an official; finds out which party told the truth
  • bound by the formula; resolves no legal issues — like a jury member today

The praetor and his edict

  • magistrate responsible for jurisdiction; elected for one year; main task: grant actiones and exceptiones
  • edict at the start of his term: programmatic promises of protection + catalogue of all formulae
from 242 BCdisputeslaw
praetor urbanuscitizen v citizenius civile
praetor peregrinusRomans v peregrini, and between peregriniius gentium (common to all mankind)

Imperial period: justice passes to the emperor; Hadrian has a definitive edict published.

The praetor's three routes

Does an actio fit the claim?
in the edict
→ grants it; its formula fixes object and course
not in edict, but valid

Some creations later entered the edict but kept names like actio in factum / utilis / ad exemplum.

Actio attacks, exceptio defends

An exceptio is granted when the wording of the actio would let the claimant win, but considerations outside it call for a decision for the defendant.

Ex 28 (cf. Case 68, Iavolenus D 39.5.25) → Ch. VII: T should give E's thing to Titius as E's gift, but gives it in his own name. E wanted Titius to have it anyway → E's rei vindicatio is met by the exceptio doli.

Why two stages? Limits the praetor's workload in a fast-growing society, yet keeps his influence on interpreting and developing civil law.

F. Who can be sued?

ubi rem meam invenio, ibi vindico — the owner not in possession sues anybody in possession. Presupposes the thing exists: destroyed → ownership lost; also lost when someone else acquires original ownership.

Possession needed — but whose? Pegasus: interdictive possession; Ulpian: any natural possessor who can restore (facultas restituendi) Case 121

ficti possessores — pro possessione dolus est
  • fraudulently enters a defence without possessing (qui liti se obtulit) — e.g. to shield the real possessor so he can go on usucapting
  • fraudulently gives up possession after LC

A fiction: facts deliberately assumed so a rule applies although not all its elements exist. (Qui dolo desiit possidere is in the formula of the actio ad exhibendum; for the RV debated.)

Why not keep the thing safe in harbour?

A possessor need not forgo a thing's use just because it is claimed — the claim is still unproven; and since he risks paying fructus percipiendi, he is under pressure to use it, even at a risk (Paulus' ship, Case 126).

A. What a servitude is

A limited right in rem in another's property: the owner must "grant to another the exercise of a right, or abstain from exercising a right otherwise belonging to the owner" (§ 482 ABGB). It curtails the owner's right and gives the holder specific rights in the asset.

  • servitus in faciendo consistere nequit — the owner may only have to permit or refrain, never to act
  • nulli enim res sua servit (Paulus D 8.2.26) — no servitude in one's own property
  • holder acquires the burdened property → servitude expires = confusio (union of the two positions; ≠ blending of substances → Ch. IX)

rural (servitutes praediorum rusticorum) — land used for agriculture

Personal — holder = a specific individual; extinguished by his death
  • ususfructus (the widest)

  • support: ius tigni immittendi (beam into the neighbour's wall) · ius oneris ferendi (rest one's building on the lower neighbour's) — the exception: owner must keep it in repair (Case 142)
  • drainage: ius stillicidii (gutter drips) · ius fluminis (divert rainwater) · ius cloacae (sewage)

C.–H. personal servitudes to superficies

C.1 Ususfructus — the widest personal servitude

ususfructus est ius alienis rebus utendi fruendi salva rerum substantia (Paulus D 7.1.1) — the right to use another's property and take its fruits, while preserving its substance.

  • use (usus) + fruits (fructus) — only the recurrent proceeds
  • fruits include rent / lease income: fructus civiles (Case 150)
  • Ex 35: Hera may live in the house or rent it out, but not turn it into a bed and breakfast
  • how he acquires the fruits → Ch. IX
  • partus ancillae is not a fruit → the owner's; the young of animals → the usufructuary (Case 149)
  • herd = universitas rerum: keep the number up by adding young animals for those dead or slaughtered (Case 149)
  • cautio usufructuaria: formal promise at the start to use it boni viri arbitratu
  • most often created by bequest — provides for e.g. the wife without making her heir

C.2 Other personal servitudes

  • usus: use only, no fruits — but a tendency to allow fruits for personal use (Cases 153, 154)
  • habitatio: real right of habitation
  • operae servorum / animalium: labour of slaves or animals

D. Protection of the holder

  • not a proprietary possessor, but the praetor gives interdicts modelled on the possessor's (usufructuary of land: interdictum uti possidetis utile)
  • vindicatio servitutis = actio confessoria (in rem): declaratory judgment that the servitude exists + owner must create a matching state of affairs
confessoria
"it exists"
⇄
negatoria
"it does not" → Ch. X

E. End — usucapio libertatis

  • holder does not exercise the right, neither personally nor through others (non usus) → owner usucapts freedom from it
  • period: 1 year movables, 2 years land
  • buildings: plus an overtly contrary state, e.g. building higher despite altius non tollendi (Case 147)
  • ≠ usucaption of a servitude: the lex Scribonia forbids usucapting praedial servitudes
  • other ends: confusio; personal servitude: holder's death

F. "Legal servitudes"

Restrictions in favour of neighbours arising directly from statute, not from agreement — similar in content, different in form.

Windfall: every other day the owner must let his neighbour collect fruit fallen across the fence → interdictum de glande legenda (from the Twelve Tables, so truly "legal").

Modern example: necessity way to landlocked property (NotwegeG).

A. The problem — Ex 36 (Labrax and Philto)

Labrax lends 100 to Philto, a money changer, for one year, trusting his country seat and art collection. Philto does not pay; other creditors appear; debts far exceed assets; bankruptcy.

With a pledge he could seize the item by an action in rem, auction it, and in insolvency demand its separation from the bankruptcy assets — reserved for him alone.

B.1 What a pledge does

  • a right in another's property: if the debtor does not pay when due, the creditor sells it (public auction) and keeps as much of the proceeds as the debt
  • in bankruptcy the others share a quota; the pledgee separates his item and, if the proceeds suffice, is paid in full

Fiducia = a trust: towards third parties the fiduciary is owner; towards the debtor he is bound by the agreement — he can do more than he may.

C. Pignus means three things

pignus
1 · the pawn
the pledged thing itself
2 · right of pledge
in rem; arises by conventio pignoris — no delivery needed
3 · contract of pledge
real contract; arises on delivery of the thing

Do not confuse 2 and 3: a lien exists as a right in rem without any delivery; the contract only begins when the pledgee gets the thing.

right of pledge

secures the pledgee's claim against the debtor; created when pledger and pledgee agree that the claim is secured by an asset of the pledger (conventio pignoris)

contract of pledge

specifies the contractual obligations between pledger and pledgee; comes into being on delivery ("real contract" = by delivery, not by mere consensus)

Pledge II — requirements, remedies, life cycle

book pp. 189–220
D.–H. right, contract, alienation, end

D.1 Requirements of the right of pledge — all three

1

a monetary claim

to be secured; no valid claim → no pledge: the pledge is accessorial (arises and lives with the debt). Debtor and pledger may differ: the debtor is personally liable, a third-party pledger only with that asset.
2

pledger's ownership or authority

bonitarian ownership suffices; otherwise nemo plus iuris transferre potest quam ipse habet. Pledger acquires ownership later (e.g. inheritance) → convalescence (G).
3

conventio pignoris

agreement that the asset secures the claim = an act of conveyance; fixes the scope (which assets, which debts); no delivery needed. The pledger stays owner but may generally no longer alienate.

D.2 / E. Remedies

remedywho · for what
actio pigneraticia in rem = vindicatio pignoris
(also actio Serviana, quasi Serviana, hypothecaria)		pledgee, once the debt is due and unpaid (at the latest), against any possessor — the pledger included; also to regain a lost possessory pledge. Condemnation: debtor-possessor → up to the debt; any other possessor → full value, creditor keeps the debt and hands the superfluum to the pledger.
exceptio pigneraticiapossessory pledgee against the pledger's rei vindicatio

E. When does the real contract begin?

  • real contract = agreement + delivery (datio)
  • possessory pledge: at the handing over
  • lien: no contract at first; it arises when the pledgee obtains possession — handed over, or by vindicatio pignoris, interdictum Salvianum, or the landlord's right to retain the tenant's things

Why a surplus is normal

Creditors insist on security worth far more than the debt (guard against a market slump) → auction proceeds often exceed the debt → superfluum for the pledger.

Settling accounts after a sale

Under the contract of pledge: pledger → the surplus by the directa; pledgee → expenses and damages by the contraria. Several items pledged: the creditor chooses which to sell. A waiver ends only the pledge — the claim against the debtor stays.

The life of a pledge

F.1 sold with consent
  • unreserved → waiver: pledge ends, buyer gets unburdened ownership
  • right reserved → buyer owns, but burdened (nemo plus iuris)
|
F.2 sold without consent
  • movable, knowingly → pledger is a fur; buyer likely gets only possession
  • good faith, or land → buyer presumably owner, but burdened
  • either way the pledgee can claim it from any possessor
➜
F.3 enforcement
  • default → sale, usually public auction (tacit pactum de vendendo, late classical); notice — postclassical denuntiatio ×3
  • lex commissoria: fell out of use, banned 320 AD
  • sale ends all pledges; shortfall still owed; juniors: surplus by actio utilis
|
H. end before sale
  • debt ceases (paid, acquitted, set off) — indivisa est pignoris causa
  • waiver · confusio
  • destruction or specificatio
  • usucapio: seemingly not

J · Blanket liens and aggregates

A blanket lien covers all present and future property (non-possessory); an universitas rerum (e.g. a shop's stock) can be pledged as one entity.

Case 159a · Ulpianus D 20.1.6; Paulus D 20.1.7 (cited): a blanket lien excludes things not usually pledged individually — household items, personal effects, clothing, slaves with a personal function or close bond to their master.
Case 159b · Scaevola D 20.1.34.2 — borrowed coins controversy
All of C's present and future assets are pledged to D. C borrows coins from E and becomes their owner. In the lien? → one could say no: the loan's value is really the lender's (aes alienum, "another's money"). Scaevola: yes — once owned, the coins are C's property legally and economically.
Case 159b · Scaevola D 20.1.34 pr — the shop
A pledges his taberna to B; goods come in, are processed, are sold; A dies. → B's pledge covers the goods in the shop at A's death.

Fixing the date of rank

Case 177 · Africanus (Iulianus) D 20.4.9 pr — the bath
A rents B's bath from 1 July, pledging and delivering slave Eros for future rent. Before 1 July he borrows from C and pledges Eros to him. → B (lessor) ranks first: the contract creating the secured debt came first, though the lease starts later.
Case 177 · D 20.4.9.1: a pledge for a conditional claim ranks before a later one for an unconditional claim (not if the condition is potestative); condition fails → pledge ends.
Case 178 · Gaius D 20.4.11.2
P and V agree on a usufructuary lease: fruits and P's movables on the estate are pledged to V. P pledges an asset to K before bringing it onto the estate. → K first: V's pledge arose only when the thing was brought in.

T lends and takes a pledge. S lends 50 with a pledge in the hyperocha. Later T lends a further 40. Who takes the excess over T's first claim? → S.
