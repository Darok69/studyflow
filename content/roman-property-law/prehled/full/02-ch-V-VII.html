A. Definition

Ownership = the property right that gives a person — the owner — exclusive control over a thing.

  • Latin: dominium or proprietas; the owner = dominus
  • jurists also simply say suum est ("it is his") to attribute an asset to a person
"Regarded as a right, ownership is the entitlement to avail oneself of the substance of a thing and its uses at will, and to exclude all others from it."§ 354 ABGB — the book: a definition useful also for Roman law

The owner's eight powers — and their limits

book pp. 70–72
D. Powers of the owner · E. Limits (IUS COGENS)

1Use & exclude

Use the thing, prevent all others from using it — or grant others the use.

Example 12 Aulus may walk in his garden and fence it off against intruders.

2Fruits

Perennial yields from the designated use; primarily the owner's. fructus naturales (fruit, vegetables) · fructus civiles (rent from a lease).

Example 13 Lysippe owns the apples and cherries; leasing the garden → the rent = civil fruits.

3Affect the substance

Modify, process, even destroy.

Example 14 Lysippe may turn her garden into a turnip field — or let it become an untouched biotope.

4Grant limited rights

Pledge, servitude …

Example 15 Lysippe pledges the garden to her bank; or grants neighbours a right to let their sheep graze.

5Alienate

Transfer ownership (conveyance): acquirer becomes owner, vendor loses all right.

Example 16 Lysippe sells and delivers a crate of apples → after the traditio the neighbour is owner.

6Authorise disposal

A non-owner can then validly dispose of someone else's property.

Example 17 Aulus must go to war; he authorises his brother Balbus, who sells the apples for him.

7Abandon (dereliction)

He gives up possession of his own accord with the evident intention of giving up ownership → res derelicta (→ open to occupatio). → Ch. IX

8Free a slave

manumissio: the slave becomes a legal subject.

The owner asserts all these rights by actions in rem. → Ch. X

E. The frame: ownership exists only inside the legal order

legal system — ius cogens = mandatory provisions that cannot be superseded by private agreement

owner's powers 1–8
the most comprehensive right — but derived from the legal system, so never unlimited

Responsibility too: the owner must ensure his property does not cause harm to others.

Example 18 · gifts between spouses
From ancient times forbidden → Aulus cannot gratuitously convey his garden to his wife Aurelia during his lifetime.
Example 19 · building height
Aulus may build a house — but bound by building rules, e.g. Augustus' constitution: no buildings over 70 feet (≈ 21 m; Strabo 5.3.7, Suet. Aug. 89.2).

cautio damni infecti

A house is neglected so far that it threatens to tumble down onto the neighbours' buildings → the neighbours can force the owner to give security for the damage about to occur.

"Legal servitudes"

Ground owners must accept restrictions safeguarding their neighbours. Structure resembles servitudes for the neighbours — imposed by the legal system. → Ch. XI

Who are the sui heredes?

Daughters (filiae familias), sons (filii familias) and the wife in manu — also adoptive children and children of predeceased children under his patria potestas. While he lives they cannot hold property; at his death they become sui iuris.

Held: Titius does not acquire ownership; in strict law E remains owner. But if E brings the rei vindicatio, Titius defends with the exceptio doli → Titius keeps the thing.

Why — benignius est ("more generous"): E's original intention, a gift to Titius, has been carried out; no rigid mechanism should let E harass Titius by reclaiming the gift when he changes his mind.

Gate 3 — the iusta causa: Julian vs Ulpian

book pp. 86–89, 93
Case 70 · Case 71 · controversy

In both cases authority and handover are unproblematic — the only question is the causa. Julian is generous; Ulpian, writing towards the end of the classical period (roughly two generations after Julian), is strict.

Case 70 (1) · Julian D 41.1.36 — will vs stipulation
Facts: A conveys land to B. A thinks he owes it under a will; B thinks under a stipulation.
Held: B becomes owner.
Why: enough that A and B agree the land is conveyed to fulfil a legal obligation, even if they disagree on which. Unproblematic as long as at least one of the two obligations really exists.
Debate: if neither exists, some modern scholars see the joint purpose itself as a cause — the causa solvendi, valid even without a real obligation to fulfil.
Case 70 (2) · Julian D 41.1.36 — gift vs loan
Facts: C hands money to D as a gift; D takes it as a loan for consumption (mutuum).
Problem: no consensus → neither donation nor loan agreed; causa solvendi impossible (no prior obligation); donatio and mutuum only arise on delivery.
Held: D becomes owner on delivery — derivatively by the traditio, not by mixing.
Why: both transactions convey ownership without consideration in return (only difference: permanent vs temporary — repayment) → agreement on the key aspects = common denominator = valid iusta causa (analogy; Julian does not drop the causa requirement).

1. Consensus — France

Justinian: mancipatio and in iure cessio gone, civil/bonitarian distinction gone; traditio for every bodily thing:

⇣

⇣
Code civil 1804: ownership passes as soon as the owner enters into an obligation (e.g. valid sale of a specific object)
  • Art 711: acquired and transmitted by "the effect of obligations"; Art 938: accepted donation → ownership without any other conveyance; Art 1196: transfer at conclusion of the contract, may be deferred; risk follows ownership; Art 1583: sale perfect on agreement on thing + price, even before delivery and payment
