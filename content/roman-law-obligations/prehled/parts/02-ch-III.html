Four Roman real contracts: mutuum · depositum · commodatum · pignus. All gratuitous in principle — exception: depositum irregulare (use of money, but interest owed). Precarium looks similar but is no contract (a factual grant).

Two kinds of trial

condictio = strict: only delivery, no interest. The others = bonae fidei iudicia: interest, counterclaims, retention.

Imperfectly bilateral

depositum, commodatum: return claim always; counterclaims (contraria) only possibly.

Paid variants are other contracts

paid keeping → locatio conductio; paid use → l. c. rei; interest → stipulatio.

Example: the mohatra normally serves the borrower → he bears accidental destruction; if it was (also) in the lender's interest to sell, he answers only for his own fault.

The standards, defined

  • dolus — deliberate harm. Close to it: culpa lata (gross negligence).
  • diligentia quam in suis — less care than for one's own things; subjective: lower for a careless man → breach = culpa in concreto (vs abstract standard = culpa in abstracto).
  • custodia — inadequate protection, typically against theft.
  • casus mixtus — accident that follows a dolose/unauthorised use → the user bears it.
casum sentit dominus — the owner bears accidental loss. That is why ownership decides risk: the depositor and the lender for use stay owners; the mutuum borrower becomes owner at the datio. → Property Ch. V
In property law custodia is the control needed to keep possessing a movable. Here it is a liability standard: answering for not guarding the thing. Same word, two jobs. → Property Ch. IV

Contrast: fenus nauticum

Maritime loan (Greek origin) for sea trade. Ship lost → borrower need not repay; ship arrives → principal + interest. The lender shares the venture's risk — close to a partnership or insurance.

Why so little mutuum in trade?

Gratuitous → mostly loans between friends. Big commercial loans used the stipulatio: principal and interest could be promised.

The lender's action: condictio

  • iudicium stricti iuris for a certa res / certa pecunia
  • money: actio certae creditae pecuniae; produce: condictio triticaria (triticum = wheat)
  • claims the equivalent: same kind, quantity, quality as received
  • no interest — needs another basis, e.g. a stipulatio
  • lender proves only the delivery; the rest is irrelevant → very efficient route to repayment

Borrower's defences

  • lender in blatant bad faith → exceptio doli
  • lender supplied pest-infested grain → actio de dolo, or exceptio doli against the condictio
Why a condictio and not the owner's rei vindicatio? After the datio the lender is no longer owner — he cannot reclaim "his" coins, only sue the borrower in personam for an equivalent. → Property Ch. X
The loan is a iusta causa traditionis (like sale, gift, dowry): the reason that makes the handover pass ownership. Julian/Ulpian on "gift vs loan" dissent show what happens when the parties disagree on it. → Property Ch. VII

Spot the real contract

Fungibles + "same amount back" + ownership passes → mutuum. Same pieces back → depositum / commodatum.

Case 2 · Ulpian D 12.1.15 — loan by agreement

Facts: under a mandate appointee B must pay money to principal A; instead they agree B may keep and use it for a while for his own business.

Decision: mutuum → A has the condictio — agreement between creditor and debtor is enough, no physical datio.

Why (analogy): what is accepted with two persons (drawee + borrower, loan on order) must hold when one person plays both roles. A shortcut: strictly B would hand the money to A and A straight back as a loan — Ulpian skips the back-and-forth.

Facts very close to Julian's Case 4 — who decides the opposite → next page.

Case 3 · Ulpian (Nerva) D 12.1.11 pr — contractus mohatrae

Facts: A gives B a bowl or a gold nugget to sell; B is to keep the proceeds as a loan.

Decision 1: mutuum once B has sold — the buyer's payment to B apparently counts as the datio.

Decision 2 — the real issue: the goods are lost by accident before the sale. Who bears it? Following Nerva: whose interest and initiative drove the deal?

accidental loss before the sale
A chiefly wanted the goods sold — B only a convenient sales agent
→ A bears the loss, no claim against B
A did not want to sell — only to lend B the proceeds
→ B bears the risk; A claims the value, presumably by actio praescriptis verbis

Why praescriptis verbis? Before the sale there is no mutuum even for Ulpian → innominate contract (→ Obligations Ch. X; cf Case 210, Ulpian (Labeo) D 19.5.19 pr). The split is the utility principle at work.

Case 4 · Africanus (Julian) D 17.1.34 pr — the procurator's loan

Facts: procurator P has collected money from Lucius Titius' (L) debtors. Wanting to use it himself, he writes proposing L lend it to him at 6 %; L agrees.

Question: mutuum with a condictio (actio certae creditae pecuniae)? Julian: no. But L sues P for principal and interest by actio mandati.

Why no mutuum — three steps:

  • 1no datio, only bare agreement
  • 2no traditio brevi manu either: P already was proprietary possessor and owner of the collected money — nothing left to "transfer". (Contrast: depositary Y asks to use the deposit as a loan → moves from natural to proprietary possession → owner → mutuum.)
  • 3no loan on order: that needs a prior conventio under which the recipient takes the money from the third party; here P collected first, the agreement came later

Instead: deferring the handover = consensual modification of the mandate → actio mandati.

+ b.f. trial covers the agreed 6 % (a condictio could not)
− every circumstance weakening the claim may be raised before the iudex

Risk: the loan serves only P → P bears it; repays even after accidental loss (utility principle).

traditio brevi manu: someone who already holds the thing for another (detentor, e.g. a depositary) becomes its possessor by mere agreement — no return and redelivery. That can be a datio; but P in Case 4 was already possessor, so there was nothing to convert. → Property Ch. III
Could a procurator acquire possession and ownership directly for his principal? A (perhaps classical) idea — but it plays no part in Julian's case. → Property Ch. III

Why: consuming the coins gives N their economic value — practically the same as ownership by datio. Ulpian seems to let this cure the loan (convalescence).

nemo plus iuris transferre potest quam ipse habet — nobody transfers more than he has. A thief owns nothing, so his "loan" cannot make N owner. → Property Ch. VII
Mixing someone's coins indistinguishably with your own makes you sole owner (even a thief); spending them in good faith = nummos consumere. The old owner then has only claims, no rei vindicatio. → Property Ch. IX → Ch. X

Depositum — safekeeping as a favour

book pp. 51–54
C.1 definition · C.3 allocation of risk

Definition

A thing is handed to someone who agrees to keep it gratuitously (cf § 957 ABGB; an animal must also be fed and cared for).

  • fungibles or not — but the very same items come back
  • whether the depositor owns them is generally irrelevant (unlike mutuum)
  • gratuitous — an act of friendship; paid → locatio conductio (Case 11)
  • no use: unauthorised use = furtum (Gai 3.196)

Actions — bonae fidei iudicia

depositor
⇄
depositary
  • ➜ actio depositi directa: above all return
  • ⇠ actio depositi contraria: expenses and losses — feed costs; his own herd infected by the deposited animals' disease
  • retention until paid — no exceptio doli needed (b.f. trial)
The depositary is only a detentor: he holds for the depositor, who stays owner and (mediate) possessor. If he uses or hides the thing he commits furtum. → Property Ch. II

Risk — base rule

Safekeeping is a favour; the depositary pursues no economic aim → fair to make him liable only for gross neglect.

depositary pays
dolus (Cases 11, 13) · culpa lata (Nerva, Case 12) · less care than for his own things — diligentia quam in suis (Celsus, Case 12; Gaius, Case 13)
depositor bears
mere slight negligence (culpa levis) and accident (casus) — he normally stays owner: casum sentit dominus

Treating the deposit worse than your own things looks dolose — hence the extension. The yardstick is his own habits: a careless man owes less (culpa in concreto).

Case 12 · Celsus (Nerva) D 16.3.32 (cited) — culpa lata and diligentia quam in suis as extensions of dolus.
Case 13 · Gaius D 44.7.1.5 (cited) — liability for dolus; care as for his own property.

Case 16 · Ulpian (Nerva, Proculus, Marcellus) D 12.1.9.9

Facts: A deposits 10 coins with B; later A allows B (probably at his request) to use them; afterwards the coins are taken from where they were kept (presumably by B).

Decision: the loan arises already with the agreed change + traditio brevi manu — not only when B takes the money out.

Consequence: B bears the risk of the 10 coins; A sues by condictio. Track B.

Typical motive: depositary needs money; a depositor sure of his solvency is content with the same amount instead of the same coins.

Case 17 · Ulpian D 12.1.10

Facts: A deposits money and at the same time permits B to use it if he wishes.

Decision: loan only once the money is actually removed.

Why: main aim = safekeeping → depositum; B's possible need is uncertain. A's permission = a standing offer giving B an option; by taking he accepts (loan or dep. irr. conventio) and the taking works as traditio brevi manu. Track C.

Silent on: interest; how to classify A–B after the taking.

Case 19 · Paulus Coll. 10.7.9

A deposits money and (possibly later) lets B use it: mutua magis videtur quam deposita — "more a loan than a deposit".

Track B or D? Cannot be settled — and need not be: either way the taker bears the periculum = repays regardless of fault.

Depositum sequestre

Two parties disputing ownership deposit the thing with a third person, the sequester, for the lawsuit; he hands it to whoever wins.

  • his possession is protected by possessory interdicts
  • winner sues him by actio depositi sequestraria
Interdicts protect possession as a fact, temporarily — normally not detentors, but by exception also the sequester, pledgee and precarist. The ownership dispute itself is fought with the rei vindicatio. → Property Ch. II → Ch. X

Commodatum — the gratuitous loan for use

book pp. 60–63
D.1 definition · D.2 risk · Cases 23, 25, 26

Definition

A thing is handed over (datio) so the recipient may use it free of charge.

  • the very same thing returns → only non-consumables
  • lender need not be owner (unlike mutuum)
  • gratuitous — paid use = rent, locatio conductio rei
  • use preserving the substance, within the agreement / bona fides; beyond = furtum (Gai 3.196)

Actions — bonae fidei iudicia

  • lender (commodator): actio commodati directa — return
  • borrower (commodatary): actio commodati contraria
  • imperfectly bilateral

Risk

borrower answers for
dolus, culpa and breach of custodia — the use benefits only him → fairly strict (Cases 23, 24)
lender bears
vis maior — as owner, casum sentit dominus (Case 26, Paul Sent 2.4.2)

But: if vis maior threatens and the borrower can still avert the harm, he must — not saving it = culpa.

The borrower for use is a detentor: he holds for the lender, who stays owner and possessor. Using the thing for a different purpose is furtum — that is why a thief-like borrower answers even for accidents. → Property Ch. II

Depositum or commodatum?

Ask: may the recipient use it? No → deposit (lenient, favour to the giver). Yes, free → commodate (strict, favour to the taker).

Case 23 · Gaius D 13.6.18 pr — three rules

① base rule
liable for harm from his fault, not for vis maior.
② excess use
liable even for vis maior if he used the thing beyond the permission and the accident results from that.
B borrows A's silverware "for a dinner", takes it on a journey → shipwreck, robbers, pirates → B liable.
Why: excess use = furtum; a fur answers for every harm, even vis maior.
③ lender also interested → liability can drop
C and D invite mutual friends; C cooks, D lends the silver.
Gaius: both interested → culpa.
Ulpian: only dolus if so agreed or the loan served solely the lender — jewels lent to his wife or fiancée so she is more richly adorned when brought to him (Case 25, Ulpian D 13.6.5.10).

One set of silverware, four answers — Cases 23 and 25 side by side

lent for B's dinner, used as agreed
B answers for dolus, culpa, custodia — vis maior stays with the lender
B takes it on a journey
excess use = furtum → B answers even for shipwreck, robbers, pirates
shared dinner (C cooks, D lends)
both interested → culpa (Gaius)
jewels lent to the bride
only the lender's interest → dolus only (Ulpian)

The pattern is the utility principle: liability falls as the lender's own interest rises. Practise: Ex 10 (pearl necklace — and the advertising variant).

Case 24 · Ulpian D 13.6.5.14 — the silver left overnight

Facts: T asks E to lay a table and lend silverware (laying = mandate, lending = commodate). Next day again; to avoid carrying it home E leaves the silver at T's house — destroyed overnight.

who bears the loss?
E posted a guard
→ E (Labeo): arranging the guard = assuming the risk
no guard
→ T liable — even if overnight it was "only" a deposit: then in the depositary's interest

Ulpian: use the actio commodati — the commodatary answers for custodia (so also dolus, culpa); any other allocation must be expressly agreed.

Controversy: Ulpian's custodia = theft / negligence; Labeo speaks of periculum, maybe vis maior — but then "T liable without a guard" clashes with the general rules.

Case 29 · Ulpian D 13.6.12.1 (fn)
Lender's slave S collects the item from borrower B and runs off. B handed it over meaning to end the loan → duty to return fulfilled. S was only sent to remind → B liable.

Third parties — how far does custodia reach?

Case 27 · Julian D 13.6.19
Damage by a third party (damnum iniuria datum) → borrower generally not liable: beyond the cura aut diligentia expected of him. (Julian also: a borrower cannot sue for borrowed clothes torn by a third party, Ulpian D 9.2.11.9.)
Dissent — Marcellus: liable if it could have been prevented, or if caused by the borrower's own guards. Ulpian (D 19.2.41) puts Julian's view into perspective.
Case 28 · Modestinus Coll 10.2.6
Thing stolen from the borrower → owner recovers from the borrower (custodia). The theft's burden falls on the commodatary → he has the actio furti.
Exception: commodatary insolvent → the owner may sue the thief himself.
Depositary: no actio furti — he does not need one, not being liable for theft by a third party.
Case 30 · Julian D 47.2.60 (59)
A lends a thing to B and secretly takes it back → B has no actio furti: A took his own property, which also frees B from returning it.
Exception: B was entitled to retain it for necessary expenses → actio furti against A.

Precarium — "loan by entreaty"

A factual grant of the use of a thing, which the grantor may revoke unilaterally at any time; usually at the borrower's request (Case 33, Ulpian D 43.26.1 pr).

Interdicts protect only a possessor who got the thing nec vi nec clam nec precario — not by force, secretly or by revocable grant — from his opponent. Against everyone else the precarist counts as unflawed; against the grantor his possession is flawed. → Property Ch. II

Pledgee's liability

A possessory pledge serves only the pledgee; the pledger gets no direct benefit → utility principle: dolus, culpa, custodia.

Pignus — one word, two things: keep them apart

real contract of pledge
arises by datio: a thing is given as security for a debt
valid even if the giver may not dispose → recipient is a contractual creditor but gets no right in rem
right of pledge (in rem)
arises without handover if:
asecured debt exists
bpledger may dispose
cconventio pignoris
The right of pledge is a limited right in rem: the creditor may sell the thing and pay himself from the proceeds — against any possessor (actio pigneraticia in rem). The contract on this page only regulates pledger and pledgee between themselves (in personam). → Property Ch. XII

Use and actions (b.f.)

No use — unauthorised use = furtum. Exception: antichresis — the use (partly) pays off the debt.

pledger → a. pigneraticia in personam directa
  • return (+ damages) when the pledge ends, e.g. debt paid
  • surplus of the sale over the debt: superfluum, hyperocha
pledgee → a. pigneraticia in personam contraria
  • expenses and damage caused by the thing
  • a substitute pledge if he got no right in rem (pledger lacked power of disposal)

Exegesis toolkit and the twelve exercises

book pp. 49–50, 68–74

Exegesis: a fragment on a real contract — ask

  • Which contract? What purpose did the conventio fix — consume, keep, use, secure? Gratuitous, or a paid variant (locatio conductio, stipulatio)?
  • Was there a datio? Physical handover, a third party paying (loan on order), traditio brevi manu, or only a bare agreement (Julian vs Ulpian)?
  • Property effect: does the recipient become owner (mutuum, dep. irr.) or stay detentor? Did the giver own or may he dispose (nemo plus iuris, Case 6)?
  • Whose interest? The typical one for the type, or a deviation (pressed services, lender's interest, mohatra)? → standard: dolus · culpa lata/dil. quam in suis · culpa · custodia.
  • Who bears the accident? casum sentit dominus; vis maior; casus mixtus after dolose / excess use.
  • Which action? condictio (strict: only delivery, no interest, exceptio doli) or a b.f. actio … directa / contraria (interest, retention, costs)? Interdicts for precarium, sequestre?
  • Third parties: theft or damage by others — actio furti for whom (Cases 27, 28, 30)?
  • Special protection: child in power → SC Macedonianum, father's approval, in rem verso.
  • Controversy: name the other jurist — Julian/Ulpian (loan by agreement, mohatra), Labeo/Ulpian (Case 24), Julian/Marcellus (Case 27), Gaius/Ulpian (Case 23).
