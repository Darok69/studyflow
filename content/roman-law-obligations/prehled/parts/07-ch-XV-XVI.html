B. Two foundations of a damages claim

Damages = the obligatory claim of the person harmed by another's forbidden conduct against the injurer.

duty to pay damages
ex contractu
breach of duties assumed by contract (also by succession, e.g. inheritance)
ex delicto
breach of duties the legal order imposes on everyone
Example 105 · bull Zeus Leonidas sells his breeding bull to Burrus for 1.000, delivery in a month. He changes his mind, slaughters and eats it. A comparable bull costs 1.200 → contractual damages 200.

Delicts = statutory definitions

  • duties arise from specific factual constellations; the definitions under which harm to protected objects is sanctioned are called delicts
  • an act is unlawful simply because it injures an object of absolute legal protection — one everyone must respect
  • core area: harm to property and to bodily integrity
Example 106 Lysippe gallops recklessly through crowded streets; her horse knocks down the old senator Cato → harm to Cato's bodily integrity. (Ex 103: arson = harm to property.)
"Absolute" protection works like a right in rem: ownership binds everyone, not just a contract partner — so anyone who damages my thing infringes it, without any prior relationship between us. → Property Ch. I

E. What is furtum?

1

another's movable

only movables: Gai 2.51 rejects the view that land can become "furtive"

2

visible taking

intent alone is not enough — it must show in an outward act of taking control

3

dolus

knows it is another's; intends to enrich himself unlawfully

  • core = modern theft (§ 127 StGB), but broader: embezzlement, misappropriation
  • furtum usus ("theft by use"): using a deposited thing, or a borrowed thing for another use (Gai 3.196)
Taking control (corpus) + intent to keep it for oneself (animus rem sibi habendi) are exactly the two elements by which possession is acquired. So the thief becomes possessor — never owner: his possession has no iusta causa, is defective towards the victim, and stolen things cannot be usucapted. → Property Ch. II → Ch. III

Who can sue for furtum?

  • primarily the owner, deprived of his thing by a third party
  • holders liable to their partner for theft (custodia): pledgees, borrowers — only if they are solvent
  • even the owner can commit furtum: taking his own thing in which another holds a right of retention (Julian D 47.2.60(59), Case 30)

Alternative competition: an owner-victim who could bring both must elect one before the praetor.

The rei vindicatio is the owner's action in rem: "wherever I find my thing, there I vindicate it" — against any possessor, but only while the thing exists (destruction ends ownership). Hence the thief-only condictio is the safer choice once the thing is gone. → Property Ch. X

G.1 dolus — malice

  • harming by malicious deceit / bad-faith conduct
  • actio de dolo: reipersecutory — compensation; any gain of the deceiver is handed back
  • subsidiary: only if the claimant has no other action for indemnification
  • for bona fides obligations (most contracts, negotiorum gestio) neither actio de dolo nor exceptio doli: the good-faith standard already covers deceit
  • exceptio doli: defendant pleads the claimant's deceit; key use = a right of retention against a restitution claim
Retention by exceptio doli is a property-law tool: e.g. a bona fide possessor keeps the thing against the vindicating owner until his expenses are refunded. → Property Ch. X

G.2 metus — coercion

  • harming by threat or force; needs a serious, unlawful or immoral restriction of freedom
  • with physical violence: subsidiary to other offences (e.g. robbery)
restitutio in integrum
praetor annuls the coerced act; coercer gives back
actio quod metus causa
penal if he does not restore — generally 4×
exceptio metus
against obligations entered into under coercion

Action and exception are framed "in rem": they name the fear, not the coercer → work also against his successors, even in good faith.

The lex Aquilia — chapters 1 and 3

A. General remarks · statutory text · pecudes

Main source of the Roman law of damages for harm to and destruction of property. Date uncertain — theories centre on the 3rd century BC; the classical picture probably differed considerably from the original wording ~300 years older. The jurists' interpretatio is inseparable from the statute — not mere interpretation but further development, itself a source of law. Basis of the later continental law of damages.

Occidere or mortis causam praestare?

book pp. 330–334
C.1–2 · Cases 263, 264 · the dog, the oxen, the bridge

occidere → actio legis Aquiliae

Active, immediate killing by direct physical action — si quis corpore suo damnum dederit (Gai Inst 3.219). Often with force (vis) — but vis is not required.

Source examples: bare hands (throttling), heel, head, sword, cudgel, projectile (D 9.2.7.1) · throwing down a load (D 9.2.7.2) · improperly performed operation (D 9.2.7.8) · giving poison to drink (D 9.2.9 pr)

mortis causam praestare → actio in factum / utilis

Death of a ch.-1 object without direct action — by omission or indirect action: the injurer "provided a cause of death" — liable if unlawful and culpable.

Omission: letting a slave one must feed starve (D 9.2.9.2) · taking his clothes so he freezes (D 19.5.14.1) · Indirect: making a horse shy so it throws the slave rider into a river · luring a slave into a trap where B kills him: A in factum, B occidere (D 9.2.9.3)

Midwife — Ulpian (Labeo) D 9.2.9 pr (Case 264)
A midwife gives medicine to a (slave) woman, who dies.
• put it into her mouth with her own hands → occidere → actio legis Aquiliae
• only handed it over, the slave took it herself → mortis causam praestare → actio in factum
Why: who performed the last physical act?
Poison and sword — Ulpian (Celsus) D 9.2.7.6 (Case 263)
• A gives C's slave poison instead of medicine; B swallows it → actio in factum
• D hands a sword to F's mentally ill slave E, who kills himself (or someone) → actio in factum
Why: the victim acts by his own hand — the giver only provided the cause.
Bridge — Ulpian (Celsus) D 9.2.7.7 (Case 263)
G pushes J's slave H off a bridge: (1) the push kills, (2) he sinks and dies at once, (3) he struggles, then drowns → always actio legis Aquiliae.
Why: the push actively set the chain going — corpore suo; immediate or later death does not matter.
Rock — D 9.2.7.7
K hurls M's slave boy L against a rock; L dies of the impact → actio legis Aquiliae.
Why: though the rock kills, the perpetrator had direct physical contact with the victim.
Push onto another — Ulpian (Proculus) D 9.2.7.3
N pushes O, who falls against Q's slave P; P dies.
• N: no contact with P → actio in factum
• O: contact, but not liable — quia damnum non dederit. Open: outside the definition, not unlawful, or not culpable? A modern lawyer: O cannot be held responsible at all.
Oxen — Neratius D 9.2.53
R drives S's oxen onto a ridge; they fall → actio in factum.
Why: driving is not physical contact causing injury. Cf Gaius D 47.2.51: a herd driven into an abyss → actio utilis.
Dog — Ulpian (Proculus, Julian) D 9.2.11.5 · T provokes a dog, which bites V's slave U.
• Proculus: actio legis Aquiliae without distinction · Julian (restrictive): only if T was holding the dog, otherwise actio in factum — only a held dog is T's direct instrument.
• Counterpoint: a thrown spear also counts as occidere → Proculus may have seen setting an unheld dog on U as direct harm, too.

Ask in every killing case

Who made the last physical contact? Did the victim act by his own hand? Instrument held or released? Is death later than the act (irrelevant for occidere)?

Causation — an excursus

book pp. 340–343
D. condicio sine qua non · adequacy · special problems

Every liability regime: one answers only for harm one has caused — in modern law often the decisive issue. In the Aquilian scheme it belongs to stage 1.

a. condicio sine qua non — equivalence

act: mentally remove it — does the injury disappear? → causal
omission: mentally add the correct conduct — does the injury disappear? → causal

No ranking of primary and secondary causes: all conditions count as equivalent.

b. Adequate causation (modern)

Liability only for adequately caused harm: the cause must not be wholly untypical for the injury. Simplified: only conduct that typically leads to such an injury.

Example 108 · New Year's Eve firecracker

A sets off a firecracker
➜
singes B's coat
CSQN ✓ adequate ✓ → liable
➜
flashes trigger C's psychosis; he jumps and dies
CSQN ✓ adequate ✗

A firecracker is not typically frightening enough to drive someone to death.

Cheat sheet

problemRoman answer
typified direct actcausation + adequacy implied
atypical harmLabeo: light push, sickly slave → liable · Paulus: wound + owner's neglect → only vulnerare
overtaking · alternative · cumulativeeach liable in full

2. The Roman approach

  • no explicit distinction between equivalent and adequate causation
  • the typified acts (occidere, urere, frangere, rumpere) are confined to active, direct impact — they already imply causation and adequacy
  • adequacy matters more in the analogous cases; where death occurs without direct impact both must first be examined
Sickly slave — Labeo in Ulpian D 9.2.7.5 A lightly pushes a sickly slave and so causes his death → liable under the lex Aquilia: "different things are fatal to different people". Would hardly count as adequate today.
Neglected wound — Paulus D 9.2.30.4
A gives B's slave a non-fatal wound; the slave dies because B neglects proper medical care.
→ A liable only for vulnerare (= rumpere, ch. 3), not for occidere (cf D 9.2.52 pr).
Why: the wound is causal for death but presumably not its adequate cause; A does not answer for the owner's neglect.

Exam angle

Ask: is the act a typified direct one (causation settled)? If not — would the death have occurred without it (CSQN), and is it typical (adequacy)? Did a third act (owner's neglect, another attacker) intervene?

3. Special problems (modern terms)

a. Overtaking causation

A: mortal wound
➜
before it takes effect B beats the slave to death
  • both liable — neither escapes by pointing at the other
  • B: occidere; A: occidere only if the mortal wound itself suffices (Julian D 9.2.51 pr, 2), else vulnerare (Celsus, Marcellus D 9.2.11.3)
  • for the amount it hardly matters: a mortal wound ≈ killing

b. Alternative causation

A or B — who did it? unknown
➜
each liable as if he alone had caused it

A presumption of causation suffices; full liability of each justified by the penal character (D 9.2.51.1; D 9.2.11.2).

c. Cumulative causation

A's act alone would kill · B's act alone would kill
➜
each could say "think me away — still dead"

Yet each is liable as if he alone had caused it (D 9.2.11.4) — provided unlawful and culpable.

Two attackers, two chapters

X injures a slave non-fatally
➜
Y then injures him mortally

→ ch. 3 action against X, ch. 1 action against Y → concurrences

F.1 Self-defence — six tests

= repelling a present or imminent unlawful attack on one's own legal interests.

a
current attack — once it is over, no self-defence
b
unlawful attack — none against lawful acts (state compulsion), none against self-defence. Attacker need not be at fault: also against a mentally disabled person or a minor
c
protectable interest: life, physical integrity, property
d
proportionality — plausible relation to the defended interest; otherwise excessive force = unlawful. A cheap oil lamp does not justify destroying an eye
e
necessary minimum — no more harm than strictly needed
f
no third parties — harm to them is not justified (Paulus D 9.2.45.4)

Defence of others

Protecting another's legal interests — justified if the other requirements are met.

Putative self-defence

Defender wrongly believes in an attack → harm not justified; but fault may be excluded if he is not to blame.

Excursus: killing a fur manifestus

The Twelve Tables let anyone kill a thief who (a) defends himself with weapons or (b) is caught in the act at night. A trace of early law; doubtful in the classical period (Coll 7.3.2) — killing may exceed an appropriate defence of property. Alfenus does not even consider killing the lantern thief.

fur manifestus = the thief caught in the act. In the theft actions this matters for the penalty: furtum manifestum → fourfold, covert theft → double. → Property Ch. II

3. Permitted self-help

The law sometimes lets a person enforce his rights himself, even if this harms the other.

Water pipe — A lays a pipe through B's house without permission; B removes it → justified, A has no claim (rescript of Alexander Severus, D 9.2.29.1)
Canopy — C's canopy overhangs D's house; D tears it down himself → Proculus: liable — he should have sued for removal
Property law knows the same idea: vim vi repellere licet — one who has been driven off by force may retake the thing by force; one of the few cases of permitted self-help. → Property Ch. II
Mare — Pomponius (Q. Mucius) D 9.2.39 pr–1
E's pregnant mare grazes on F's pasture; F drives her off; she miscarries.
• Q. Mucius: F liable only if he beat her or chased her too vigorously / maliciously → other driving-off justified (necessity / self-help)
• Pomponius (much later): no justification — F liable for all harm from the chasing

4. Authorisation by the legal system

Officials may encroach on rights in the public interest: iuris executio non habet iniuriam (D 47.10.13.1).

  • allowed: seize assets by force (D 9.2.29.7) · smash beds put on a public road (D 18.6.14) · pull down houses against fire (D 43.24.7.4)
  • exceeded: seized assets returned damaged → actio legis Aquiliae; seized animals starve because owners may not feed them → actio in factum (D 9.2.29.7)

5. Consent of the injured party

The person at risk declares that he assumes the risk → harm is not unlawful.

  • glass cutters: by agreement not liable for accidental frangere of glasses accepted for cutting (D 9.2.27.29) — a wilful act stays unjustified
  • slave entered in a contest by his master: injuries within the rules → no claim (D 9.2.7.4) — the master's consent, so "consent of the injured" is a misnomer
  • surgery: consent covers only an operation lege artis

When the victim was careless

All or nothing

Several decisions denying liability seem to rest on the victim's own carelessness. Roman law does not split the loss:

Rome
injurer pays 100 %
or
injurer pays 0 %
modern (e.g. § 1304 ABGB)
injurer's share
victim bears the rest

Usual label "contributory negligence" is questionable: carelessness towards one's own interests is not negligence in the technical sense.

Two modern explanations

▪ Culpa compensation

The injurer's fault is offset against the victim's carelessness; if the latter weighs at least as much, the injurer's culpa counts as compensated. dolus is too heavy to be offset.

▪ Acting at one's own risk

Someone voluntarily enters a typically dangerous situation (dangerous sports, trespassing) → he assumes the risk; the injurer's duty of protection lapses → no fault at all.

K. Concurrences of claims

The actio legis Aquiliae is an actio mixta — reipersecutory and penal (Gai 4.9). As a reipersecutory action it cannot in principle be cumulated with other reipersecutory actions (very probably the same for the analogous actions). Three answers appear in the sources:

⇄ choose

one action excludes the other

+ top up

after one action, sue under the other for the difference

＋＋ cumulate

both actions in full

1. With contract and reipersecutory claims

⇄ contract or delict Where both give a claim, the victim generally has to choose (cf the oven case: actio ex locato bars the Aquilian claims).
+ Paulus D 44.7.34.2 A lender of clothes (commodatum) gets them back damaged and recovers with the actio commodati → may additionally sue under the lex Aquilia for the excess of the delictual claim (amounts can differ: highest value of the year).
⇄ rei vindicatio Owner compensated via the vindication → must give up the Aquilian action (D 6.1.13). Same for an heir suing by hereditatis petitio for a killed inherited slave (D 5.3.36.2).
The rei vindicatio is the owner's action to get his thing back from a possessor — or its value if it is not restored. Since both it and the Aquilian action compensate the same loss, he cannot collect twice. → Property Ch. X

Who sues whom? Claimants and noxal liability

book pp. 365–369
L. Claimants, defendants; noxal liability

1. Claimants — the circle widens

owner — actio directa (D 9.2.11.6)
co-owners: each pro rata, even against a co-owner (D 9.2.19, 20)
⇣ analogous actions for others
usufructuary / usuary — for harm to the thing they hold rights in rem over (Julian: Aquilian; Ulpian: utilis) · Paulus: even against the owner (D 9.2.12)
pledgee — up to his secured claim; excess → owner · Paulus: only if no other satisfaction (debtor insolvent) · also against the owner (D 9.2.17)
bona fide possessor — analogous action (D 9.2.11.8), even against the owner (D 9.2.17)
usufructuary lessee — against whoever damages it, with security that the owner will not also sue (D 9.2.27.14)
custodia obligors (lessee, borrower) — should get one if they answer for the damage · Julian disagrees: not liable to the lessor, so no action needed (D 13.6.19, Case 27)
pater familias of the injured shoemaker's apprentice (Case 276) — whether free Romans' injuries gave claims elsewhere is left open

Active heritability: the claim passes to the heir(s); loss between death and entry → belongs to the hereditas iacens.

Usufruct = right to use another's thing and take its fruits, keeping its substance intact (Paulus D 7.1.1); a pledge = a right in rem in a thing securing a debt. Both holders lose value when the thing is damaged — hence their own action. → Property Ch. XI → Ch. XII
A bona fide possessor wrongly but honestly believes he is the owner (e.g. bought from a non-owner) and is on the way to ownership by usucapion. Co-owners each hold an ideal quota. → Property Ch. VIII → Ch. V

2. Defendants

  • as a rule the injurer himself — without restriction if a citizen sui iuris
  • denies responsibility before the praetor and is found liable → double (duplum, litiscrescence; Gai 4.9)

Noxal liability — step by step

Slaves, daughters in power, uxores in manu cannot be sued under civil law; sons in power can, but judgment cannot be enforced → is the power-holder liable?

aa
assess the actual perpetrator (the slave): definition, unlawfulness, fault — amount
bb
if accountable → sue the principal: whoever holds power when the action is brought — noxa caput sequitur
cc
principal chooses: pay compensation or surrender the perpetrator — noxae deditio (D 9.4.2 pr). Surrender pays off when damages exceed the slave's value; the victim bears the rest.
dd
no surrender if the principal knew and could have prevented it — incl. all harm done on his orders → actio directa as if he acted himself: the dependant is his "tool" (Aquilian for direct, analogous for indirect harm)

Passive heritability

perpetrator dies
➜
liability ends — not passed to heirs (Gai 4.112; D 9.2.23.8)
·
slave dies or is freed → noxal liability ends

Exception: heirs answer to the extent the deceased was enriched by the delict.

Ex 103–116

Exam: a Digest fragment on the lex Aquilia — ask

  1. Who is the claimant — owner? co-owner, usufructuary, pledgee, bona fide possessor, pater familias? Who the defendant — a slave (→ noxal)?
  2. Object: slave or pecus killed → ch. 1; anything else / injury only → ch. 3
  3. Verb: occidere, urere, frangere, (cor)rumpere? Or mortis causam praestare?
  4. Directness: corpore suo, contact, instrument? → actio directa · else in factum / utilis (which jurist says which?)
  5. Thing only taken away or consumed? Intent to enrich → furtum
  6. Causation: intervening acts (owner's neglect, second attacker), alternative / cumulative?
  7. Justification: attack (self-defence) or danger (necessity)? Current, proportionate? Self-help, magistrate, consent?
  8. Fault: dolus / culpa / casus — which bonus standard, which place? Incapacity, orders?
  9. Victim's carelessness: own risk or culpa compensation — all or nothing
  10. Amount: highest value (from death or wound?) / 30 days; extra interest; no affection value
  11. Concurrences: contract, furtum, rei vindicatio, ch. 1 + ch. 3
  12. Controversy: do two jurists disagree (Julian vs Proculus, Q. Mucius vs Paulus / Pomponius, Celsus vs Servius)? Why?
