1Five eggs and an earthquake — Lucius / Quintus → Ch. III

Facts: Lucius "borrows" five hen's eggs from Quintus for baking and agrees to give back the same number the next day. Before they are used, an earthquake destroys the eggs.
a · Which contract?
➜
real contract mutuum = conventio + datio of fungibles; return tantundem eiusdem generis ac qualitatis
➜
datio: Quintus hands over 5 eggs ✓ · conventio: same number back next day ✓ · not commodatum (fn 2): eggs are to be consumed, not returned unharmed
➜
MUTUUM
b · Lucius' property position before the quake?
➜
possession = control + intent for himself; ownership by traditio (derivative)
➜
control + intent as borrower → proprietary possessor ✓ · Quintus owner (assumed) = authorised transferor ✓ · mutuum = iusta causa ✓
➜
Lucius owner
c · What may Quintus demand?
➜
claim to 5 eggs under the loan → condictio triticaria (loan of goods, not money)
➜
loss by vis maior (earthquake) hits the owner: casum sentit dominus; Lucius has been owner since delivery
➜
Lucius owes 5 eggs
Traditio passes ownership only through three gates: an owner (or authorised person) hands over, possession really passes, and there is a iusta causa. The mutuum itself is such a cause, so the eggs were Lucius' the moment he took them, and the quake destroyed his property. → Property Ch. VII

2The vase that belonged to Pericles — Leo / Zenon → Ch. VI

Facts: Leo sells Zenon a vase that belongs to a third party for 200 and gives a stipulatio duplae against eviction. Zenon pays at once and receives it. A month later the owner Pericles finds the vase with Zenon; Zenon pays him 230 to keep it.
Zenon v Leo on the stipulation (2 × 200 = 400)?
➜
stipulatio duplae: Leo promises double the price if Zenon is evicted
➜
eviction = a third party wins an action in rem; the buyer surrenders the thing or is condemned to pay its value (also: buyer sues the possessor and loses, Pomponius D 21.2.16.1). Pericles never sued ✗
➜
no 400
Zenon v Leo under the sale?
➜
emptio venditio (vase + 200): buyer is owed delivery and undisturbed possession; title warranty only after eviction (principle of eviction)
➜
Zenon keeps the vase only ex alia causa (he bought it again from the owner). Not technically eviction, but treated as equal under bona fides (Pomponius D 21.2.29 pr, Case 106)
➜
actio empti: 230
expectation interest
Leo was not owner: nemo plus iuris transferre potest quam ipse habet, so his delivery gave Zenon possession only. Pericles stayed owner and could have taken the vase from any possessor by rei vindicatio. Losing to that action is exactly what "eviction" means. → Property Ch. VI → Property Ch. X

3The wine cellar — Ago (buyer) / Brontes (seller) → Ch. V

Facts: On 1 March Ago buys the whole contents of a cellar (60 amphorae) from wine trader Brontes. Ago is to collect on 15 March; until Ago takes possession then, Brontes bears the risk of deterioration or loss.
1 Marchsale concluded and perfect: object + price, goods specified (whole cellar), no condition
10 Marchearthquake breaks 10 amphorae → question a
15 Marchagreed delivery: Ago loads 20, refuses 30 without good reason
17 Marchearthquake breaks the 30 → question b
a · Must Ago pay for the 10?
➜
subsequent impossibility; seller not at fault → not liable. Liability for vis maior only from a guarantee — none here (fn 5) → Ago bears the performance risk
➜
payment risk: rule periculum est emptoris from perfection — sale perfect on 1 March ✓. But the parties put the risk on Brontes until 15 March; the rule is only ius dispositivum and gives way
➜
Ago does not pay for the 10
b · What must Ago pay after 17 March?
➜
20 taken: seller performed → price due. 30 refused: properly offered at the agreed time, not taken = mora creditoris
➜
risk clause covers only conclusion → agreed delivery date ✗ no longer applies. A buyer in default of acceptance bears accidental loss → for the 30 Ago bears performance and payment risk
➜
pays for 50 (20 + 30) · Brontes: actio venditi
Contract ≠ conveyance. On 1 March the sale alone makes Ago neither possessor nor owner; ownership would pass only with traditio on 15 March. So in sale, risk follows perfection of the contract, not ownership. That is why Ago can carry the risk of wine that is still Brontes' property. → Property Ch. III → Property Ch. VII

Two risks — keep them apart

riskmeans
performance riskI get no thing and no damages (the seller is freed)
payment riskI must still pay the price although I get nothing

Payment risk only matters for the party who already carries the performance risk. The book always asks performance risk first, then payment risk.

The risk ladder used here

  • Default: casum sentit dominus
  • Sale, from perfection: buyer bears vis maior twice over (periculum est emptoris)
  • Agreement may shift it — the rule is ius dispositivum; read the clause's time limit
  • Default of acceptance: the risk goes back to the buyer who refused

Same pattern in the exam cases

  • 6 Nike's ox: the same clause (seller bears risk) + buyer fails to turn up — solve it like this case
  • 5 mules · 7 horse burnt in default · 8 donkey before delivery · 18d slave dead before the sale

Method notes

  • Test perfection element by element (object, price, specified goods, no suspensive condition).
  • State the general rule, then why it is displaced (agreement) and why it comes back (default).
  • End with a number and the action: "Ago pays for 50; actio venditi".

Why a condictio and not a rei vindicatio for the 200? A payment is a datio: the coins handed over became Brontes' property, so Carus is no longer owner and cannot vindicate them. He can only claim the value back in personam. If the recipient had not become owner, the payer would keep the rei vindicatio. → Property Ch. VII → Property Ch. X
Lesson 1 — accessoriness. First compute what the debtor still owes on the day of payment; the surety never owes more.
Lesson 2 — duty to inform. Whoever knows about a payment must tell the other side; the one who stays silent bears the cost.
Lesson 3 — assignment. A claim cannot simply be transferred; use the mandatum in rem suam so that nobody is paid twice.

What the five model solutions teach — your checklist

  1. Classify the transaction first (mutuum not commodatum; sale; fideiussio + mandatum).
  2. Property position when it matters: who possesses, who owns, who carries casum sentit dominus.
  3. One row per claim: who v whom, for what, by which action.
  4. Requirements one by one, each tied to a fact; when the rule bends (risk clause, ex alia causa, duty to inform), say why.
  5. Result with a figure and the action: 230 · 50 amphorae · 600 · 200 · 250 · nothing.

1A statue sold to fund a loan → Ch. III → Ch. X

Facts: Asked for a loan, the lender tells the borrower to collect her bronze statue from a woman who keeps it in deposit, sell it for at least 50,000 and use the proceeds interest-free for a year. He collects it.

Q: lender's claims and actions if (a) before the sale the statue, properly secured, falls off the cart in a tornado; (b) it is sold for 60,000 cash. issues to check

  • contractus mohatrae: Ulpian (Nerva) D 12.1.11 pr — mutuum only once he has sold; the buyer's payment counts as the datio. Julian: no mutuum at all disputed
  • (a) no loan yet → no condictio. Risk by whose interest drove the deal: sale chiefly for the lender → she bears it; only to lend him the proceeds → he bears it, she claims the value by actio praescriptis verbis (Case 210). He asked, she proposed the statue open
  • (b) loan arises at the sale → condictio after a year, no interest. Loan of 50,000 or 60,000? The book has no rule on the surplus open
Until the sale the statue stays the lender's: the depositary and then the borrower only hold it for her (detention), so an accident hits the owner unless the deal shifts it. The borrower, authorised to sell, can still make the buyer owner by traditio. → Property Ch. II → Property Ch. VII

2The uncle's moneybag lent by mistake → Ch. III → Ch. XIII

Facts: 1 Jan: interest-free loan of 100,000 for three months; a third man stands surety. By mistake the lender hands over a bag her uncle had deposited with her. The borrower pours the coins into his coffers and spends them, partly on marble slabs he then sells her for 100,000 (unpaid). She demands 100,000 from the surety.

Q: assess the lender's claim against the guarantor. issues to check

  • At delivery no mutuum: another's coins (Ulpian D 12.1.13 pr, Case 6). Once mixed / spent in good faith the borrower becomes owner → condictio arises, the loan is "cured".
  • Surety accessory, but Paulus D 46.1.56.2 (Case 51 a): he stands for every legal ground arising from the transfer of the money → liable once the coins are spent.
  • He may raise the debtor's defences that go to the debt (Marcian D 44.1.19). Set-off with the unpaid marble price: the notes give no rule on set-off by a surety open
Coins mixed with one's own become the mixer's property (original acquisition). Before mixing the uncle could still vindicate them; afterwards only a condictio is left. → Property Ch. IX

3Julia's brooch at the party → Ch. III → Ch. XV

Facts: Julia gives Ismene a brooch for safekeeping. Two weeks later Ismene takes it from her safe and wears it to an evening event; on the way home robbers take it.

Q: What is the legal situation? issues to check

  • depositum: gratuitous, actio depositi directa; depositary liable for dolus (+ culpa lata / diligentia quam in suis); casus normally on the depositor.
  • Using a deposit = furtum (Gai 3.196, furtum usus).
  • Dolose use → liable for every accident it leads to: casus mixtus (Ulpian D 16.3.1.25, Case 15) → Ismene owes the value, though robbery is vis maior.
  • Against the robbers: actio furti (penalty) + condictio furtiva or rei vindicatio (choose one).
Robbery does not end Julia's ownership: the robbers only possess. The brooch is now a res furtiva that nobody, not even a good-faith buyer, can usucapt (lex Atinia), so Julia can vindicate it from whoever holds it. → Property Ch. II → Property Ch. VIII

4Stichus and Pamphila → Ch. IV → Ch. XIV

Facts: Dexter agrees to sell Nike two slaves. (a) Price: whatever "our friend Lucius will fix". (b) Variant: 25,000, paid at once; Pamphila had died days before the sale (Dexter unaware). As a circus pair they would resell for 35,000.

Q: (a) contract formed? (b) validity; claims between them? issues to check

  • (a) pretium certum; price set by a third party (Gaius Inst 3.140, Case 56): Labeo, Cassius — no sale; Ofilius, Proculus — valid, Lucius to estimate as a vir bonus. Formed at agreement (Proculus) or when the figure is accepted (Labeo) disputed
  • (b) initial objective impossibility for Pamphila. Partial → id quod actum est: a circus pair (cf Ex 30) + lump sum (Marcianus D 18.1.44) → whole sale void.
  • Nike: 25,000 back by condictio indebiti. The 10,000 resale profit is expectation interest → not owed. Reliance loss only with seller's fault? Pomponius/Celsus v Modestinus disputed

5Tiro's team of mules → Ch. IV → Ch. V

Facts: 15 March: Tiro buys a team of two mules from Varus for 10,000, pays 5,000 down; collection and the other 5,000 on 1 April. Tiro arrives with a slave hired for 50. (a) One mule had died on 10 March (Varus unaware). (b) One mule died of natural causes a few hours earlier. The survivor is worth 4,000.

Q: claims between Tiro and Varus in (a) and (b). issues to check

  • (a) death before 15 March = initial impossibility (partial). A team = one economic unit → whole sale void, 5,000 back by condictio; or divisible → survivor at a reduced price (id quod actum est) open
  • The 50 = reliance loss; owed only if the seller's fault is required and present — Varus did not know disputed
  • (b) sale perfect on 15 March (price fixed, team specified); natural death = vis maior → periculum est emptoris: Tiro gets the survivor and still owes the other 5,000; he bears his own 50.

6Nike's ox and the risk clause → Ch. V

Facts: 1 May: Nike sells her ox to Gaius for 7,000; he is to fetch it on 15 May; the seller is to bear the periculum. (a) 13 May: lightning kills the ox. (b) 15 May: Gaius does not turn up; two days later lightning kills it.

Q: What is the legal situation in (a) and (b)? issues to check

  • Lightning = vis maior; sale perfect on 1 May → normally the buyer pays.
  • (a) the clause displaces the rule (non-mandatory) → Nike bears performance and payment risk → Gaius pays nothing, gets nothing.
  • (b) ox properly offered, not collected → Gaius in mora creditoris; the clause covers only the time up to the agreed collection → risk back on Gaius → pays 7,000 (actio venditi). Whether his default needs fault: "on balance unlikely".
Model: this is XVII Case 3 (Ago's amphorae) in miniature — copy its two-step structure.

7Calypso dies in the fire → Ch. V → Ch. XIII

Facts: 1 Jan: Claudia buys the horse Calypso from Hermes for 20,000; delivery and payment 10 Jan at his stud farm. In her presence Xerxes stands surety. 10 Jan: she falls ill and does not come; that night a fire caused by Hermes' slight negligence kills Calypso. Claudia refuses to pay.

Q: (a) Can Hermes sue Xerxes for 20,000? (b) Xerxes pays — what then? issues to check

  • First the main debt (accessoriness). mora creditoris: horse offered, not taken; fault not needed "on balance" → illness does not help Claudia open
  • In default of acceptance the seller answers only for dolus + culpa lata → slight negligence frees Hermes; Claudia pays. Without default: culpa → Hermes liable, no price.
  • (a) Xerxes owes what Claudia owes → 20,000 (actio ex stipulatu), with her defences.
  • (b) surety in her presence = mandatum (D 17.1.6.2) → actio mandati contraria v Claudia; or assignment of Hermes' claim.

8Cato's donkey before delivery → Ch. V

Facts: 1 March: carrier Cato sells Iris a donkey (worth 650) for 600; delivery 1 April. Meanwhile he keeps using it. 20 March, on a transport supervised by his son Gripus, it stumbles into a pothole and must be put down.

Q: What is the legal situation? issues to check

  • Sale perfect 1 March → buyer bears vis maior only. A stumble: vis maior or lesser accident (preventable by utmost care)?
  • Seller answers for dolus + culpa. Using the sold animal, cf D 19.1.54 pr: Labeo — not liable if he used it as he would an unsold one (diligentia quam in suis); Paulus — liable if a careful pater familias would not have disputed
  • custodia in sale disputed: with it → expectation interest (650; 50 net if price unpaid); without → Iris bears only performance risk: nothing paid, nothing owed.
  • Fault of the son as helper: the notes give no rule open

9The locked door — Irene's land → Ch. V

Facts: 1 Jan: Irene sells and conveys land to Leo for 12,000 in monthly instalments of 1,000, payable at her house on the 1st; if he pays late, the land counts as unsold. He pays five; on 1 June her door is locked; on 2 June she claims the land back.

Q: What is the legal situation? issues to check

  • lex commissoria (Pomponius D 18.3.2): a resolutive condition of non-payment, but not automatic — it gives the seller an option to withdraw once the buyer is in default.
  • Debtor's default needs his fault (si per eum stat); debtor's and creditor's delay exclude each other. Leo offered properly; the locked door = Irene's mora creditoris → Leo not in default → no withdrawal. The book does not apply this to the clause expressly open
  • If she could withdraw: the iusta causa lapses, she vindicates and Leo loses the exceptio rei venditae et traditae.
If the land is Italian it is a res mancipi: by mere traditio Leo is only bonitarian owner and Irene keeps the bare quirite title. Against her rei vindicatio Leo pleads the exceptio rei venditae et traditae — unless the clause took his causa away. → Property Ch. VII → Property Ch. X

10Leaking amphora, a vase given by its owner → Ch. VI

Facts: 1 May: Leander sells Bellona an amphora (1,000) and a vase (2,000); delivery and payment 1 June. (a) Next day oil worth 500 has leaked through a tiny crack; the amphora is now only decorative. (b) Her uncle Carus recognises the vase (sold with a stipulatio duplae) as his, once deposited with Leander's father, and gives it to her.

Q: claims between Bellona and "Ago" (book's slip for Leander) in (a), (b). issues to check

  • (a) a leaking vessel is defective (Case 114). Aedilician remedies only for slaves and animals at market → actio empti: price reduction or rescission.
  • The 500 oil (consequential loss): only for a knowing seller, or — Labeo — whoever sells vessels answers for their soundness anyway. Leander's knowledge not stated disputed
  • (b) no eviction → no claim on the stipulatio duplae (Paulus D 21.2.9). Possession now ex alia causa, like buying from the owner (Case 106: actio empti). A gift cost her nothing — whether and how much is owed is not settled open
Leander could not make Bellona owner (nemo plus iuris). Carus' gift is a traditio by the owner with donatio as cause; as she already holds the vase, the change of intent suffices (traditio brevi manu) → now she owns it. → Property Ch. III → Property Ch. VII

11Helena's willow boughs → Ch. IV → Ch. V

Facts: July: basket weaver Felix agrees with Helena that he will get the boughs of her willows in October and pays the whole price (500) at once. In October she refuses to let the boughs be cut.

Q: (a) Felix wants to vindicate the boughs; (b) Helena: no valid contract was ever made. issues to check

  • (a) a rei vindicatio needs ownership. The sale itself transfers nothing; the boughs are still part of her trees → hers → vindication fails. Felix's remedy: actio empti for his expectation interest.
  • (b) a sale of future things is valid: emptio rei speratae (conditional on the thing coming into being) or emptio spei (the hope itself, unconditional). A lump price for the year's boughs points to emptio spei (cf "whole harvest for 50,000"). Either way valid; her refusal = non-performance.
Before separation, fruits are part of the mother thing and belong to its owner. Felix would become owner only when Helena hands them over (traditio), e.g. by letting him cut and take them. → Property Ch. IX → Property Ch. X

12Laura's two leased horses → Ch. VII

Facts: Flavius lets Laura use two horses for two months; she pays 150 per horse per month. (a) After one month one horse goes lame; Laura proves 200 lost income. (b) After two months she must surrender the second horse to Agathon, who proved his ownership in court.

Q: Which claims exist between Laura and Flavius? issues to check

  • locatio conductio rei: lessor must provide a usable thing; actio conducti / locati.
  • (a) unusable horse → remissio mercedis or termination, only ex nunc: 150 for month 1, nothing for month 2. The 200: yes if Flavius knew of the defect (D 19.2.30 pr); unaware lessor — Cassius (leaky barrels) still grants the interest disputed. Lameness: vis maior or defect of the thing? open
  • (b) eviction of the lessee: lessor liable for her interest (D 19.2.9 pr). It came at the end of the term → little or no loss; 300 rent for horse 2 owed.
Laura held the horse only as detentor, yet Agathon could sue her: Ulpian lets the rei vindicatio run against any holder able to restore (facultas restituendi); Pegasus wanted interdict possession. → Property Ch. X

13Marcus pays 105,000 → Ch. XIII → Ch. III → Ch. XIV

Facts: Lea, with her uncle Marcus, asks Helena for a loan; Marcus stands surety; Lea gets a mutuum of 100,000 for six months. At maturity Helena demands 105,000 (5,000 interest) from Marcus; unsure whether interest was stipulated, he pays. He tells Lea nothing. A week later Helena's procurator asks Lea for 105,000; she pays 100,000. Marcus wants 105,000 from Lea.

Q: Is Marcus' demand lawful? issues to check

  • Interest: a mutuum yields only tantundem; interest needs a stipulatio (none shown) → Lea never owed 5,000 → nor did Marcus (accessoriness).
  • Surety at her request, in her presence → mandatum. But he paid and kept silent, then she paid again: Ulpian D 17.1.29.3 (Case 177) — silence ≈ culpa lata → no actio mandati contraria; Lea must assign him her condictio indebiti v Helena for 100,000.
  • The 5,000: condictio indebiti Marcus v Helena — needs a mistake; does "unsure" count? (who knowingly pays a non-debt gets nothing) open
  • Mirror of XVII Case 4: there the debtor was silent, here the surety.

14A racehorse bought with a lost letter → Ch. XI

Facts: Bellona's slave Nastes runs an art shop as his peculium (5,000). Before a journey she gives him a letter authorising him to buy Rufus' racehorse for 6,000. Nastes loses it; Rufus sells him the horse for 6,000 without ever learning of the letter. Rufus is never paid. (b) Nastes is not her slave but her free (sui iuris) shop manager.

Q: Whom can Rufus sue, with which actions, for how much — (a) and (b)? issues to check

  • (a) Nastes' own debt = natural obligation only. quod iussu needs the iussum made known to the third party who relies on it (Gai 4.70) → Rufus never saw it → no.
  • de peculio: up to the peculium at judgment (≤ 5,000, minus Bellona's own claims). de in rem verso: as far as the horse reached Bellona's property (≤ 6,000).
  • (b) institoria only for deals within the shop's business — a racehorse for an art shop? → probably no; Papinian's quasi-institoria needs Rufus to know of the mandate → no. Rufus sues Nastes himself: actio venditi 6,000.
A slave acquires for his master: the horse handed to Nastes became Bellona's (through the peculium). A free manager acquires for himself (classical law knows only indirect agency); Bellona would need a second traditio. → Property Ch. III

15Two sureties and a silent part payment → Ch. XIII → Ch. XIV

Facts: Felix (at Bellona's request) and her uncle Nastes each stand surety for 15 with Omphale, who lends Bellona 15. At maturity Bellona pays 9 and does not tell Felix. Omphale's procurator gets 15 from Felix; another procurator gets 6 from Nastes.

Q: (a) effect of the three payments on Bellona, Felix, Nastes; (b) Felix's claims; (c) Nastes' actions. issues to check

Bellona pays 9Felix pays 15Nastes pays 6 still owed60 · 9 not owed6 not owed
  • (a) accessoriness: after 9, both sureties owe only 6; Felix's payment ends the debt for all.
  • (b) Felix: condictio indebiti v Omphale (9); actio mandati contraria v Bellona — her silence breached the duty to inform (as Ago in XVII Case 4) → 15, then assign the 9 to her.
  • (c) Nastes: condictio indebiti v Omphale (6). No recourse v Bellona: paying a non-existing debt is not useful gestio (D 12.6.25); and an uncle — animus recipiendi or pietas?
  • Co-sureties: no recourse between them without assignment (D 46.1.39); beneficium divisionis never invoked.

16Grapes left to the pests → Ch. IV → Ch. V → Ch. XIII

Facts: 2 Jan: Ceres sells her vineyard's harvest to trader Merops at 300 per hundredweight (he can resell at 400); Felix stands surety. July: pests attack; Ceres could fight them but deliberately does not, having fallen out with Merops. October: no crop; Ceres and Felix deny any claim.

Q: What is the legal situation? issues to check

  • Price per unit → emptio rei speratae (cf "1,000 per ton"): conditional on the harvest; the seller bears the yield risk.
  • A party who prevents the condition is treated as if it were fulfilled (Pomponius D 18.1.8 pr, Case 64, fn 25) → sale effective; Ceres' dolus = non-performance.
  • Merops: actio empti, expectation interest = 100 per hundredweight × a normal harvest (resale profit = lucrum cessans); the quantity must be proved open
  • Felix: owes what Ceres owes; whether that includes damages for her dolus — the notes are silent open

17Livia's deposit turned into a loan → Ch. III → Ch. XI

Facts: Titus' slave Livia has a peculium. 1 June: Rudens deposits a bag holding 100 with her. 2 June: to pay 80 her absent master owes from a sale, she asks Rudens to have the 100 as a one-month loan; he agrees. On her return armed Lusitanians have stolen the bag. Her remaining peculium: 50.

Q: (a) the contract at each stage; (b) Rudens' claims v Livia; (c) v Titus. issues to check

  • (a) 1 June depositum (sealed bag). 2 June: agreed change to mutuum at the depositary's request → the loan arises at once (Ulpian D 12.1.9.9, Case 16). Contrast: leave to use "if you wish" given at the deposit → loan only when the money is taken (D 12.1.10, Case 17).
  • From then the borrower bears the risk; the robbery does not free Livia.
  • (b) condictio — but a slave's debt is only a natural obligation.
  • (c) de peculio up to 50 (minus Titus' own claims). de in rem verso: money meant for Titus' debt but lost before payment — Julian (D 15.3.17 pr): versio already if genuinely meant for the master disputed
Livia already holds the coins; the new agreement alone turns her detention into possession and ownership (traditio brevi manu) — acquired for her master through the peculium. → Property Ch. III

18Rupilius & Tiro, ceramics → Ch. IX → Ch. VI → Ch. IV

Facts: Rupilius gives 40,000 capital, Tiro makes and sells the vessels. (b) Tiro sells Claudia an amphora (150) with a hairline crack he did not know of; her wine (40) leaks, cleaning costs 20. (c) On the way to market robbers take Tiro's inherited seal ring (200) and the vessels (400). (d) Tiro buys two slaves for a lump 2,000; one had been killed the night before the sale.

Q: (a) contract type, sharing; (b) Claudia's claims, against whom; (c) Tiro v Rupilius; (d) Tiro v Ramses. issues to check

  • (a) societas (consensual; capital or labour as contribution); nothing agreed → equal shares in profit and loss (D 17.2.29 pr); never societas leonina. actio pro socio.
  • (b) a partnership is no legal person; only Tiro contracted → actio empti v Tiro: price reduction; the 40 + 20 from an unaware seller — Labeo's vessel guarantee disputed
  • (c) D 17.2.52.4: robbed on a business trip → actio pro socio for half of the business loss: vessels → 200. The private ring lies outside the shared risk (cf Ex 65) open
  • (d) lump sum, one dead before conclusion: whole sale void (Marcianus D 18.1.44) — or two separate apprentices, divisible → reduced price (id quod actum est) disputed

19A dowry gambled away → Ch. XIV → Ch. XIII

Facts: Aulus gives 25,000 as dos to Polybios, fiancé of his daughter Agatha. Polybios' uncle Dexter secures by fideiussio Polybios' possible duty to return it if there is no wedding. Agatha calls it off; Polybios, devastated, loses the 25,000 at cards.

Q: Aulus' claims against Polybios and against Dexter. issues to check

  • Dowry given for a marriage that fails → condictio ob rem / causa data causa non secuta (Example 90).
  • Money gone: a recipient who disposed of it in good faith is released; one who knew he had to give it back owes the value → he gambled after the break-up → 25,000. Gambling as such is not treated open
  • Dexter: accessory → 25,000; may raise defences going to the debt, not personal ones (the beneficium competentiae of a husband on dowry return is personal); a surety for a future duty is not discussed open
Why no rei vindicatio of the coins? The dowry purpose was a valid cause, so ownership passed to Polybios on delivery. A cause that fails later does not undo the transfer; it only obliges him to give back — and the coins are gone anyway. → Property Ch. VII

20The amphora that held only ¾ → Ch. VII → Ch. XIII → Ch. XIV

Facts: Oil trader Severin orders from potter Pertinax a custom amphora holding one metrete for 800 (delivery 15 May, payment 30 May); Blandus, present, stands surety. It holds only ¾. (a) 30 May Pertinax accepts Severin's reduction to 600; Severin is short of money; Pertinax' procurator gets 800 from Blandus, who knows nothing. (b) Severin will reimburse Blandus only 600.

Q: (a) claims between Blandus and Pertinax; (b) Blandus v Severin. issues to check

  • Classify: locatio conductio operis — or a sale if the potter used his own clay (Pomponius D 18.1.20, Case 63). Short capacity = defect (Case 114) → reduction; here agreed: 600.
  • (a) accessoriness: Blandus owed 600 → 200 indebitum → condictio indebiti v Pertinax (procurator = direct agent; mistake ✓).
  • (b) surety in the debtor's presence = mandatum → actio mandati contraria. Severin knew of the reduction and did not tell → as in XVII Case 4: 800, then Blandus assigns the 200 condictio to Severin.

21Milo's unwanted guarantee → Ch. XIII → Ch. XII → Ch. VIII

Facts: Seius borrows 1,000 from Cinna, who presses for early repayment. 1 March: Seius asks Milo to stand surety; Milo agrees. Seius then gets an extension to 10 June and tells Milo he no longer wants him. Next day Milo becomes surety anyway. 10 June: Milo pays Cinna 1,000; Seius: "your problem".

22Pegasus' tendon → Ch. XVI → Ch. XV

Facts: Xerxes orders his slave Stichus to harm his arch-enemy Belias by injuring Belias' racehorse Pegasus. Stichus cuts a tendon with a knife; Pegasus is finished as a racehorse that could have won large sums. Soon after, Xerxes manumits Stichus.

Q: Belias' delictual claims (a) against Stichus, (b) against Xerxes. issues to check

  • Horse injured, not killed → ch. 3 rumpere, direct (knife) ✓ · unlawful ✓ · dolus ✓.
  • (a) a master's order excuses the dependant; the freed slave can be sued himself (D 9.4.6) but may plead his former master's order → probably nothing.
  • (b) the master who ordered is liable as perpetrator: actio directa, no noxae deditio. Amount: highest value within 30 days + lucrum cessans only if realistic and reasonably certain — race prizes are a chance? open
  • Plus actio iniuriarum: injuring a horse to insult its owner (Example 107).

23A board off the cart, a broken cane → Ch. XVI

Facts: On a steep road on the Capitol an untied board slides off Pia's donkey cart and badly injures the foot of Quartus, a slave of Verres. In pain, Quartus goes for her with his ivory cane (10,000); she defends herself skilfully and breaks it.

Q: Verres' delictual claims against Pia (a) for the injury, (b) for the cane. issues to check

  • (a) ch. 3 rumpere. Direct? Throwing down a load is direct (D 9.2.7.2); a board sliding off by itself → maybe only in factum open. culpa: unsecured load where people pass (cf pruner, D 9.2.31). Amount: treatment + loss within 30 days.
  • (b) frangere ✓ — but self-defence? Current, unlawful attack (fault not needed); she did no more than needed to stop it → justified, no claim. Excess only if she struck after the attack ended (lantern case, D 9.2.52.1).

24A roof tile and a sleeping draught → Ch. XVI

Facts: Agathon repairs his roof with no warning sign in the street; through his clumsiness a tile breaks the arm of Belias, Brutus' slave. Q: Brutus' delictual claims (a) against Agathon, (b) against Philo. issues to check

  • (a) broken bone = frangere, ch. 3; culpa: no warning above a public street (pruner, D 9.2.31). For the death: Celsus/Marcellus — only wounding → 3,500; Julian — he "killed" too → value disputed

  • No double recovery for the same loss; a contract claim against Philo could not be cumulated with the Aquilian one.

25Pushed into a ravine → Ch. XVI → Ch. XI

Facts: Helena wants Belias, a slave of Brontes, dead. To avoid suspicion she has her own slave Castor — to whom she gives a peculium of 9,000 — push him into a ravine with his bare hands. 26The watchdog and the fleeing boy → Ch. XVI

Facts: Amatius, Julia's 12-year-old slave, breaks into Bellona's stables to take a horse for a ride. Bellona's slave Pulcher catches him; Amatius drops the horse and runs; Pulcher sets the leashed watchdog on him and the boy is badly injured. Angry, Julia does not have him treated; he dies.

Q: Julia's possible claims for damages against Bellona. issues to check

  • Harm through a dog set on him: Proculus — Aquilian action; Julian — only if the dog was held, else in factum disputed
  • Justified? The attack on property was over (horse dropped, boy fleeing) → no current attack, no self-defence; killing a thief (Twelve Tables) is doubtful in classical law → unlawful.
  • Julia's neglect: the wounder answers only for the wound, not the death (Paulus D 9.2.30.4) → ch. 3 loss, not the boy's value.
  • Bellona: noxal liability for Pulcher (pay or surrender), unless she knew or ordered. Amatius' age matters only for his own liability.

27Diana's winter coats → Ch. XI → Ch. XIII

Facts: Diana, emancipated (now sui iuris) daughter of Rufus, runs his clothes shop. She buys winter coats from Nastes for 25,000, payable in three months; at her request Leo stands surety. The winter is mild; she cannot pay on time.

Q: (a) Nastes' claims; (b) Leo pays 25,000 — against whom, with which actions? issues to check

  • (a) v Diana: actio venditi (fully liable). v Rufus: actio institoria — a manager may be sui iuris; buying coats is within a clothes shop's business → full 25,000. v Leo: actio ex stipulatu; a mild winter is no defence.
  • No SC Macedonianum: Diana is not in power, and this is a sale, not a money loan.
  • (b) v Diana: actio mandati contraria. v Rufus: assignment of Nastes' claim (beneficium cedendarum actionum); cf D 17.1.10.5 (Case 262): surety's recourse against the owner by analogous institoria — there the owner had mandated the borrowing open

28Aulus' pepper loan → Ch. XI → Ch. XIII → Ch. XII

Facts: Aulus, Solon's slave with a peculium, deals in spices and asks Daphne for an interest-free loan of 10,000. Daphne asks Solon, who agrees. She pays only after Cato stands surety — without Aulus' or Solon's knowledge. Aulus cannot repay; the peculium is now worth 500.

Q: (a) Who must repay Daphne, with which actions? (b) Cato pays 10,000 — recourse against Solon? issues to check

  • Aulus: natural obligation only — but it can be secured by a surety.
  • (a) Solon agreed to Daphne herself → a iussum known to the creditor → actio quod iussu for the full 10,000. de peculio only up to 500. Cato: actio ex stipulatu 10,000.
  • (b) no mandate (no knowledge) → actio negotiorum gestorum contraria: paying another's debt can be useful gestio (Labeo D 3.5.42) — but whose affair: Aulus' natural debt or Solon's quod iussu liability? (cf Ex 87) open
  • Or: assignment of Daphne's quod iussu claim against Solon (beneficium cedendarum actionum).
