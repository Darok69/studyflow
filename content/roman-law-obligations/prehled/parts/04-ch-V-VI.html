2. Conditions — the tree

future event on which the effects depend
certain → time clause dies
uncertain → condicio

a. Suspensive (precedent)

Effects only once the condition is met; until then uncertainty.
Ex 32: "If my ship returns safely from Greece, it is sold to you for 100.000." Even if already delivered, only fulfilment creates the causa for ownership and for keeping the thing.

b. Resolutive (subsequent)

Valid from consensus, dissolved once the condition occurs.
Ex 33: Peitho sells his cart for 10.000; "if I win at gambling tomorrow, it is yours as a gift".
fulfilment works…what happensseller's remedy
ex tunc (back in time)sale annulled retroactively → no causa ever existed → buyer never became ownerrei vindicatio (in rem)
ex nunc (from now)the causa for keeping lapses, but the completed transfer of ownership standscondictio or the contract action (in personam)

fn: suspensive conditions can likewise work ex tunc or ex nunc.

Ownership passes by traditio (delivery) only if there is a valid legal ground (iusta causa) — here the sale. If the sale disappears ex tunc, the buyer only ever possessed; the seller is still owner and sues any possessor with the rei vindicatio. → Property Ch. VII → Property Ch. X

c. Conditions as a built-in way out

Conditions often reserve a right to cancel the sale: after a trial period (pactum displicentiae), if the buyer pays late (lex commissoria), if a better offer comes (in diem addictio) → next page.

3. Mancipation sale: lex mancipio dicta

UTI LINGUA NUNCUPASSIT, ITA IUS ESTOXII Tables VI.1 — what is declared orally at the mancipatio shall be binding
  • Originally: oral side agreements spoken at the mancipatio = a norm the parties create themselves.
  • Typical for a mancipated slave: duty to manumit; ban on manumission; duty to export; for a female slave: ban on prostitution.
  • Breach → ownership reverts to the seller; he may vindicate even from a third-party purchaser → effect in rem, not only in personam.

Ex 34 · cf Paulus D 18.1.56 (Case 142)

Murena
mancipates slave woman: "not to be prostituted"
➜
Thermus
prostitutes her (breach) and sells her
➜
Capito
third party, now in possession

Decision: Murena vindicates from Capito. Why: ownership reverted to him at the moment of breach — the lex mancipio dicta acts in rem.

Mancipatio = the formal civil-law ceremony (scales, coin, five witnesses) for conveying res mancipi — slaves, Italian land, draught and pack animals. It gives quirite ownership. The oral words spoken at it become binding law. → Property Ch. VII

4. Pacta adiecta in the consensual sale

  • Side agreements are possible also with the consensual emptio venditio = pacta adiecta.
  • Like the essentialia they become binding parts of the contract, enforced with the sale actions (actio empti / venditi).

B. Non-performance

1. The object perishes — when?

object perishes
before conclusion = initial impossibility
→ no contract; possibly reliance damages → Ch. IV
after conclusion, before performance = subsequent impossibility → valid contract, performance impossible
a. seller at fault → liable: damages for non-performance (this page)
b. outside his responsibility → question of risk → C.

2. Liability for non-performance

  • From conclusion both must perform; before delivery the seller may not wilfully/negligently prevent or impair delivery.
  • Culpable loss → the impossible primary performance (intact goods) is replaced by its money equivalent = secondary performance (damages).
  • Measure: put the buyer where he would be after proper performance — his interest in having the object (Ulpian D 19.1.1 pr, Case 87).
Until delivery the seller is still owner — the sale alone transfers nothing; ownership passes only with traditio. His property-law freedom to deal with the thing is limited by his contractual duty to deliver. → Property Ch. VII

4. Measure of liability: dolus malus + culpa

Case 86 · Labeo (Paulus) D 19.1.54 pr. Facts: after the sale, before delivery, S gives the sold slave an order; carrying it out, the slave breaks his leg.
Labeo: not liable if it was an order S used to give before the sale and would give to an unsold slave.
Paulus: was the task dangerous? If a reasonable, careful head of household would not have ordered it → at fault, liable.
LabeoPaulus
subjective: the seller's own practice = diligentia quam in suis rebusobjective: the diligens pater familias

3. Expectation interest (= "positive interest")

A party's interest in proper performance by the other = the loss when the other does not perform properly.

−
actual assets now
=
expectation interest
(difference method)
  • Seller: the agreed price (+ interest on arrears in default).
  • Buyer: the object's market value. Often = price; but the buyer can always reclaim the price even if the value is lower.
Agatha buys a vase worth 150 from Bubo for 100

What counts — four refinements

Cover purchase · Ex 38 (cf Paulus D 19.1.21.3, Case 91): Gaius buys wheat for 100; Cleopatra culpably fails to deliver. He could buy for 130 but doesn't; two slaves (240 each) starve → claim only 30. Duty (Obliegenheit) to minimise damages.
This buyer's wealth · Ex 39: Lykaon needs a flute-player for Cara (penalty 100). Slave worth 80 → interest 180 if paid (80 + 100 avoided) — only if no timely substitute purchase.
Ex 40: last volume of a nine-volume set: single volume 100, full set 1.200 — the whole exceeds the parts.
Lost profit (lucrum cessans) · Ex 41: Ago wilfully withholds Stichus (value 100, price 100). Lysippe had resold for 120 and promised Europa 50 on non-performance → interest 170 (100 paid + 20 profit + 50 penalty).

damnum emergens (loss of the object) + lucrum cessans (resale profit). A mere chance of profit is not compensable.

2. Risk (periculum) and the scale of accidents

Risk = hazard of accidental loss, destruction or deterioration — accidental = neither party at fault.

fault

→ liability

lesser accident (casus minor)
utmost diligence (custodia = guarding) could have prevented it

  • vis maior: natural death, shipwreck, fire, pirates, armed robber gangs, flight of slaves.
  • custodia standard: diligentissimus pater familias with his own things (Gaius D 13.6.18 pr, Case 23) — e.g. the borrower → Ch. III. The lex Aquilia asks only for the diligens.
  • Harm despite utmost care counts as vis maior. Ex 43: borrowed silver guarded by slaves; robbers take it at knifepoint.
Casum sentit dominus — "the owner feels the accident": whoever owns the thing bears its accidental loss. The buyer becomes owner when an authorised seller delivers by traditio, so under this rule the risk would pass with delivery. Roman sale departs from it → right. → Property Ch. V → Property Ch. VII

3.1 Periculum est emptoris — the jurists' sales rule

PERFECTA EMPTIONE PERICULUM AD EMPTOREM RESPICIETPaulus D 18.6.8 pr (Case 79/1) — once the sale is perfected, the risk lies with the buyer
  • Unlike modern law, risk is not tied to traditio (passing of ownership) but to perfection → next page.
twofold riskmeaningafter vis maior
non-performance riskgetting no performance — neither primary (goods) nor secondary (damages) — although the contract is validbuyer: no goods, no damages
payment riskhaving to perform without receiving the other side's performance. Matters only for whoever already bears the non-performance riskbuyer: must still pay full price
Ex 44: Probus buys a specific vase (worth 120) from Daphne for 100; an earthquake destroys it after perfection, before delivery → no vase, no damages, still pays 100.

Why so buyer-unfriendly — and its limits

  • Cash sales prevailed: obligation and performance coincided, so no risk problem arose.
  • With a time gap, not having to take delivery at once was seen as the buyer's advantage.
  • Limit 1: only from perfection (goods deliverable) — many vis maior cases are therefore outside it.
  • Limit 2: non-mandatory — the parties could agree otherwise.

Risk II — perfection: the moment the risk jumps

book pp. 127–133
3.2–3.4 perfection

The risk timeline of a sale — who bears a vis maior loss?

● conclusion = emptio contracta (binding already)
★ PERFECTION = emptio perfecta
● traditio (delivery)
Phase 1 · concluded, not perfected
a. stock reduced but delivery still possible → seller bears it: genus non perit, perform as agreed.
b. delivery impossible → buyer gets nothing (non-performance risk) but pays nothing (no payment risk).
Phase 2 · perfected, not delivered
periculum est emptoris: buyer bears both risks → no goods, no damages, pays the full price.
Perfection does not require that delivery be due yet.
Phase 3 · delivered
buyer is owner anyway (casum sentit dominus) — risk passes on traditio at the latest.

Perfection = the sale is ready for execution

It is certain whether performance is due and exactly what is owed: quid, quale, quantum? (Paulus D 18.6.8 pr). Four gates:

1

ascertained

unascertained goods singled out from the stock → duty becomes one for specific goods
2

no suspension

suspensive condition fulfilled / suspensive term elapsed. A resolutive condition does not block
3

quantity / price fixed

unit-price sale: not perfect until counted (Gaius D 18.1.35.5, Case 62)
4

not defective

presumably: perfected once the defect disappears or is cured
"Ascertainment" is also called specification — it has nothing to do with specificatio in property law (making a new thing out of someone else's material, a natural mode of acquiring ownership). Don't mix them up in an exegesis. → Property Ch. IX

The examples on the timeline

Ex 45 · genus non perit: Priamos (300 amphorae) sells Ismene 20 on 15 May, to be picked on 15 Aug. Earthquake on 30 May destroys 170 → he must pick 20 of the remaining 130. Whole cellar destroyed → sale impossible, no oil, no price; no substitutes owed.
Ex 46 · cf Gaius D 18.1.35.7 (Case 80): 1 Jan Lydus buys 100 amphorae at 20 (price 2.000 certain) · 1 Feb jointly picked and chalk-marked = perfected → risk on Lydus · 1 Apr collected. Joint picking lets the buyer keep perfection close to traditio.
Suspensive condition: Diana buys Terentius' umbrella for 30 "if it rains tomorrow" → not perfected while uncertain. Ex 48 · suspensive term: Phaon sells Juno his racehorse for 500.000 "on his birthday" → perfected on the birthday.
Ex 49: Solon buys all wine barrels in Nike's cellar at 100 each → not perfected until the barrels are counted (total price unknown).
Ex 50 · limited generic sale: Aristo buys 100 metretes from Iokaste's cellar; the whole cellar is destroyed before ascertainment. Analogy to Case 84 could put the risk on the buyer — prevailing opinion: no periculum est emptoris (cf Case 80): perfection is a separate prerequisite.

3.4 Alternative sale · Case 84 · Paulus D 18.1.34.6

obligatio alternativa: one of two individually specified things is owed. Choice: seller in case of doubt (or buyer, if agreed). Unperfected while it is open which thing; normally perfected by the choice — or when all items but one become impossible by vis maior.

variantfactsPaulus
1 · one after the otherone dies before perfection, soon after the second1st death → only the survivor is owed → ascertained = perfected. 2nd death (vis maior) → buyer bears both risks: pays, gets nothing

Delay — mora debitoris and mora creditoris

book pp. 136–139
D. Delay / default

1. Debtor's delay (mora debitoris)

  • Debtor does not offer the owed, still possible performance when due, in the agreed form. (Permanent obstacle = non-performance / risk, not delay. Roman sources, though, call any deviation mora — cf Case 90.)
  • Consequences only if the debtor is at fault: si per eum stat (cf Pomponius D 12.1.5).
  • Buyer suing must offer the price: the seller may hold back the thing quasi pignus (Ulpian D 19.1.13.8).
A pledge (pignus) is a security right in rem: the creditor holds the debtor's thing until paid. The seller is treated "as if" he had such a security — he may keep the goods until the price is offered. → Property Ch. XII
who is lateconsequences
seller (delivery)① liable also for later accidental loss → expectation interest (Case 87)
② compensate the delay damage: a slave delivered late → what the seller earned from his labour since the due date + what the buyer could not earn (Neratius D 19.1.31.1, Case 88)
③ delay so serious that staying is unreasonable → bona fides probably allowed withdrawal → Ch. II
buyer (price)default interest only (customary local borrowing rate) — no further damages (Hermogenian D 18.6.20(19), Case 89)
Ex 52: Vibia sells Cato a specific vase, due 1 March; she forgets. 2 March lightning destroys it. Risk had passed at perfection — but from 1 March she is in delay → answers for accident too → liable.
Ex 53: Pausanias does not pay Niobe 100 on 1 Jan. On 1 Feb she misses a bargain (earring worth 150 for 100) for lack of cash → only default interest, no lost profit. Why: she should have borrowed; the defaulter reimburses the loan interest. Modern law adopted this.

2. Creditor's delay (mora creditoris)

  • Creditor fails to accept performance properly offered: buyer won't take the goods / seller won't take the price.
  • Fault needed? Unclear — on balance unlikely.
  • Needs a proper offer → debtor's and creditor's delay exclude each other.
  • Buyer in default: seller liable only for dolus and culpa lata. Loss by vis maior, lesser accident or slight negligence → buyer pays, gets nothing.
  • Deposit (esp. money) at public places, e.g. temples; probably from Diocletian on it discharges like performance.
  • Storage costs → seller's actio venditi.
Case 85/1 · Paulus D 18.6.13(12). Facts: S puts beds that B bought but did not collect in time on a public road; the aedile has them smashed (aediles were also traffic police). Decision: prevailing view — buyer bears the risk. Why: B was in default of acceptance.
Case 93 · Ulpian D 18.6.1.3. Facts: wine sold from S's store, time limit for measuring out; B does not turn up. Decision: after notice S could even pour the wine away; better keep it and claim storage costs; also the lost rent for barrels he could have let, or the cost of hiring barrels from a third party. Why: the costs are caused by B's late acceptance.
Case 92 · Pomponius D 19.1.9. Facts: A bought stones, agreed to remove them from B's land; he doesn't. Decision: B sues on the sale for removal. Why: the contract made taking delivery a duty → A is in creditor's delay (entitled) and debtor's delay (obliged to remove).

Exam: a Digest fragment on risk, delay or a side clause — ask

  • Which sale? specific, generic (limited stock), alternative? Any condition, term, pactum adiectum?
  • When did the loss happen — before conclusion, between conclusion and perfection, between perfection and traditio, after?
  • Was the sale perfected? ascertained · price/quantity fixed · no suspensive condition/term · not defective.
  • Why was it lost? fault (dolus/culpa — Labeo's subjective vs Paulus' objective test) · lesser accident (custodia?) · vis maior?
  • Was anyone in delay? debtor (liable for accident) or creditor (only dolus/culpa lata)?
  • Result in two lines: does the buyer get goods / damages? Must he pay the price? Then compute the expectation interest (difference method, cover purchase, lucrum cessans).
  • Side clause: does it give an option or work automatically? ex tunc (→ rei vindicatio) or ex nunc (→ condictio)?

The map of Ch. VI — two kinds of defect, five remedies

defect shows up after delivery → warranty claims (liability regardless of fault)
B. Defect in title — a third party takes the thing away (eviction)
Ex 54: Diokles sells and conveys land to Melitta; Niketas, the civil-law owner, wins a rei vindicatio against her.
→ actio auctoritatis · action on the stipulatio duplae · actio empti
C. Defect as to quality — the thing is worse than owed
Ex 55: Gaia sells and delivers a cow to Pius; it already had a disease at delivery and must be put down.
→ actio redhibitoria · actio quanti minoris · actio empti · guarantees

1. Obligations before and after delivery

  • Primary duties: price ⇄ goods. Secondary: pre-contractual duties to inform → Ch. IV, duties of care between conclusion and delivery → Ch. V.
  • Obligations do not end with delivery and payment: flaws may surface later.

2a. Warranty = liability regardless of fault

  • The seller answers for defects of title and quality whether or not he caused them or culpably failed to disclose them.
  • dolus still matters: it makes liability stricter.

2c. Can warranty be excluded?

  • Yes: the rules are dispositive — restrict or exclude by agreement.
  • Limit: dolus malus. Excluding it is contrary to bona fides (Pomponius D 19.1.6.9, Case 101) → even after a total waiver the buyer can sue a seller who knew and kept quiet.

The evictor sues with the rei vindicatio (civil owner) or the actio publiciana (the praetorian action of someone treated as if he had completed usucapion); a pledgee with the vindicatio pignoris. → Property Ch. X → Property Ch. XII

3a. What does the seller owe? Peaceable possession

Rome: uti frui habere licere — use, fruits, possession (short: licere habere, Julian D 21.2.8, Case 100). No duty to make the buyer owner.
Modern Austria: principle of procuring ownership → warranty claims whether or not the buyer is evicted.

3b. Principle of eviction — no eviction, no claim

  • The bare fact that the buyer did not get ownership gives him nothing — only a disturbance of his possession does.
  • Eviction occurs (Pomponius D 21.2.16.1, Case 103) when the buyer:
1

surrenders

the thing under the clausula arbitraria
2

is condemned

to pay the assessed value
3

loses as plaintiff

lost possession to the owner, sues in rem — and loses

Resale chain (Marcellus D 21.2.61, Case 105): the evicted 2nd buyer first claims from his seller; once done, eviction takes effect between 1st buyer and 1st seller.

In the formula of the rei vindicatio the judge cannot order return: the defendant is pressed to give the thing back (clausula arbitraria); otherwise he is condemned to pay its value in money. Either way the buyer has lost — both count as eviction. → Property Ch. X

Why the eviction principle makes sense

As long as the buyer possesses in peace, performance is not (yet) defective — and he may never suffer: as a usucapient he gains original ownership once usucapion is complete → usucapion cures the defect in title.

Usucapio = acquiring ownership by possessing in good faith on a valid title for 1 year (movables) / 2 years (land). Stolen things (res furtivae) are excluded by the lex Atinia. → Property Ch. VIII

Three routes, one rule of thumb

  • Fixed sum (2 ×) → auctoritatis / stipulation: no need to prove a loss.
  • Actual interest → actio empti: the value of the thing plus every advantage lost through eviction.

1b–c. Time and disclosure

  • Present at delivery, not arisen after perfection; the cause suffices (infected slave/animal). After perfection: fault → non-performance, accident → risk. After delivery → nothing.
  • Bona fides: the seller must tell the buyer of defects. dolus covers speaking unclearly to mislead and cunning concealment (Florentinus D 18.1.43.2).
Ex 58 · cf Gaius D 18.1.35.8 (Case 113): Leda sells her estate to Bruno, knowing his mortal enemy Castor owns the neighbouring estate, and says nothing → warranty claims.

1d. No warranty

Apparent defects → caveat emptor. "Good-looking slave" = check it yourself; "has learnt a trade" = seller answers (Florentinus D 18.1.43 pr, Case 118).
Mere praise of one's wares, where it is clear the seller didn't mean to be bound (Ulpian D 21.1.19 pr, Case 132) — blatant sales talk.

4a. The edict's rules spread through bona fides

  • Goods outside the edict (not slaves, not draught/pack/herd animals) → actio empti.
  • The aediles' rules became the standard of fair dealing → by (material) analogy the actio empti gives price reduction (Cases 116, 115) and redhibition (Case 121).

Case 121 · Ulpian D 19.1.11.5. Facts: A sells B a slave woman, letting B believe she is a virgin though he knows she is not. Decision: no ground for redhibitio, but actio empti to rescind → restitution of performances (keeping her: presumably price reduction). Why: lack of virginity is no morbus/vitium of the edict (abstractly also: not bought on the market).

Exam: a Digest fragment on warranty — ask

  • Which defect? title (a third party's right in rem) or quality (the thing itself)?
  • Title: did the seller own the thing? (validity of sale ≠ transfer of ownership) — has the buyer been evicted (surrender / condemnation / lost as plaintiff)? If not: did the seller know, stipulate ownership, or did the buyer succeed the owner ex alia causa?
  • Which form of sale? mancipatio → actio auctoritatis; stipulatio duplae? was one owed? → compare duplum and the actio empti interest.
  • Quality: inherent? ordinary or agreed use? apparent? praise? id quod actum est? present at delivery?

Three sentences to remember

  • Title: no eviction, no claim — the seller owes peaceable possession, not ownership.
  • Remedies after eviction: fixed double (auctoritatis, duplae) or actual interest (actio empti).
  • Quality: without fault → reduce or rescind; with knowledge/assurance → full interest.
