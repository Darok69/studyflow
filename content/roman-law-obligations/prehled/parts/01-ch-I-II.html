A. The nature of the law of obligations

The vinculum iuris

may claim → his side = a right / claim
⟷
OBLIGATIO
two-sided "legal bond" that creates a duty to perform
⟷
debitor (obligor)
must perform → his side = a duty
  • Typically two parties: A may claim something from B, B owes it to A. Either side may be several persons (majority of creditors / of debtors).
  • Roman obligatio covers both rights and duties; English "obligation" usually means only the debtor's side.
  • It binds only the particular creditor and debtor: A's claim lies only against B → obligations are "relative" / "personal" (in personam).
OBLIGATIO EST IURIS VINCULUM, QUO NECESSITATE ADSTRINGIMUR ALICUIUS SOLVENDAE REI … — an obligation is a legal bond that binds us of necessity to perform something.Iust Inst 3.13 pr — the classic definition

Relative vs absolute rights

Example 1Helena's claim to the horse — only against LeoAjax's ownership of the horse

Only when a third party interferes with a right in rem does an obligation arise between holder and intruder → claims for restitution, damages or unjustified enrichment.

A right in rem (ownership = dominium, servitude, pledge …) is a right over a corporeal thing that everybody must respect, enforced by its own actio in rem; Roman law allows only a closed list of them. Here it is the mirror image of the obligation: one binds one person, the other binds all. → Property Ch. I

Example 1 · Leo, Helena and the stallion Hector

Facts: 3 Jan: horse trader Leo sells the stallion Hector to Helena for 900; delivery against payment due 17 Jan. Leo knows the horse belongs to Ajax; Helena does not.

Held: the sale is valid — the seller's lack of ownership does not affect it. Ajax wins against either possessor. The sale can then no longer be performed as agreed → defective performance: Leo must compensate Helena (actio empti). → Ch. V–VI

The rei vindicatio is the owner's action to get his thing back from any possessor; he must prove his ownership. Leo could sell but not make Helena owner: a sale only creates an obligation, ownership passes by a separate conveyance (traditio), and nobody transfers more than he has (nemo plus iuris). → Property Ch. Insolvency: where in rem beats in personam

  • Debtor insolvent → bankruptcy proceedings; his assets are liquidated for the creditors.
  • The shortfall is shared equally: all unsecured claims are cut by the same quota (e.g. one third) → a purely contractual claim loses value.
  • A claim for restitution based on a right in rem survives the third party's insolvency.
Example 2 · Agatha's statue of Apollo — Agatha gives her painted marble statue to Flavius for safekeeping; after a quarrel he leaves it in the rain, the paint washes off (damage 1.400). Flavius goes bankrupt; quota 20 %.
statue (owner, in rem)
returned in full
damages 1.400 (in personam)
280
the rest (1.120) is lost — she ranks pro rata with the other creditors.
Flavius as depositary only holds the statue for Agatha (detention, naturalis possessio); Agatha remains owner. So the statue never falls into Flavius' estate and she can vindicate it in full. → Property Ch. II, V

Liability vs obligation

historically first: LIABILITY
having to answer (financially) = the creditor's right to seize. Originally the person: debt bondage, sale trans Tiberim
➜
today: OBLIGATION
having to perform — give, do, refrain. Liability matters only when the debtor fails or performs defectively
personal liabilityliability in rem
with all one's assets — the rulelimited to specific assets; prime example: the pledge — debt unpaid → the creditor seizes the pledged asset
  • Rule: the debtor is liable for performance — execution lets the creditor seize his assets.
  • Natural obligations: not enforceable in court, but what was paid may be kept. → Ch. II.E
  • For delicts modern usage speaks of "liability" (e.g. for defective goods).
Pledge (pignus) = a limited right in rem: if the debt is unpaid, the creditor sells the pledged thing and pays himself from the proceeds. In bankruptcy the pledged thing is separated from the estate — no sharing with the unsecured creditors. That is why it is "liability in rem". → Property Ch. XII

C. The content of an obligation

Performance = any human conduct: positive acts and omissions. Proper performance as agreed discharges the obligation, which then ends. Roman jurists used three terms:

dare
to give
Delivery of a thing: the seller "gives" the goods, the buyer the money. Narrower sense: transferring ownership.
facere
to do
An action: labour or a specific result. Typical of the contract for work, e.g. transport for money.
praestare
to owe / be liable / guarantee
General term: standing surety; accessory duties such as warranty.

A dare can also create an obligation: in real contracts the delivery (datio) creates the recipient's duty to return → Ch. III; the condictio indebiti arises from a dare → Ch. XIV

Dare in the narrow sense is property law: the contract only obliges to transfer (title); the transfer itself is a separate act (mancipatio, in iure cessio, traditio). A sale alone gives neither possession nor ownership. → Property Ch. III, VII

"Law of obligations" today

  • Umbrella term for obligations from the production, distribution and allocation of goods and services.
  • Excluded: relative rights mainly about personal or family matters (family law, law of persons); inheritance-law obligations form their own category.
  • Marriage is a contract in modern law, but too different from economic contracts to be "contract law".

Four sources of obligations

book pp. 6–9
D. Types of obligations · Revision

The Roman classification by legal basis

OBLIGATIO — where does it come from?
1. Contracts → Ch. II
Bilateral legal transactions recognised by Roman law. Content and scope follow the agreement (lex contractus); the obligation arises from the parties' understanding = a transaction that imposes a legally enforceable duty.
2. Delicts → Ch. XV–XVI
Acts forbidden by law; violation → compensation and/or penalty (poena). Arises when someone injures another by an unlawful (usually culpable) act. Key three: iniuria, furtum, damnum iniuria datum.
3. Quasi-contracts → Ch. XII, XIV
Lawful acts outside any contract. Most important: negotiorum gestio and the condictiones. Iust Inst 3.27 adds: ending co-ownership (communio), guardianship (tutela), legatum per damnationem.
4. Quasi-delicts
Later-classical / post-classical term for certain praetorian delicts, incl. liability irrespective of fault; also the judge who perverts justice (iudex qui litem suam fecit).
Example 3 · delict
Pius smashes Xerxes' glass goblet in anger → Xerxes claims for damnum iniuria datum (the goblet's value).
Example 4 · quasi-contract
After an earthquake Laura has her absent neighbour Antonius' damaged villa repaired (cost 3.000) → negotiorum gestio balances principal and gestor.
Example 5 · quasi-contract
Daphne pays moneylender Agrippa 500 believing she owes it; she does not → recovers it with the condictio indebiti.

Delict: private or public?

Claims to compensation or penalty for the victim = private law; the state's power to punish crimes = public law.

Quasi-delicts → modern strict liability

occupiers of apartments / shops: things thrown, poured, placed or hung that fall and injure passers-by (actio de deiectis vel effusis, de posito vel suspenso; cf § 1318 ABGB)
+
skippers, innkeepers, stable-keepers (nauta, caupo, stabularius): liable without fault for damage or theft of guests' property by their staff
➜
today: strict liability for operational risks of lawfully used dangerous equipment, e.g. motor-vehicle keepers — now important beside fault-based liability

Action against skippers etc.: actio in factum adversus nautas caupones stabularios; for property damage an action analogous to the actio legis Aquiliae, for theft one analogous to the actio furti.

Furtum = taking another's movable with intent to enrich oneself. The thief becomes possessor but never owner — so the owner can still reclaim the thing by rei vindicatio from whoever holds it. → Property Ch. II
Communio = co-ownership: each co-owner has a quota of the thing; it is ended by the actio communi dividundo — the obligations from that are "quasi-contractual". → Property Ch. V

Exegesis/exam: Ch. I — ask

  • Who is creditor, who debtor? Several on one side?
  • Is the claim in personam (against one person) or in rem (against every possessor)? Which actio?
  • Where does the obligation come from: contract, delict, quasi-contract, quasi-delict?
  • What is owed: dare, facere, praestare? Has it been discharged by performance?
  • Liability: personal or in rem (pledge)? Insolvency — does the claimant hold a right in rem? Fault needed, or strict?
  • Enforceable at all, or only a natural obligation?

Contract = bilateral legal act by which one or both parties bind themselves to perform.

Bilateral act, one-sided duty — mutuum: both must agree to the loan, but paying out the money gives only the lender a claim (repayment); the borrower gains no rights from it. → Ch. III

Lex contractus — the contract as a norm

  • The contract is both the source of the parties' rights and duties and the yardstick for measuring them.
  • Contracts are legal norms, like statutes binding the parties → terms = lex contractus.
  • Valid contract → establish scope and content: id quod actum est (what the parties agreed on).
  • Ambiguities, conflicts → interpret the wording; if that fails, supplementary interpretation.

Freedom of contract = the core of private autonomy

Private autonomy: individuals shape their relationships within private law on their own initiative. Its chief expression:

2

free to choose the partner

3

free to determine the content

The limits — freedom inside a frame

① factual reality — nobody can be obliged to perform the factually impossible

② the law — what is forbidden (unlawful) or offends society's fundamental values (contra bonos mores) cannot (or only very narrowly) be a valid contract. Unalterable norms = ius cogens.

③ the lawgiver's choice — decides which transactions bind legally and which only morally or socially

Donation as the test case

  • Donations are permissible — except between spouses (forbidden in Roman law).

B.2 Roman typology · B.3 Modern law

Roman law has no general concept of a binding agreement: contractus = a legal transaction that can be enforced independently in court — and only listed types can.

The Roman typology — does the agreement have a name and an action?

agreement between the parties
Regulated contracts — nomen + own actio
  • sale, lease, interest-free loan, mandate …: each has a name that also designates the court claim → stands for enforceability
  • most defined by subject matter (emptio venditio = goods for money); the stipulatio by its form (fixed ritual promise)
  • four types by how they arise: consensual, real, verbal, literal → next page
Innominate contracts → Ch. X
  • atypical, e.g. barter (goods for goods): fits no category → no nomen, no specific actio
  • the praetor may still grant remedies: actiones in factum
  • formula opens with a description of the atypical deal (verba praescribere) → actio praescriptis verbis
Nudum pactum — a bare agreement
  • not "clothed" with enforceability → not actionable
  • but effects in two other ways (below)
  • Roman pacta are unenforceable and so not binding — the stipulatio offered a flexible, enforceable form of promise for any content

How a pactum still has effect

① as a defence
exceptio pacti. Informal pactum de non petendo (agreement not to sue): the creditor sues anyway → the exception defeats the claim.
② as an add-on
pacta adiecta attached to an actionable contract → enforced by that contract's action. E.g. pactum displicentiae (purchase on approval) → Ch. V

Roman vs modern

Roman lawmodern law
binding ifit fits the typologyevery agreement made seriously, legally permitted, factually possible (cf §§ 879, 883 ABGB)
principlenumerus claususpacta sunt servanda — from canon law
but—asks for a causa: for enforceability, for a transfer of ownership, for keeping possession → not every agreement is automatically binding
An exceptio is a defence written into the formula right after the claim: on its wording the claim is well-founded, but outside facts favour the defendant, who must prove them. So with a pactum de non petendo the debt still exists — the exceptio pacti only blocks the action. → Property Ch. X
"Causa for a transfer of ownership": traditio passes ownership only with a iusta causa (sale, loan, gift, dowry) — it is causal; mancipatio and in iure cessio are abstract. Austria: § 380 ABGB, titulus + modus. → Property Ch. VII

Delay (mora)

Debt due at the fixed time; the debtor does not perform although he could → default.

debtor's delay (default of performance): the cause lies with him — no tender, or not as the contract requires. Performance becomes impossible during his culpable delay → liable even for accidental loss.
creditor's delay (default of acceptance): he refuses a performance offered in accordance with the contract. Only if the debtor is not in default — never both for the same duty.
  • Withdrawal: normally only after a period of grace; the defaulter is liable for delay damage either way. Fixed-date deal (bridal bouquet for the wedding day) → rescind at once + compensation.
  • Delay ends with performance or any other end of the obligation.

The scale of responsibility

FAULT → debtor liable for non-performanceACCIDENT (casus) → a question of risk
dolus (malice)
breach knowing it harms the creditor; dolus malus = intent to harm. Wider: opposite of bona fides. Cannot be excluded.
culpa (negligence)
short of the diligence the contract requires, no intent. culpa lata = gross, culpa levis = slight.
custodia = casus minor
loss that special precautions (guarding) could prevent — mainly theft.
vis maior
events no one can foresee or control: earthquakes, armed bands (robbers, Germans, pirates), natural death of a slave → debtor not liable.
  • Contractual liability requires fault; it is ius dispositivum — parties may widen or narrow it, but never exclude dolus.
  • Default for accidents: casum sentit dominus — the owner bears the risk of accidental destruction; the contract can shift it.

Custodia — accident or fault?

no duty of custodia in the contract → loss from lack of guarding = accident, not the debtor's business
⇄
heightened duty to preserve the thing → failure to guard = culpable defective performance → liable

Custodia liability = for typical guard-preventable harm (esp. theft) without the creditor proving a breach of care → in effect strict; Gaius and others later reinterpret it as breach of a duty to guard. → Ch. III

Casum sentit dominus — but who is dominus? A sale alone does not make the buyer owner; ownership passes only with the conveyance (traditio). → Property Ch. VII
In possession law custodia is the test whether you still possess a movable: can you lay hands on it at any time? Here the same idea becomes a liability standard. → Property Ch. IV

Defective performance — and how a contract ends

book pp. 29–33
F.4 Misperformance, warranty · F.5 Termination

4.1 Misperformance (positive violation of contractual duties)

The debtor does perform, but breaches a contractual obligation or a duty of care and so injures the creditor.

The haulier's column — a haulier carrying a column fulfils his contract for work; the column breaks on the road through inexpert handling → misperformance. (cf Gaius D 19.2.25.7, Case 170) → Ch. VII

4.2 Warranty — liability for defects irrespective of fault

Remedies for defects of quality and of title, whether or not the debtor knew or should have known. → Ch. VI

b. title defect: a third party holds a right to the object.

third party enforces its right in rem in court
⇣
eviction — only now a warranty claim
⇣
at least price back / release from further rent; classical jurists: the interest in performance — even against a debtor who did not know
  • Why eviction first? A debtor of a non-monetary performance owes no unencumbered title, only undisturbed enjoyment: uti frui habere licere. As long as the creditor enjoys it in peace, there is no title problem.
  • Debtor knew of the title defect → stricter: claim without prior eviction.
  • Late classical jurists treat alike: the creditor keeps the thing on another ground (alia causa), e.g. he buys it from the third-party owner.
Eviction = the third party, e.g. the true owner, wins his rei vindicatio against the buyer (Ajax vs Helena, Example 1). Undisturbed enjoyment means for a sale proprietary possession, for a lease natural possession (detention). → Property Ch. II, X

Six ways a contractual relationship ends

① solutio — fulfilment extinguishes all claims; only warranty and claims for culpable defects / breach of care remain.
② confusio — creditor and debtor become one person (debtor inherits from creditor). Distinguish concursus causarum: he gets the same performance from B on another obligation → can no longer claim from A.
③ waiver — ius civile: an obligatio stricti iuris only by stipulatio = acceptilatio (formally an acknowledgement of receipt). Praetor: informal pactum de non petendo → exceptio pacti.
Excursus: extension — moves the due date later; also by informal pactum de non petendo (exceptio pacti). In a bonae fidei iudicium the iudex considers waiver and extension anyway.
⑤ novation — a new obligation replaces the old, which ends as the new one arises. → Ch. VIII
⑥ delegation — order to perform: an existing obligation fulfilled within a three-party relationship. → Ch. XIV

④ compensatio — set-off

1. Unjustified enrichment — two kinds of claim

someone is enriched without a justifying causa
condictions — performance received without cause must be returned; Roman remedy: condictio (term still used today).
Key case: payment of a debt that does not exist → condictio indebiti (Example 5, Daphne) — denied if the payer knew there was no debt.
"Performance" = delivery and receipt with the conscious cooperation of transferor and acquirer. → Ch. XIV
restitution not based on performance — benefit from another's asset without performance and without causa.
Culpable use → the owner claims damages. Non-culpable interference: whoever becomes owner of an attached part by accessio or of processed material by specificatio must compensate the loser.
Remedies: actiones in factum, or an exceptio doli against the owner's rei vindicatio — he wins only after compensating.
Condictio vs rei vindicatio: the vindicatio is in rem — the owner against any possessor, only while the thing exists. The condictio is in personam — against the recipient, for the thing or its value, so it works when ownership has passed (e.g. after a conveyance on an invalid sale). → Property Ch. VII, X
Accessio: inseparably joined things → the owner of the main thing acquires the secondary one. Specificatio: a new thing made from another's material (wine from grapes) → disputed who owns it. Either way the loser of ownership gets only a compensation claim. → Property Ch. IX
