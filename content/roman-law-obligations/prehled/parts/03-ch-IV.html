Name + synallagma — do ut des

Consensual contract: the parties agree to exchange goods for money. Double name = once from the buyer's side (emptor), once from the seller's (venditor).

venditor
owes the goods · is owed the price
sues by actio venditi
⇄
emptor
owes the price · is owed the goods
sues by actio empti

Bilateral: each is debtor and creditor of the other. Synallagmatic: each binds himself in order to get the other's performance.

Consensual: creation ≠ performance · Example 12

Bare consensus suffices, no form. Needed: agreement on the essentialia negotii = goods + price. Neither payment nor delivery is needed to conclude.

1 JanLucius shows Melitta a gold necklace; he hesitates → no sale
15 Jan · marketoffer "100" + acceptance → sale concluded; both obligations arise today
16 Janslave collects + pays → both obligations performed and extinguished
Variant: on 16 Jan her slave is robbed on the way; she has no savings left and no longer wants it. Still bound to pay? Yes — the obligation arose validly on the 15th. This is why consensual creation matters: events between agreement and performance date can make the deal unattractive. (Cash purchase: creation and performance almost coincide.)

A set of obligations

  • Main duties: deliver the goods · pay the price.
  • Subsidiary duties of disclosure, protection, care: e.g. the seller must not culpably damage sold-but-undelivered goods, must warn of dangerous properties, answers for agreed qualities (warranty for defects as to quality).

Bonae fidei negotium

The formulae of actio empti / venditi do not list the duties; the iudex decides what is owed: quod dare facere oportet ex fide bona → leeway in assessing it.

meansstandard of honest contracting parties: "good faith", basic standards of business integrityacquirer's unawareness that the seller was unauthorised (can matter in sale too: seller unaware of a defect)

Interpretation — id quod actum est

  • Narrow: what the parties expressly said or intended · wide: what parties typically agree in this type of contract · plus what they would have agreed had they known (today: supplementary interpretation, hypothetical will).

Error in substantia — wine, slaves, silver, gold

book pp. 81–84
B.4 · Cases 75–78 · three positions

The spectrum — how much does a mistake about the material matter? (controversy)

NARROW: only the outward object countsWIDE: even a partial difference in material voids
Marcellus (high classical): consensus on the outward appearance suffices; substance irrelevant (agreed on the amphora → wine/vinegar does not matter).
Ulpian: substance matters, but narrowly — no sale only if the substances are completely different (soured wine, non-virgin, jewellery with some gold → valid).
Julian: goes furthest — relevant even if the material differs only partly (silver-plated table → no sale).

Agreed by "at least some jurists": no sale where the substance is wholly different (copper as gold, lead as silver) and with error in sexu.

Case 75 · Ulpian D 18.1.9.2
Facts: A sells B wine; it turns out to be vinegar.
Marcellus: valid — they agreed on the object (e.g. this amphora).
Ulpian distinguishes: wine that went sour = practically the same substance → valid; liquid that was always vinegar = different substance → no sale.
Case 76 · Ulpian D 18.1.11.1
V1: female slave, B thinks she is a virgin; she is not → not error in sexu → valid (buyer gets a warranty claim, D 19.1.11.5, Case 121).
V2: B thinks the slave is a boy → error in sexu → no sale.
Case 77 · Julian D 18.1.41.1
Facts: both parties think a table is solid silver; it is only silver plated.
Julian: no sale.
Why: for Julian a partial difference in material is enough.
Case 78 · Ulpian D 18.1.14
Facts: co-heirs sell one heir jewellery all believe solid gold; it is mostly bronze.
Ulpian: valid.
Why: the item does contain some gold → not completely different.

Why do they disagree?

Possibly different philosophies of a thing's "essence": Aristotelian jurists → the outward FORM (consensus on form suffices); Stoic jurists → the SUBSTANCE (must match what was agreed). The book calls it a parallel to the debate on specification.

specificatio asks who owns a new thing made from another's material: Sabinians → owner of the material, Proculians → the maker; compromise media sententia. Same form-vs-matter split. → Property Ch. IX

Error in substantia ≠ quality defect?

Quality defect = delivered object has a material defect impairing its proper or agreed use → contract valid, buyer has a warranty claim.

gold-plated table sold as solid gold
material mistake excludes consensus → no contract
contract affirmed, table delivered → plating = deviation from agreed quality → buyer need not accept; may in some cases withdraw

How the "valid" jurists protect the buyer

Jurists who affirm a sale despite an error in substantia give the buyer a warranty claim instead.

Non-virgin sold as virgin: Ulpian → sale valid (Case 76) + warranty for defect as to quality (D 19.1.11.5, Case 121).

Gate 1 — money, or partly money

Case 52 · Paulus D 18.1.1.1 — A gives a toga, receives a tunica.
Nerva + Proculus (Paulus agrees): not a sale — one cannot tell who is seller and who buyer. Sabinians (Sabinus, Cassius), minority: sale. (school controversy)
Case 53 · Pomponius D 19.1.6.1 — E sells T a tenement house; T pays a fixed sum + renovates another house of E.
Pomponius: sale; E sues for the renovation with the actio venditi. Renovation alone as consideration → no sale.

Rule: partly money, partly another performance = still a sale.

Sale vs donatio

  • Donatio = gratuitous; contract law for non-gratuitous deals (e.g. warranty) does not apply.
  • An informal donative promise (not a stipulation) is not enforceable; the donatio only justifies keeping the gift. Ex. 18: if Leo refuses to deliver, Niobe has no actio empti.
  • Contrast: once a valid sale exists, the seller may defer or waive the price — a creditor may dispose of his claim.
donatio is a valid iusta causa traditionis (and causa usucapionis): handing over a gift does transfer ownership, even though the promise to give was not enforceable. → Property Ch. VII

Pretium iustum — from free price to "just price"

classical private autonomy: parties set the price freely; it is "naturally permissible" to buy a thing worth more for less (Paulus D 19.2.22.3, Case 60) — liberal contract law.
post-class. fairness gains weight: gross violations of equivalence become unacceptable.

Middle Ages scholars build the doctrine of the "just price".
ABGB §§ 934 ff: all non-gratuitous contracts, for whichever party gets less than half the value of its own performance.

The goods I — species, genus, alternatives

book pp. 92–93
D.1–2 · a clearly defined object is essential
What exactly is owed? (agreement on a clearly defined object = essential element of sale)

Specified goods (species)

An individually specified object is owed.

Ex. 22 Calenus sells Daphne "his vineyard" after a joint inspection.

Unascertained goods (genus)

A class (genus) is the reservoir; a quantity is owed; every item of the class is equally suitable.

Ex. 23 "four hectares of vineyard in the Alban mountains" → Calenus may pick any four.

Limited genus

From a limited class: "100 metretes of wine from my cellars". Dispute: did Rome know only this, or also the unlimited genus?

Ex. 24 "one sheep from my herd" → limited genus.

Alternative obligation

Two (or more) ways of performing, fixed as items or classes; choice typically with the obligor (cf Paulus D 18.1.34.6, Case 84).

Ex. 24 "the sheep Ara or the sheep Rando".

So the same kind of thing can be owed as species (Ex. 22 "his vineyard") or as genus (Ex. 23 "four hectares").

The goods II — selling the future: res sperata vs spes

book pp. 94–96
D.3–5 · Pomponius D 18.1.8 pr (Case 64)

A specified object must exist — yet a future thing (future fruit, unborn slave child or animal) or a mere chance can be sold (Pomponius D 18.1.8 pr, Case 64).

Emptio rei speratae — a hoped-for thing

  • The thing does not exist yet; the parties count on it coming into existence.
  • Its coming into existence = an uncertain future event on which validity depends → condition precedent (on condiciones → Ch. V).

Example 25: on 1 Jan Pharnakes sells Lysippe "the next calf born of my cow".

1 Jan · agreementsale made under a suspensive condition
1 June · calf borncondition met → sale effective ex tunc = as of 1 Jan
cow dies firstcondition fails → the sale never takes effect
Fore-effects (Case 64): before the condition is met, neither party may prevent its fulfilment — otherwise contract actions lie against him, and he is treated as if the condition were fulfilled.

Emptio spei — buying the chance itself

  • The buyer pays a fixed price whether or not the thing comes into existence: the object is an immaterial hope = speculative purchase.
  • Unconditional: validity does not depend on the hope coming true → the buyer bears the risk.
  • Under bona fides the seller is probably obliged to try to make the hope come true.
  • Classic: a fisherman's catch sold in advance for a lump sum (Case 64).

Reliance damages — on what basis?

book pp. 101–103
E.2.3 · quod interest ne deceptum esse

Why the seller owes anything: bona fides → duty to disclose

bona fides requires (potential) partners to tell each other of known impediments to performance
➜
a seller who knows delivery is impossible and stays silent acts with dolus (= the opposite of bona fides)
➜
today: pre-contractual duties of protection, care, disclosure = culpa in contrahendo

Three constructions in the sources

the deceived buyer claims his reliance loss with …
aa · actio in factum
no sale → strictly no contract claim; some jurists may have allowed an analogous action.
aa · actio de dolo
if the seller acted with dolus. Subsidiary (only if no other claim), needs dolus malus, conviction → infamy → Ch. XV
bb · actio empti
(i) homo liber / res furtiva, buyer unaware → sale valid (Cases 65, 66, 68)
(ii) other cases: jurists deny a valid sale yet grant the actio empti (Case 67)
Case 67 · Modestinus D 18.1.62.1 (late classical) — A buys land that is loca sacra, religiosa or publica, believing it private.
Decision: no binding sale, but the buyer has the actio empti for his interest in not having been deceived: quod interest, ne deceptum esse.
Why: the seller's conduct is seen as a breach of bona fides, on which the claim rests. The phrase refers to the buyer's disappointed hope of a valid promise — it does not necessarily mean the seller knew. It is the source of the modern term "reliance damages".

Summary — what the buyer gets

  • Always: price back (condictio indebiti).
  • Plus reliance loss (architect, transaction costs) — via actio empti / in factum / de dolo.
  • Never the lost bargain — expectation damages need a valid sale (e.g. Case 109/2 V1, subjective impossibility).

Neratius' threshold — burnt house

≤ ½ burnt
> ½ burnt

contract performed at a price reduced by the loss in value — both bound

buyer not bound: may withdraw, reclaim a price paid

Aim: a plausible quantitative threshold for total impossibility. The jurists look at both sides: only when most is gone may the buyer choose.

in property law a house is only an accession to the land: superficies solo cedit — what is firmly built on land follows the land, which is always the main thing. In the contract the parties made the house the main object, so the land still being deliverable does not save the sale. → Property Ch. IX

Case 70a · Paulus D 18.1.57 pr — the burnt house (controversy)

Facts: K buys a house from V; unknown to both, it had burned down before conclusion.

① Nerva, Sabinus, Cassius
no sale; price recoverable by condictio.
Why: "I am buying your house" → the house is the object. The land is still deliverable, but economically this is total impossibility.
② Neratius — it depends how much burned:
• major part → buyer not bound, may withdraw and reclaim a price paid
• half or less → contract performed at a price reduced by the loss in value

Read ② as: a quantitative line for "total"; below it the sale is valid with price adjustment — resembling the remedies for quality defects. Contrast Case 70/1 (Paulus D 18.1.15 pr): the book's example of practical impossibility.

Exegesis: sale, partial impossibility — ask

  • Classify: is the impossibility initial (before/at conclusion) and objective? Practical or legal (res extra commercium)? Did the parties know?
  • Total or partial? What exactly is the object of the sale under the agreement (house vs land; one unit vs several items)?
  • Id quod actum est: would the parties have contracted for the remainder? Purpose? Lump sum or separate prices?
  • Which jurist, which criterion? Nerva/Sabinus/Cassius vs Neratius' threshold — a school controversy to name.
  • Consequences: no sale → price back by condictio (indebiti); reliance damages (quod interest ne deceptum esse, Case 67) — on which basis? Adapted sale → reduced price.
  • Distinguish: subsequent impossibility (risk, Ch. V), mere inability (warranty), quality defect.
  • Property overlap: superficies solo cedit — why does it not decide the contract question?
