A. The problem — Example 74

Clemens' livestock stall. Ago buys a pig, Bellona a goose; Clemens trusts them, gives a week to pay, they take the animals. Two weeks later: Ago is a son in paternal power, Bellona is Dexter's slave.
Ago (son)
can be sued — but owns nothing → judgment cannot be enforced
Bellona (slave)
owns nothing and cannot even be sued

→ The creditor needs access to the wealth of the principal behind them (dominus / pater familias).

Persons alieni iuris (slaves, children in patria potestas) own nothing themselves and acquire only for the power-holder. That is exactly why a contract with them leaves the creditor facing an empty pocket. → Property Ch. I · III

Debts of persons in power

  • binding as natural obligations (obligationes naturales): can be validly performed; needs some capacity to act — even slaves have it
  • sons: enforceable obligations (Gaius D 44.7.39: sued like a pater familias), but execution fails — no property
  • daughters, uxores in manu, slaves: no enforceable obligations at all (Gai Inst 3.104)

B.1 What the actions do

The "additional" actions extend a contractual claim to the principal. de peculio, de in rem verso, quod iussu need a debtor in the principal's power; exercitoria and institoria do not.

B.2 Why should the principal pay?

a. Benefit

He profits from the business his "manager" conducts → he should answer for obligations incurred in his name.

b. Trust

Commerce lives on trust: a creditor who reasonably relied on the partner's wealth gets access to it.

Example 74 cont. Dexter writes to Clemens asking him to sell Ago a pig = iussum. Clemens relied on Dexter's wealth → price claim against Dexter by actio quod iussu.

B.3 Structure — added, not transferred

HOC ENIM EDICTO NON TRANSFERTUR ACTIO, SED ADICITURPaulus D 14.1.5.1 — the edict adds an action, it does not transfer it
  • not an independent claim: the contract claim + a further debtor
  • Example 74: actio venditi with a quod iussu clause added to the formula
  • modern law: direct agency binds the principal from the outset → no extension needed

Which can the principal limit?

  • peculium: no — standardised, comprehensive authority; a limiting order has no effect
  • praepositio (ship, business): yes — by explicit declaration to prospective counterparties
  • iussum: its own scope — one deal or a whole class of contracts

The actio de peculio — cap, valuation, several creditors

C. The ACTIO DE PECULIO
A peculium = assets the dominus lets a person in his power manage independently — a "branch". In property law it is his general, abstract intent to acquire: whatever the holder acquires in business, he acquires for the dominus, even without his knowledge. Here the same grant also creates liability. → Property Ch. III

C.1–3 When does it arise?

  • granting a peculium → liable for the dependant's debts
  • every contractual obligation of the holder — not only those linked to the grantor's economic aims
  • authority is standardised: an order limiting the holder's scope does not affect the action (Gaius D 15.1.29.1 · Case 243/1; Paulus D 15.1.47 pr · Case 243/2)
  • contrast: limits on an institor are valid → no actio institoria for deals the praepositio excludes (Case 243/2)

C.4 Extent of liability

PRO VIRIBUS
capped at an amount — this is the de peculio type
CUM VIRIBUS
limited to particular assets
  • cap = value of the peculium, but the creditor may reach all the dominus' assets
  • the dependant's own liability is unlimited — matters on release from power (Ulpian D 15.1.44 · Case 241/3)

C.5 Valuing the peculium

  • value at the time of judgment — it fluctuates with business and markets (Papirius Fronto, Marcian D 15.1.40 pr · Case 241/2)
  • deduct the dominus' claims against the peculium; add the holder's claims against the dominus (Tubero/Celsus, Ulpian D 15.1.5.4 · Case 241/1)

  • grantor may withdraw / reshape it at any time …

  • not dolose: paying a debt — it validly lowers the value and so the cap for later creditors

Special bankruptcy — merx peculiaris

For a peculium devoted to trade, the praetor may order a special bankruptcy. If the dominus administers it and dolo malo pays a creditor too little → actio tributoria for the shortfall. It reflects his own duty of even distribution, so it is not an adjectitial action.

D. ACTIO DE IN REM VERSO · E. ACTIO QUOD IUSSU

D.1–2 Liability as far as the dominus is enriched (versio)

  • stands next to de peculio in the same part of the formula → used where there is no peculium or the claim exceeds it (Servius, Alfenus D 15.3.16 · Case 245: peculium assumed exhausted, then: was there a versio?)

D.3 Three kinds of versio

a. pays the master's debt

he saves the payment

Example 77 Scipio owes 300 rent; slave Maevius borrows from Felix and pays it.

Case 246 (Scaevola D 15.3.20 pr): father's dowry/maintenance for daughter paid with a loan from her husband — maintenance named first.

b. necessary expenses

master again spared paying

Example 78 Scipio leaves without provisioning his slaves; son Victor buys bread and wine on credit to feed them.

Case 246: food for the father's slaves also a versio.

c. into regular capital

assets he manages directly, not through dependants

Example 79 Maevius buys seed and sows it on a farm Scipio runs himself.

Toga case (Neratius, Paulus D 15.3.19 pr): son buys a toga, dies; unknowing father uses it for the funeral → in rem patris versum.

D.4 How much? — and must the money arrive?

  • creditor sues on his contract, but gets at most the enrichment — and if the agreed price was lower, only that price (Gaius D 15.3.12 · Case 244)
  • needs an actual versio: proceeds never reach the dominus → no claim, even if the dependant pretended they would (Ulpian D 15.3.3.9 · Case 247)
  • Julian (Africanus D 15.3.17 pr · Case 248): if the dependant genuinely meant to use the money for the master, the action lies even if the slave loses it without fault — versio already with the datio; perhaps beyond the prevailing view

Parallel: utiliter coeptum of negotiorum gestio — usefulness judged ex ante → Ch. XII · same action as an enrichment remedy → Ch. XIV.K

F. The ship operator (exercitor)

  • makes known he put a captain (magister navis) in charge → liable for all business obligations the captain takes on in running the ship
  • praepositio = appointment as managing captain; unqualified → all of the ordinary operation

Ordinary business (Ulpian D 14.1.1.7 · Case 252; Pegasus D 14.1.1.8 · Case 254):

contracts to carry goods
buying things useful for the ship
repair costs
sailors' wages
Case 253 · Ulpian D 14.1.1.12. praepositio limited to collecting freight. The captain charters out the whole ship → exercitor not liable on that lease. Narrowing works by explicit declaration to prospective counterparties.

Who can be captain / manager?

in the principal's power
in another's power
in nobody's power

All three work for exercitoria and institoria — unlike de peculio / de in rem verso / quod iussu.

G. The business manager (institor)

  • business owner makes known he entrusted management to an institor → liable for all obligations causally connected with running the business (Ulpian D 14.3.5 pr, 1 · Case 256)
  • praepositio = externally recognisable appointment (Ulpian D 14.3.11.2 · Case 258)
  • unqualified → all obligations in the ordinary course; e.g. a business loan (Ulpian D 14.3.5.13 · Case 257)
  • restriction by explicit declaration (D 14.3.11.3 · Case 258): if the praepositio forbids borrowing → no action for a loan the institor took anyway, even in the ordinary course (Case 257)
Case 259 · Ulpian D 14.3.5.10 — the laundry. Owner leaving on a journey puts an assistant in charge. Purely internal (procurator) or also authorised to deal with third parties (institor)? Ulpian: adjectitial liability only in the second case. Text (probably Labeo's) unclear to whom — recent view: one of his assistants (discipulus).

Modern parallel

"Apparent authority", § 1029 sentence 2 ABGB: whoever entrusts someone with managing his affairs is presumed to have authorised what that management normally requires — again third-party reliance on the appointment.

G.5 Papinian's analogies — actio quasi institoria

Sources: actio (utilis) ad exemplum institoriae actionis. Institor = deals with third parties · procurator = internal administration.

a. procurator praepositus
gets a praepositio for external dealings → liable as for an institor. Case 260 (Papinian D 14.3.19 pr): procurator appointed to take up loans → dominus liable alongside him, not just subsidiarily — even if the procurator is solvent or bound by stipulation.
⇣
b. no praepositio, only a mandatum
third party contracts knowing of and relying on the mandate. Case 261 (Ulpian/Papinian D 19.1.13.25): mandate to sell; buyer bought because he knew → liable by analogy. Case 262 (D 17.1.10.5): a surety who paid because of a mandate to borrow has recourse against both dominus and procurator.
⇣
c. significance
the most important step towards a general concept of direct agency — acquiring rights and incurring obligations for the represented person.

Negotiorum gestio — acting for another uninvited

book pp. 260–263
A. Definition · B. Another's business

A.1 Definition

Someone deliberately takes care of another's affairs without being bound or authorised by mandate or other contract.

Both are bonae fidei iudicia.

A.3–4 Origin and nature

  • origin: looking after an absent friend's affairs out of amicitia
  • earliest form: defending an absens indefensus — undefended absence (indefensio) led to missio in bona (seizure and sale for the creditors); if the gestor loses, judgment goes against him
  • like mandatum: (free) management of another's affairs — but no agreement → a quasi-contract: duties from a contract-like factual situation
  • common law: partly "agency without specific authority", but far more restrictive

The gates of the gestor's claim

1

Another's affair (negotium alienum)

falls within, or directly benefits, the principal's legal sphere

2

Intent to manage for him

knowingly, with animus rem alteri gerendi

3

No mandate / contract

otherwise contract rules apply

4

Intent to be reimbursed

animus recipiendi — not pietas, not a gift

5

Usefully begun

utiliter coeptum: judged ex ante, by the principal's own interests → next page

B.2 Mixed interests suffice

A deal serving the gestor that has a separate component serving another is negotiorum gestio to that extent (as in mandatum).

Example 80 · Aron and Iokaste co-own a house in equal shares. Without asking her, Aron spends 10,000 on the roof — necessary, serves him and her → as gestor he claims half (5,000).
Co-owners each hold an ideal share of the whole thing, so repairing the roof is partly "his" and partly "her" business. → Property Ch. V

B.4 Family ties or a gift? — no reimbursement

  • support owed by family ties or out of pietas is not another's business: parents → children (Case 225), stepchildren (Case 226), uncle → sister's daughter (Modestinus D 3.5.26(27).1 · Case 224)
  • paid neither as a legal duty nor out of pietas, but expecting reimbursement → actio n. g. contraria (Cases 225, 226)
  • gift or gestio? depends on the circumstances and type of expenses (Celsus D 24.1.47 · Case 227)

Suretyship — three persons, one debt

book pp. 271–274
A. Problem · B. Characteristics · C. FIDEIUSSIO

A. Why security? — Example 81

Nike, short of cash and with nothing to pledge, asks Agamemnon for a bridging loan. He wants someone who promises to repay if she cannot: her friend Pelias. Loan 10,000. At the first instalment Nike is insolvent → Agamemnon claims from Pelias and loses nothing — unless Pelias is insolvent too.

Real vs personal security

pledge (real)suretyship (personal)
what is liableonly the pledged thingall the surety's assets — a second estate
kind of rightright in remclaim in personam
if the other is bankruptpriority over other creditorspro rata with all other creditors
A pledge gives the creditor a right in the thing itself (he may sell it to satisfy the debt and ranks ahead of others); a fideiussio gives only a claim against another person, not a real right in his property. → Property Ch. XII

B. The triangle

  • ② is a contract creditor–surety, but tied to ① → the creditor has a claim against both
  • more persons possible: several sureties = co-sureties; a surety for the surety (sub-surety)
  • ③ = internal relationship: a contract (mandate) or none at all (negotiorum gestio)
  • creditor sues the surety presumably by actio ex stipulatu

B.4 An intercession

The surety "steps between" (intercedere) and assumes another's debt. SC Velleianum: women may not intercede — the act is still valid, but she has an exceptio SC Velleiani.

C. Fideiussio — the classical form

forerunners: sponsio, fidepromissio — secure only stipulated claims (others must first be novated)
➜
classical: fideiussio — the most important form

A verbal contract = a kind of stipulatio: creditor's question, guarantor's "yes".

Money is special: mixing another's coins indistinguishably with one's own gives original ownership of the whole; spending them in good faith blocks the owner's rei vindicatio against the recipient. Recourse — getting the money back from the debtor

book pp. 276–280
E. The guarantor's right of recourse

E.1 The problem. Formally the surety is sued like the debtor; materially he pays someone else's debt (Example 81: Pelias paid; Nike later inherits a fortune). Recourse = the secondary obligor's right to reimbursement from the person the debt materially belongs to. Which action depends on the internal relationship ③.

Route 1 — mandate (E.2)

surety at the debtor's request or apparent approval
➜
surety = debtor's mandatarius
➜
actio mandati contraria
  • apparent approval: e.g. standing surety in the debtor's presence (Ulpian D 17.1.6.2 · Case 51 seq)
  • payment = expenses of duly performing the mandate
  • further duties: whoever is asked to pay must notify the other — so the creditor is not paid twice

Case 177 · Ulpian D 17.1.29.3 — the double payment

1. surety G (under mandate) pays creditor C — without telling debtor D
2. D pays C again — a debt no longer owed
3. G sues D by actio mandati contraria → no claim: not informing comes very close to dolus (culpa lata, for which even a mandatarius is liable)
4. but G may require D to cede D's condictio indebiti against C → G must recover the second payment from C and bears C's insolvency risk
Exercise 88 · gold ring 7,000. Surety in the debtor's presence pays without telling him; a week later the creditor (disorderly accounts) also takes 7,000 from the debtor. → the Case 177 pattern: mandate? duty to inform? who reclaims the double payment?

Route 2 — negotiorum gestio (E.3)

no contract with the debtor
➜
pays with animus recipiendi, not to donate
➜
actio n. g. contraria
  • he pays because the suretyship obliges him — but discharges a debt that materially belongs to the debtor = intervention in another's affairs → Ch. XII
  • normally useful: it frees the debtor from the creditor
  • not useful if the debt did not exist or the debtor had a permanent exceptio → then he reclaims from the creditor by condictio indebiti (Ulpian D 12.6.25 · Case 51 e) → Ch. XIV.D

Surety prohibente domino — disputed

most jurists: no
NG rules discourage unauthorised meddling → no actio n. g. contraria. Paulus, Pomponius (D 17.1.40 · Case 223/1); Julian, followed by Justinian (C 2.18(19).24.1 · Case 223/2)
some: yes
the payment enriches the debtor at the surety's expense → NG action or at least an actio utilis (names not given in the excerpts)
Case 262 · Ulpian (Papinian) D 17.1.10.5. A surety who guaranteed and paid because of a mandate to borrow (from a dominus to his procurator) has recourse against both: the dominus (by analogy to the actio institoria) and the procurator. → Ch. XI

Route 3 — assignment: beneficium cedendarum actionum (E.4)

surety pays the creditor
➜
creditor assigns his claim against the debtor
➜
surety sues on the creditor's action
  • technique: mandatum in rem suam between creditor and surety
  • advantage: securities still attached pass with it — pledges, other sureties
  • why the claim does not lapse on payment: the surety, as it were, buys the creditor's claim (Paulus D 46.1.36 · Case 51 h)
  • historically the most important basis of the surety's recourse

From favour to statute

classical paying surety may require the creditor to assign (agreed assignment)
Justinian a right to demand assignment = cessio necessaria
ABGB claim passes by law (§ 1358) = cessio legis
A pledge given for the debt does not vanish when the surety pays: with the assigned claim the surety also takes over the creditor's real security — that is the practical value of route 3. → Property Ch. XII

Vindicate or condict? — the property-law test

book pp. 288–290
A. General · B. Interplay with property law

A. What is unjustified enrichment?

Assets moved without a justifying legal cause → they went to someone not entitled. Reversed above all by condictiones, also by various actiones in factum.

Example 83 · Agatha → Belias. Both wrongly think she owes him 100 under a sale; she pays. He mixes the coins with his own → becomes owner. → Agatha: condictio.
Example 84 · Castor plants Pomona's olive trees on his land by mistake; they take root → he is owner by accession. → Pomona: actio in factum for her loss.

B. The decision tree — first ask property law, then obligations

An asset moved from X to Y without a (lasting) legal cause. Did Y become owner?
NO — X is still owner
→ rei vindicatio (or actio publiciana). Traditio needs a iusta causa.
Example 85 slave delivers Jove's statue to the wrong house (Cassandra) → Agrippa vindicates.
Example 86 Philto, a prodigus, sells a vase without his curator → no causa, Helena not owner.
YES — by traditio with iusta causa
the causa normally also justifies keeping → no claim. Exceptions:
  • title pro soluto: owner even if the debt did not exist → condictio
  • causa falls away later (contract dissolved ex nunc) → owner for now, but must re-transfer
YES — by abstract conveyance
mancipatio, in iure cessio: ownership passes even if the deal was void ab initio → X loses his action in rem → enrichment claim (condictio sine causa in the narrow sense)
YES — by original acquisition
accession, consumption of money, specificatio → must be balanced:
  • by performance → condictio (Example 83)
  • no performance → praetorian remedies, e.g. actio in factum (Example 84) → Ch. XIV.K
YES — by usucapio
after the period the usucapient is owner and the former owner has no enrichment claim. Same for fruits acquired by a bonae fidei possessor: no compensation.
Traditio transfers ownership only with a iusta causa (sale, gift, dowry …) — it is causal. No causa → no ownership → the thing can still be vindicated. → Property Ch. VII
Mancipatio and in iure cessio are abstract formal acts: the cause does not matter, ownership passes anyway — so only the law of obligations can undo it. → Property Ch. VII
Mixing another's coins with one's own or spending them (nummos consumere), accession and specificatio make the acquirer owner without the old owner's act — the old owner's vindicatio fails. → Property Ch. IX
The rei vindicatio works against anyone who possesses the thing, but only while it still exists; a condictio is a claim in personam for a sum or thing. → Property Ch. X · usucapio → Ch. VIII

B.2 Not only things — claims can be acquired without cause too

Example 87. Cato promises Felix 100 by an abstract stipulation (meant as dos for Felix's marriage to Cato's daughter; no reason stated). The marriage never happens; Felix sues. The promise is valid → Cato raises an exceptio doli; later he may also sue to be released: condictio liberationis.

The condictio — and the condictio indebiti

book pp. 291–294
C. The CONDICTIO as an action type · D. CONDICTIO INDEBITI

C. What a condictio is

  • actio in personam for a specific sum (certa pecunia) or a specific thing (certa res)
  • also used in contract: lender's claim for repayment (for money: actio certae creditae pecuniae); a stipulated certum
  • jurists often just say "may bring a condictio" — types are distinguished by the facts

The family of condictions

indebiti — paid what was not owed (D)
ob rem = causa data causa non secuta — expected result fails (E)
ob turpem vel iniustam causam — immoral / illegal purpose (F)
sine causa (narrow) — abstract conveyance without valid causa
ob causam finitam — cause existed, later ceased (E)
liberationis — be released from an abstract promise (B.2)

condictio sine causa has two meanings: the narrow one above, or an umbrella for all enrichment condictions. The condictio ex causa furtiva is, from a modern view, a delictual damages action → G

D. Condictio indebiti — reverse a performance of a debt wrongly believed to exist

1

Performance

  • a transfer that makes the recipient owner (datio) → Gaius: a real obligation (Gai Inst 3.91 · Case 229)
  • recipient not owner → the performer keeps rei vindicatio / publiciana
  • intentional on both sides; a pure property-law shift is no performance
Example 88 Solinus unknowingly solders Cara's iron onto his statue → owner by accession, no performance; Cara: perhaps actio in factum.
Case 230 · Julian D 12.6.33 building on another's land by mistake → no condictio (no transaction); a builder in possession has only a right of retention.
2

Not owed (indebitum)

  • counts as not owed: debt barred by a permanent exceptio (Example 89); paid before a suspensive condition is fulfilled (Pomponius D 12.6.16 pr · Case 235/3); a ward's debt contracted without the tutor — not even natural (Neratius D 12.6.41 · Case 232)
  • owed: a natural obligation — e.g. son repays despite the SC Macedonianum; paying before the due date (Paulus D 12.6.10 · Case 235/2)
Example 89 Damon promised Lysippe 50; she informally waived it; he pays by mistake → pactum de non petendo = permanent exceptio → reclaims 50.
3

By mistake

  • he believed himself obliged; mistake of law probably suffices (§ 1431 ABGB says so)
  • paid knowing nothing was owed → no condictio: similis videbitur ei qui donat (Paulus D 46.2.12)
  • recipient knows there is no debt → accepting is furtum → actio furti + condictio furtiva
Under superficies solo cedit whatever is built on land belongs to the landowner — the builder loses his materials by accession, not by a "payment". That is why Julian refuses a condictio. → Property Ch. IX
Furtum = knowingly and unlawfully taking possession of another's movable to enrich oneself. Someone who knowingly pockets a mistaken payment commits it. → Property Ch. II

H.1 The triangle of a payment order (delegatio)

Defendant liable for its value if the loss was due to his negligence / fault, or happened after he fell into default. Contrast the thief: fur semper in mora est → always liable → G

K.1 No performance — no condiction

Some unjustified shifts rest on original acquisition, not performance:

Example 98. Thisbe, in good faith, builds a house with her own materials on Eudemos' land → superficies solo cedit: Eudemos owns it.
Example 99. Alcesinus carves a statue from Peitho's wood, thinking it his own → by specificatio he is owner (at least on the media sententia and the Proculian view).

Thisbe loses her materials, Peitho her wood — but there was no performance → no condictio.

Specificatio = making a new thing from another's material. Sabinians: the material's owner keeps it; Proculians: the maker owns it; the media sententia gives it to the maker if the thing cannot be turned back into its material. Either way the loser needs a remedy in obligations. → Property Ch. IX

Exam: enrichment — ask

  • Property first: did the recipient become owner? If not → rei vindicatio. If yes, how (traditio, abstract act, original, usucapio)?
  • Performance? If none → praetorian remedy, measured by enrichment.
  • Which condiction? mistake about a debt · failed expectation · shameful purpose · theft.
  • Exclusions: knowing payment, natural obligation, in pari turpitudine, ward without tutor.
  • Three persons? Draw the triangle, find the defective relationship.
  • Amount: still has it? sold it (surrogate)? good or bad faith? lost through fault or in default?
