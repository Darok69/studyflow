The one who works is conductor in a contract for work, but locator in employment. Both actions are bonae fidei iudicia.

Common features

  • Synallagmatic: use / result / labour ⇄ merces.
  • Consensual: agreement on the performance and the merces is enough.
  • merces must be: money (exception: tenant farming), seriously meant (rent nummo uno is void, Case 146), certain (merces certa, Case 144).

Lease I — duties, security, ending

book pp. 178–180
B.1 locatio conductio rei · B.2 termination

Use (and, if agreed, the fruits) of a thing against rent. Objects: chiefly material, non-consumable things, but also rights (a usufruct can be leased). Roman jurists did not split lease and usufructuary lease; the ABGB calls both Bestandvertrag.

Lessor (locator) owes

  • place the thing at the lessee's disposal, usable as agreed, kept in good repair (Case 148)
  • as owner, bears the risk of vis maior damage / loss
  • bears the rent risk: no or reduced rent after vis maior
  • probably reimburses necessary / useful expenditure (Case 159); lessee has a ius tollendi for additions he built (D 19.2.19.4)
⇄

Lessee (conductor) owes

  • rent for the use
  • from takeover: liability for custodia — damages for harm or loss through dolus, culpa or lack of proper guarding
  • usufructuary lease of a farm: duty to cultivate it properly (Case 160)
The lessee only detains the thing (naturalis possessio: he holds it for the lessor); the lessor stays possessor through him and is usually the owner. That is why the lessor carries the owner's accidental risk here, and why the lessee has only a contractual claim against the lessor, no right in the thing. → Property Ch. II

Security: pignus tacitum — arises automatically with the contract

Lessor of a flat
lien over what the tenant brought in = invecta illata
Lessor of farmland
pignus in the fruits of the land
Invecta illata = movables (furniture, slaves) brought in to stay. The landlord may seize them himself, without a court action; the tenant gets them back by interdictum de migrando. The farm lessor has no seizure: he sues by vindicatio pignoris. → Property Ch. XII
The colonus (usufructuary lessee) becomes owner of the fruits only when he gathers them (perceptio); until then they belong to the lessor, who cannot have a pledge in his own property. Unlike a usufruct (a right in rem), the lease gives only a contractual right, enforced by actio conducti. → Property Ch. IX · XI

Lease II — damage to the thing: who bears what?

book pp. 180–184
B.3.a–b · Cases 163, 151

① Lessee's fault / no custodia

Damage or destruction through his negligence or fault, or loss through lack of custodia (Cases 161, 162):

  • lessee pays damages
  • and the rent stays due: he cannot escape rent through damage he caused himself

② Vis maior — from outside

vis cui resisti non potest → lessor's sphere. As owner he bears the loss: casum sentit dominus.

  • performance becomes defective = a quality defect → lessee's warranty: (1) rent reduction (remissio mercedis, Case 152) or (2) rescission
  • only ex nunc: full rent for the time before (unlike sale)

③ Vitia quae ex ipsa re oriuntur

Defects "arising out of the thing itself": wine turning sour, seed ruined by worms or weeds.

  • production risks tied to the lessee's own activity → his sphere
  • loss on the lessee; full rent stays due

Rent reduction in practice

Case 148 A new building nearby cuts off the flat's light → rent reduction, even termination.
Case 149 Lessor unexpectedly must tear down part of the house, seriously curtailing use → lessee may deduct from the rent. A minor inconvenience gives no reduction.
Colonia partiaria (tenant farming, sharecropping): the colonus partiarius pays a fixed share of the crop, not money → crop-failure risk is shared permanently; the lessor gets a proportional share of the yield (Case 153).

Servius in Ulpian D 19.2.15.2 · Case 151 — sort the events

vis maior → lessor (no or reduced rent)
jackdaws or starlings descending · enemy invasion · mudslide washing away the crop · fungus or extreme heat on the olives · earthquake making the field cave in
"out of the thing itself" → lessee (full rent)
wine turns sour · seed ruined by worms or weeds

Rationale: outside events = lessor's sphere; production risks = lessee's sphere. The same sphere logic returns in the contract for work (Case 167).

Ulpian (Labeo) D 19.2.13.7 · Case 163 — the tenant and the approaching soldiers

Rent on a time line — warranty works only ex nunc

contract → use as agreed

Contrast sale: warranty remedies there reach back (ex tunc). A lease is a continuous obligation, so only the defective period is affected. → Ch. VI

Casum sentit dominus: an accidental loss falls on the owner of the thing. The lessee does not own the thing; what he loses is the use he pays for, hence the rent reduction. → Property Ch. V

d. Eviction — "sale breaks lease"

A third party's right in rem deprives the lessee → lessor liable for the expectation interest (he may offer a comparable flat, Case 155). Typical case: the lessor sells (Case 158).

lessor sells the house
➜
buyer becomes owner, owes the lessee nothing
➜
buyer evicts the lessee
➜
lessee sues lessor under the lease

The byword misleads: not the sale but the eviction "breaks" it, and the lease is not terminated: the lessor's performance has become defective.

The lessee has only a claim in personam against his lessor. The new owner has a right in rem, good against everyone (rei vindicatio), so he can take the house from any holder. → Property Ch. I · X

Protecting the lessee: a promise by the buyer?

The lessor can make the buyer promise not to evict. The lessee gets no claim from it: no true contracts for the benefit of third parties — alteri stipulari nemo potest (D 45.1.38.17). If the buyer evicts, he is liable to the seller.

Ulpian D 19.2.7 + Tryphonin D 19.2.8 · Case 156

X owner
✕
A lessor
rent 50
➜
B lessee + sub-lessor
rent 60
➜
C sub-lessee, evicted by X
  • B is liable to C and passes the loss to A by actio conducti — he can get more than the 50 he paid. Tryphonin: the interest must be paid; C's may exceed 60 (substitute flat costs 80).
  • C gets 80 from B (60 refunded + 20). a. B had prepaid 50 → claims 80 from A. b. B had not paid → claims 30 (80 − 50).

True contract for a third party

A and B contract so that C can claim performance himself. Roman law does not know it.

For a claimless third party

A and B agree performance to C, but C gets no enforceable claim. This is all the lessee gets from the buyer's promise.

Practise it

Ex. 48 Daphne leases to Eros (2 years, 500/year) and sells after one year to Felix: Felix wants 700 · Felix demolishes, Daphne offers another house at 500 · Felix had stipulated to let Eros stay, then evicts him.

Contract for work I — result, adprobatio, materials

book pp. 187–189
C.1–2 locatio conductio operis

An opus (work, result) for a fee: from a musical performance or a heavy-goods transport to teaching or building a house.

Contractor (conductor) owes

  • the agreed result
  • for the customer's things he holds: dolus, culpa, custodia
  • imperitia (lack of skill) = negligence: he works as an expert (Case 165)
  • has a right of retention over the customer's things until paid
Case 166 · the launderer A fullo stores a coat so that mice gnaw it, or returns the wrong coat → breach (presumably of custodia).
⇄

Customer (locator) owes

  • the fee, even if the opus is not delivered for reasons outside the contractor's sphere
  • no fee if the work fails within the contractor's sphere or through his fault
Ex. 61 · Phobos' coat Coat stays uncleaned because Phobos insists on getting it back next day → Helena keeps the fee. Helena suddenly falls ill → she loses it.

Adprobatio — acceptance, comparable to traditio in sale

contractagreement on opus + fee
workcontractor holds the customer's things → custodia; risk on the contractor's sphere
offer of the workcontractor presents it
adprobatiocustomer accepts → contract fulfilled; risk passes to the customer
afterdefects → warranty rules (Case 167a)

Who owns the materials?

Often the customer supplies them. The contractor does not become owner by specificatio: both agree the material is worked on for the customer.

Specificatio = making a new thing out of another's material. Without consent the maker can become owner (irreversible change) or the material owner keeps it (reversible, e.g. metal). With a work contract, ownership stays with whoever supplied the material and ordered the work. → Property Ch. IX

C.2 Contract for work and materials

The contractor uses (mainly) his own materials and must deliver and transfer ownership of the work. Disputed at first; classical law: a sub-type of emptio venditio (Cases 63, 144). But building contracts are always locatio conductio, whoever supplies the materials, since the principal thing (the land) comes from the customer.

A building firmly fixed to land follows the land: superficies solo cedit. The customer owns the house as owner of the land, so the builder is never "selling" it. → Property Ch. IX

Contract for work II — harm, fee risk, spheres

book pp. 189–194
C.3 case law · Cases 169, 170, 165a, 168, 167, 167a

a. Ulpian D 19.2.13.3 · Case 169

Facts A slave left for vocational training is taken abroad, where he is captured or dies.

Decision: culpable breach by the contractor.
Why: capture and death are vis maior, but his breach made them possible. Not liable if the contract allowed taking him abroad.

a. Gaius D 19.2.25.7 · Case 170

Facts A transports B's column for a fee; it breaks while being handled.

Decision: A answers for his own and his helpers' fault; standard = the most careful man (diligentissimus). Breaks from a hidden flaw despite such care → not liable.
Fee (Gaius silent): probably owed as far as performed; e.g. column carried, gate damaged on unloading → fee + damages.

Gaius Inst 3.205 · Case 165a

Facts The customer's thing is stolen from the contractor.

Decision: the contractor bears the loss via custodia → he has the actio furti. If he is insolvent (his liability worthless) → the customer as owner keeps it. Same pattern for the commodatary (Case 28).

Furtum = taking another's movable to enrich oneself; the actio furti is a penalty action. Here it goes to whoever bears the loss. → Property Ch. II

c. Labeo (Paulus) D 14.2.10 pr · Case 168 — the slave who died on board

Facts A ships several slaves of B for a fee; one dies on board (natural death = vis maior → owner B loses the slave).
  • Only question: full freight?
  • Paulus + prevailing view: yes once the slave was on board: periculum est locatoris.
  • Labeo: no freight for the dead slave.
  • The parties can shift the risk: payment due only on disembarkation → nothing owed for him.

d. Labeo (Paulus) D 19.2.62 · Case 167 — the sphere theory

Facts A builds a canal on B's land; after completion, before adprobatio, the embankment collapses.
vitium operis
faulty workmanship → contractor's sphere → no fee
vitium soli
soil unsuitable → customer's sphere → customer pays

Other cases, incl. vis maior from neither sphere: periculum est locatoris. Labeo (no spheres): collapse → contractor gets nothing. Cf § 1168 ABGB.

e. Javolenus D 19.2.51.1 · Case 167a — paid daily, work faulty

Facts: fee in daily instalments; contractor must prove quality by adprobatio; work done faultily.
Decision: contractor liable; customer sues by actio locati. The daily pay changes nothing.

Open reactions: refuse adprobatio → contract unfulfilled, contractor in debtor's delay; or approve, then claim price reduction (even rescission) by actio locati. If he knew of the defect: probably consequential damage too.

Exception: if the daily pay means the customer supervises and dictates the technical execution, the defects are no longer the contractor's.

Who bears the fee risk? — the jurists on one line

Labeo (Cases 167, 168)
strict for the contractor: collapsed canal → nothing; dead slave → no freight for him
Paulus: spheres (Case 167)
vitium operis → contractor loses fee; vitium soli → customer pays
Paulus + prevailing view (Case 168)
periculum est locatoris: once on board, freight owed despite vis maior
Party agreement (Case 168)
e.g. pay only on disembarkation → the risk shifts back to the carrier

Separate from the fee: the thing itself. Its accidental loss stays with the owner (the dead slave is B's loss); the contractor pays damages only for fault or under custodia.

b. Receptum: guarantee liability

nautae (boatmen), caupones (innkeepers), stabularii (stable keepers) guarantee that nothing of the guests' / passengers' belongings is stolen or damaged → actio de recepto.

express promise salvam fore
➜
customary → objective duty from the mere handover (recipere)
  • Strict: no fault needed. Vis maior excluded by exceptio doli → same scope as custodia.
  • Why: to curb the dishonesty of these trades (D 4.9.3.1). Today: "danger of the open house" (§§ 970 ff ABGB).
  • No locatio conductio needed: also free carriage / lodging (Case 215). Freight and personal belongings (Case 214); interest enough, not ownership.

C.4 Locatio conductio irregularis

Normally the customer stays possessor and owner; the contractor is a mere detentor. Two exceptions (Case 172): the contractor becomes owner.

Precious metal Customer supplies silver / gold for vessels or rings; contractor may use it or his own → owner on handover; must transfer ownership of the finished work and account for the material.
Grain in the hold Several owners' grain is poured together and mixed → carrier becomes sole owner; each delivery to a recipient = transfer of ownership.

Why: practical. Smith works from his own stock; carrier ships consolidated cargo and passes ownership without proving third-party ownership. Risk: he is probably liable only for negligence, despite owning the cargo.

Normally, owners who mix grain with consent become co-owners of the mixture (commixtio) and divide by actio communi dividundo. Here, instead, the carrier is sole owner. → Property Ch. IX

D. Employment — operae, not an opus

A person puts his labour at another's disposal for a set period for pay; typically repeated activity following another's directions. In Rome most labour came from slaves; paid work stood low socially.

employee = locator
render the services
employer = conductor
pay the wage

Wage risk — again by sphere

  • Rule: full wage if non-performance is not the employee's fault.
  • Employee's own illness → no operae, no wage.
  • Employer's illness or death → employer side bears it.
Ulpian D 19.2.19.9 B hired as A's scribe for one year; A dies soon after. Rescript of Caracalla + Septimius Severus: full wage, as it is not the scribe's fault, minus what he earns elsewhere that year (cf § 1155 ABGB).

Exam: for any locatio conductio fragment — ask

  • Which type? A thing, a result, or labour? Who is locator, who conductor → which action?
  • Is it really L. C.? Money merces, seriously meant and certain? If free → commodatum / mandatum; own materials → sale; else innominate.
  • What failed, and from whose sphere did the cause come? Fault, own sphere, other's sphere, vis maior?
  • Separate the two questions: damages for the thing vs rent / fee / wage (Cases 163, 168, 170).
  • Before or after adprobatio? Did the lessor know of the defect? Effect ex nunc only?
  • Is a third party involved (new owner, sub-lessee, thief)? Who has the claim, who the actio furti?

Mandatum — a free favour that binds

book pp. 203–205 · A–B

The agent (mandatarius) undertakes to carry out, free of charge, a legal or factual act for the principal (mandator). Content: any affair of another, a legal act (standing surety for him) or a factual one (erecting a statue in his honour). A "transaction among friends": the sources often cite amicitia.

The two sides and their actions — an imperfectly bilateral contract

⟵ actio mandati contraria — can arise
expenses and losses from the execution · never a fee

Both are bonae fidei iudicia: the intentio refers to oportere ex fide bona.

a. Consensual

Formed by agreement alone. Despite "to mandate" = "to order", it is not a one-sided command.

b. Consent must last

If agreement falls away, either party may end it by unilateral declaration (revocatio / renuntiatio) while there is still res integra = ending causes neither side reliance loss.

Case 175: why does the value rise go to the physician? Buildings firmly fixed to land belong to the landowner: superficies solo cedit. So the baths are his, not Quietus'. → Property Ch. IX

Ulpian (Celsus) D 17.1.16 · Case 175

Facts Aurelius Quietus spends time each year in his physician's gardens at Ravenna. He asks the physician to build a ball court, steam baths and other health buildings at Quietus' expense; the physician builds them. actio mandati contraria for the costs?

Decision: reimbursement only of the costs exceeding the rise in value of the physician's own property.
Why: as far as his land gained value, building served his own interest; only the excess was spent purely in the mandator's interest.

The duty to "surrender what he obtained" = the second handover, enforced by actio mandati directa.

Why two handovers? A free agent sui iuris could, as a rule, not acquire possession for another (exceptions: tutor, curator, procurator). And traditio transfers ownership only with (1) the transferor's ownership or authority, (2) transfer of possession, (3) a iusta causa — each of the two handovers must meet all three. → Property Ch. III · VII

C. Mandatarius owes

  • 1. Content: carry out the transaction as agreed, safeguarding the mandator's interests (e.g. hand over the thing bought).
  • + bona fides secondary duties (protection, care, information): tell the mandator at once if he cannot perform (Case 185) or of other important facts (Case 177).
  • 2. Liability: does not perform though he could → expectation interest (Case 185).
  • Standard: only dolus + culpa lata — fitting a gratuitous act of friendship.
Ex. 66 · Laura and the medicine Seriously ill Laura asks Xerxes to bring a medicine from Greece. He breaks his leg → he must tell her so she can ask someone else. Not telling her = culpa lata → liable.
⇄

D. Mandator owes

  • 1. Expenses: only those incurred bona fide in duly performing the mandate (Case 178).
  • 2. Losses: if directly linked to executing the mandate, irrespective of fault = the mandator's liability for risk. Why: whoever benefits from the free performance should carry the risk of accidental harm (Case 181).
  • Limit: not the general risk of life: robbery, shipwreck, illness (Case 179).
  • 3. He too has bona fides secondary duties.
Ex. 70 · Omphale's painting She asks Teukros to obtain a painting, then buys it herself → she must tell him to stop trying.

Can the mandatarius recover it? — the ladder for expenses and losses

Ex. 67 · the hired horse
Bellona is to carry Agathon's message to Cassius; she hires a horse though riding was not agreed → not within the mandate, not reimbursed.
Ex. 69 · the robbed messenger
Orestes, carrying Elektra's message, is robbed of 100 → causal, but robbery is general risk of life → no claim.
Ex. 68 · the thieving slave
Vibia buys a slave for Solon; while at her house he steals from her → claim by actio mandati contraria: a mandate-specific loss.
How much? — disputed
Neratius, Paulus (Case 182): limited to the slave's value, like noxal liability. Julian (Case 181): the whole loss. Mandator knew he was a thief and did not warn → whole loss in any case.

Why the duties are so unequal — the principle of utility

Agent: acts gratuitously, gains nothing
→ light liability: only dolus + culpa lata
Mandator: gets the benefit of the free performance
→ heavy liability: bears the accidental, mandate-specific losses

The principle of utility is set out in Ch. III (III.C.2) → Ch. III and returns for innominate contracts → Ch. X

E. When the agent cannot or does not act as agreed

1. Impossible, no fault
agent not liable; mandator cannot demand performance but reimburses expenses made before. E.g. the thing cannot be had at the agreed price: if the agent buys dearer, the mandator cannot demand it; an offer at the higher price + acceptance = a new contract.
2. Possible, not done as agreed
agent liable for the mandator's interest (Cases 185, 183). Exceeding the mandate, e.g. paying more than agreed → disputed ↓

Gaius Inst 3.161 · Case 183 — land for 100,000, bought for 150,000

Facts E mandates T to buy land for 100,000 sesterces; T pays 150,000 although 100,000 would have sufficed.
Sabinus + Cassius (Gaius)
E has actio mandati for his interest in correct performance. E may take the land, but need not; he may refuse it even at 100,000. If E does not want it, T has no expense claim, even when willing to hand over at 100,000.
Proculians (Gaius D 17.1.4, Case 184/2)
If T hands the land over, he can recover the agreed 100,000. The overpayment stays his own loss.

Why the Sabinian harshness: T's breach is culpable, so E keeps a free choice. Remarkable: T gets nothing even when he offers the original price.

F. Death — mandatum morte solvitur

rule death of either party ends the mandate — a highly personal relationship; rights and duties do not pass to heirs (unlike a buyer's delivery claim). Ex. 66: Laura dies before Xerxes leaves → the contract lapses.
begun fully true only if execution has not yet begun: expenses already made are reimbursed by the mandator's heir; gains go to the heirs.
unaware the agent who executes bona fide, not knowing of the mandator's death, keeps the actio mandati contraria for costs and losses (Gai Inst 3.160, Case 186).

Excursus — moving a claim: novatio, mandatum in rem suam

book pp. 212–214 · H
how Rome did "cessio" without cession

1. The problem: cessio

Cession = transfer of a claim: a new creditor (cessionary) instead of the old one (cedent) can demand performance from the debtor (debitor cessus).

But a Roman obligatio is a personal bond, with effects only between creditor and debtor. Transfer inter vivos needs a construction. Two routes ↓

2. Novatio — replace the bond

A new obligation (by stipulation) replaces the old; the old one is extinguished, with its securities (pledges, guarantees). Can change the creditor, the debtor or the legal ground (e.g. rent claim → stipulation enforceable by condictio).

Change of creditor needs the debtor's stipulation with the new creditor, at the old creditor's request (delegatio obligandi).

3. Mandatum ad agendum in rem suam

No debtor consent needed: the creditor mandates the new creditor to sue on his behalf, and they agree he keeps the proceeds (basis: e.g. a sale of the claim). He litigates for himself → in rem suam; works like a cession.

Example 71 · Melitta's debt sold by novation

Achilles
creditor: 800, due in 2 years
➜
Pytheas pays Achilles 700 for the claim
➜
Melitta promises Pytheas by stipulation what she owes Achilles

→ Achilles' claim is extinguished; Pytheas has a new one. Underlying deal = a purchase of a not-yet-due claim for 700.

A pledge is accessory: it exists only to secure a claim. Novation kills the old claim, so a pledge or guarantee securing it falls away too. Think of Ex. 62 (Eros' horse). → Property Ch. XII

Weak points of the mandate route — and the repairs

Gordian the procurator in rem suam gets an actio utilis even where the mandate lapsed by the mandator's death (C 4.10.1)

A. Two or more persons, one economic purpose

synallagmatic contract (sale, lease)
A ⇄ B
performance for counter-performance
societas
A ➜ common goal ⟵ B
joint activity towards the agreed aim
  • Consensual; claims between partners: actio pro socio, a bonae fidei iudicium.
  • The agreement defines the goal (usually maximum profit, but e.g. also building and jointly using a house) and each partner's contribution; the rest is filled in by bona fides.
  • No legal person: a purely contractual tie that governs only the partners' internal relations.

B. Contributions and shares

Contributions in kind, capital or labour; the agreement fixes type, amount and the shares in profit and loss.

C.1–2 Standard of liability

Make the contributions and pursue the aim; thwarting it in breach of bona fides → liable for the others' interest. Dolus and culpa; in special cases custodia.

usual: objective
bonus pater familias
partners: subjective
diligentia quam in suis rebus = culpa in concreto

Why (Gaius D 17.2.72, Case 198): whoever picks a careless partner has only himself to blame.

C.3 Beneficium competentiae

A partner condemned under the actio pro socio pays only id quod facere potest — what he can afford.

Exception (Ulpian D 17.2.63 pr, Case 201): he fraudulently reduced his means → condemned for what he could otherwise have paid.

C.4 No set-off of gains

A partner who negligently causes a loss cannot offset it against a special gain he made for the partnership elsewhere (Paulus D 17.2.25, Case 197). Rejected concept: compensatio lucri cum damno (today: "set-off of benefits").

D.1 Ulpian (Julian) D 17.2.52.4 · Case 200

Facts A and B are partners in the clothing trade. On a buying trip A is robbed: his goods and money lost, slaves wounded.

Decision: loss is shared → A has actio pro socio against B for half.
Why: a loss suffered without fault while pursuing the common purpose is a common loss; losses are shared like profits. Expressly covers the money and items carried for purchasing: pursuing the business was the condicio sine qua non of the loss.

Contrast mandate: robbery there is the agent's "general risk of life". → Ch. VIII

D.2 Ulpian (Celsus) D 17.2.58 pr · Case 196 — the quadriga: how far does shared risk reach?

Facts T owns three horses, E one. They agree to sell them together as a quadriga; E gets ¼ of the price. E delivers his horse to T; it dies before the sale.
Why it matters: shared risk covers at any rate the risks typical of the agreed purpose; its extent depends on the type of partnership.
aim = only selling (Celsus)

aim = co-owning a quadriga
partners meant to co-own the horses → loss shared according to the shares (co-ownership → F, next page).

E. How a partnership ends

  • a partner's death or insolvency
  • aim achieved · purpose impossible (Case 196)
  • term expires (fixed-term)
  • termination (renuntiatio) by any partner → dissolves the whole societas; an open-ended one is a continuing obligation, so terminable
Limit 1 — no untimely termination (Case 194): whoever ends it at a moment that causes losses a later, reasonable date would have avoided must compensate them.
Limit 2 — fixed term: early end only for important reasons, e.g. an essential condition fails, or a partner does damage making continuation unreasonable (Cases 193, 194: renuntiatio ex necessitate). Ordinary vs extraordinary notice: as for lease → Ch. VII

F. Contributions in kind — who owns what?

a. quoad usum
contributor keeps ownership and gives only the use; ownership untouched during and after
b. quoad sortem
owner towards third parties; internally counts as partnership property → divided on dissolution per the agreement
c. quoad dominium
co-ownership transferred to all partners in the agreed shares = condominium; also when partners buy jointly
Condominium: each co-owner owns a share (e.g. ⅛) of the whole thing; he may dispose of his share, but the whole needs all. Any co-owner can end it by actio communi dividundo. → Property Ch. V
The societas itself owns nothing: no legal personality; assets belong to single partners or jointly to several
➜
On dissolution, always: claims under the agreement (accounts, profit / loss shares) → actio pro socio. Ex. 72: Agatha's premises + Midas' labour → only these claims
➜
Plus, if co-owned: partition by actio communi dividundo. Ex. 73: Kreon and Hecuba buy land and build the restaurant on it

Don't mix them up: societas (contract) vs condominium (property)

  • Is there a common aim — or only an exchange (Cases 196, 208, 208a)?
  • Shares agreed? Default equal; societas leonina?
  • Fault measured by diligentia quam in suis; beneficium competentiae; no compensatio lucri cum damno.
  • A loss: was it typical of the agreed purpose, and did pursuing it cause it?
  • Ended how? Untimely? Fixed term + important reason?
  • Winding up: actio pro socio only, or also actio communi dividundo?

D. The aestimatory contract

E hands goods to T for sale
➜
sold: T pays the estimated value, keeps the surplus
unsold in time: returns the goods

Action: actio de aestimato, an actio praescriptis verbis with an apparent model formula in the edict (Case 207).

Ulpian D 19.5.13 pr · Case 208 — what is it not?

  • mandate: T is not gratuitous; his reward is the chance of a surplus (a sales mandatarius hands over all proceeds, claims only expenses)
  • societas: no partnership intent; E gets a fixed price, not a share
  • sale (unsold goods may be returned) · lease (reward not fixed) — evidently not considered

But Ulpian D 17.2.44 (Case 195): a societas if they intend to sell together, one giving goods, the other the selling effort → actio pro socio. Doubtful: one wants the value, the other the surplus.

E. Analogies to nominate contracts

As in all bonae fidei iudicia: duties first from the agreement, id quod actum est. Scope, defects, liability and risk follow the most similar nominate contract. Modern law: atypical and mixed contracts are handled the same way.
