Continent: the norm exists first, the case is subsumed under it. Common law: norm and case come into being together, exactly what the continental model was built to prevent.

The codification debate

FOR: Bentham
"A complete digest: such is the first rule." No custom, foreign law, pretended natural law, pretended law of nations. Roman law = a field of eternal disputes; borrowing makes it arbitrary.
If we add a finite and an infinite quantity, the sum will be infinite.Bentham → a closed code mixed with endless Roman law is no longer closed.
AGAINST: and they win
  • customary law shaped over centuries by the courts
  • concrete and flexible
  • tailored decisions → freedom
  • made by judges, seen as the best legal experts
  • plus: distrust of Parliament

Continental logic: only the legislator may make law. English answer: the legislator is the one you would least want to make it.

The 19th century: two tracks

Legislation
  • codification attempts fail: criminal law 1833 and 1878–80; digest of statute + case law 1866–80
  • instead: Consolidation Acts: statute + case law of a field, approved by Parliament, often changing law by selection and reinterpretation
  • codes for the colonies, from India 1860 Topic 13
Case law tightens
  • binding even if the rule stands in one judgment, via the ratio decidendi (the rules the decision rests on)
  • binding vertical + horizontal: appellate courts bound by their own judgments
  • strict conditions for overruling

→ certainty by method, not by statute.

Three cornerstones

  • Locke (1632–1704): precursor of the Enlightenment; sovereign exists for the good of the subject; right of resistance belongs to the people, not the individual; separate legislative from executive.
  • Montesquieu: three powers, separated but each able to check the others; if even two are in one hand, there is no freedom.
  • Kant (1724–1804): the universal law of freedom (below).
Freedom … insofar as it can coexist with the freedom of every other in accordance with a universal law.Kant → the one innate right of every human being.

Where it began: England

Limiting the ruler's power originates in 17th-century England: with the Bill of Rights 1689 Parliament obtained the right to enact statutes, the "beginning of constitutionalism" Constitutions III

What counts as a constitution? Three texts

Any society in which no provision is made for guaranteeing rights or for the separation of powers has no Constitution.Art. 16, Declaration 1789 → maximal answer: a document is not enough.
German Federal Act, 8 June 1815, Art. 13: "estates constitutions will occur in all German states": the old kind, by estates.
Final Act of Vienna, 15 May 1820, Art. 57: sovereign princes, so the entire authority of the state stays united in the head of state; estates take part only in certain rights.

Three texts, three incompatible meanings of one word.

Suffrage: whose "people"?

Typical restrictions: property, tax, sex, literacy, age, residence. 19th-century representation of "the people" is representation of a fraction of it.

Declaration 1789 — Arts. 1 and 2

  • Art. 1: born and remain free and equal in rights; distinctions only for the common good (not by birth).
  • Art. 2: four natural rights: liberty, property, security, resistance to oppression (an ordinary right, not a last resort).
  • Inspired by the US Declaration of Independence 1776; predecessor: Virginia Bill of Rights, the first catalogue; preamble + 17 articles.

Who was brought in?

  • The rising bourgeoisie drives it; franchise tied to property: not the working class.
  • Constitutionalism = logical continuation of enlightened absolutism: ruling for the subjects becomes ruling through them.
  • "Community of subjects" → "people of the state": feeds nationalism, which in turn drives constitutionalism.

It won huge power by renouncing power: nobody could appeal. Europe in 1803: mostly absolutism, nothing to review. Austria: review only from 1920 = 117 years later.

England: a different path

  • No written constitution. Sources: Acts of Parliament · case law · conventions (binding without being law in the ordinary sense)
  • Principles grown over centuries: constitutional monarchy · parliamentary sovereignty · rule of law

The 17th-century steps

Charles I power concentrated; religious rivalry → monarch vs Parliament
1648–60 civil war → Commonwealth under Cromwell
1660 monarchy restored, with a strong Parliament
1689 Bill of Rights: basis of the modern constitution; "beginning of constitutionalism"

Reached a century and a half before the continent, by fighting rather than drafting.

Comparative summary

  • Constitutions, written or unwritten, are made to limit the monarch, against absolutism.
  • The idea of fundamental rights consolidates in the Enlightenment.
  • Same words in all three models, three different things.

Professionalisation (Sunde)

Lawyers give more and more time to law and must meet special criteria. Effect: an internal view: for the professional, good law = internal coherence; for the layman = justice in the single case. Legal education could be added to the model's institutional structure.

Today: convergence from opposite sides

  • LL.M. programmes worldwide → specialisation
  • judge-centred systems turn to educating lawyers (Germany)
