The definition used in this course — four elements, all needed

Law is a regulatory system which is created by humans for humans and is generally effective, and which may apply organized coercion.Vienna School of Legal Theory — the lecturer's own definition
1

created by humans

= positive law. Strained by the sharia: some norms held to come from God → Topic 2

2

for humans

it regulates human conduct

3

generally effective

by and large in force, not obeyed in every case; a dead letter has ceased to be law

4

may apply organized coercion

organized, not private force; a possibility held in reserve, not a feature of every norm

Two classic answers

Conciliatory: law is a special case of social norms (Raiser).

Five theoretical answers (exercise class)

  • Body of norms set apart by state-organized coercion — Kelsen, Vienna School
  • Actual behaviour: "the prophecies of what the courts will do in fact" — Holmes, legal realists (norm scepticism)
  • Necessary result of history — Historical School
  • Cultural phenomenon — Marxism, policy science, law and economics
  • Instrument for a purpose — sociological jurisprudence, jurisprudence of interests; Weber: an "inner spiritual force" enforced by external power

Four pictures, one problem

Nazi Volksgerichtshof · Eleanor Roosevelt with the UDHR · the Crusades · the Taliban. Each was effective, used organized coercion and called itself law.

Point: if law were defined by justice, most of what legal history studies would not be law. So validity and justice are kept apart.

The breadth of the definition decides the object of study: law = state statutes would exclude customary, religious and tribal law and shrink global history to the European state. ubi societas, ibi ius

Why "European and Global"?

The peculiarities of European legal culture only become visible in comparison. Alone it looks like the natural shape of law; next to Jewish, Islamic and Chinese law it is one development among several.

Global sources paradox: a surplus (bureaucracies, digital data) and a shortage (many cultures left almost nothing) at once.

The task: appropriate the strangeness of the sources without abolishing it.

Aims — Mitteis and Ogris

… allowing its complicated structure to emerge from its simplest basic elements …Heinrich Mitteis (1889–1952) — the constructive aim
… counter the delusion that the development of a legal order will always lead … to a higher degree of perfection.Werner Ogris (1935–2015) — the critical aim

Why it is useful to a lawyer

  • Understanding today's institutions as historical products
  • Critical distance: the "natural" is time-bound, so changeable
  • Comparative capacity: several orders in time → foreign orders in space
  • plus: it teaches you to work with a source

Serving function and own value: "understanding and explanation of past realities" (Wieacker).

Commonest mistake: starting at step 5 — context without saying what the text is.

Historical-critical method: external criticism (genuine? who, when, where; original, copy, forgery) → internal (what it says, why so, what it omits) → interpretation: linguistic (meaning then), systematic, historical, teleological.

Further methods: comparative · statistical · prosopography (common traits of a group, e.g. judges) · praxeology (unconscious practices → social structure) · ethnography (a culture seen from within) · oral history · iconography.

Worked model — May Laws 1868 (RGBl. no. 47): ① statute in the imperial gazette, after the 1867 Basic Law → ② May Laws punched holes in the Concordat of 1855 (abrogated 1870) → ③ key word relative Notzivilehe: civil marriage only as a fallback → ④ banns (Aufgebot), declaration of consent, impediment not recognized by the state → ⑤ ABGB marriage: state law executed by priests as state organs; compulsory civil marriage only in 1939.

The Fertile Crescent — why law starts here

Crescent around the Syrian desert (Egypt to Iraq, parts of Turkey and Iran), watered by the Nile, Euphrates, Tigris; term by Breasted (1916). Law is visible only once written: hieroglyphics and cuneiform, both before 3000 BC.

① irrigation → coordination
② dense cities of strangers
③ long-distance trade
④ writing (cuneiform)

Already the Neolithic: property implies law. Also the home of the three monotheistic religions.

The Code of Hammurabi (c. 1750 BC)

If a man put out the eye of another man, his eye shall be put out.Code of Hammurabi § 196 — the talion
  • basalt stele in cuneiform, found 1901 at Susa (Elamite war booty), now in the Louvre
  • prologue + c. 282 propositions + epilogue; casuistic if — then
  • relief: Shamash hands the king the insignia → divine legitimation, secular text
  • Talion only between equals: status decides — awīlum (free), muškēnum (dependant), wardum (slave) → for a slave, compensation not retaliation
  • talion reappears as a guiding principle in Judaism and Christianity
Disputed nature: statute? record of custom? model decisions? royal self-presentation? Parties practically never invoke it in court records. Name the possibilities, assert none.

Oldest "international law"

Stele of the Vultures (Eannatum of Lagash, c. 2460 BC, Louvre): border dispute with Umma over the field Gu-edin → battle → Umma's ruler swears never to cross again, on pain of punishment = oath + sanction: the elements of a treaty.

Also the peace treaty Ebla – Abarsal (3rd millennium BC).

Biblical law: Torah (10th/6th c. BC): precepts between people / between man and God; oral Torah → Mishnah → Jerusalem (c. 400) and Babylonian Talmud (c. 500). Parallels ≠ proof of borrowing → Topic 5

Greece — no single "Greek law"

  • Persians: largest empire yet; failed against Greece 490 and 479 BC
  • Greece = many poleis, each with its own law; well studied only Athens and Gortyn
  • Law of Gortyn (5th c. BC): slave goes to a free woman → children free; free woman goes to a slave → children slaves
  • law was part of rhetoric, not a discipline; no legal scholarship
  • Hellenism (after Alexander): cultural fusion, no uniform legal order

What Greece left: legal philosophy

  • Sophists (5th c. BC) doubt that law is god-given; Protagoras: man is "the measure of all things" (parallel with the Enlightenment)
  • → nomos (recorded, later "positive") vs physis (valid by nature)
  • Cicero: lex est ratio summa insita in natura → a statute against natural law does not bind → Topic 8

Division did not break legal unity: the Law of Citations was issued by both emperors. After 476 legal production lives on only in the East.

Church and empire

  • NT from c. 60 AD, with law (indivisible marriage)
  • councils 431, 451 exclude Copts, Nestorians → eased the 7th-c. Muslim takeover
  • later Latin/Greek split: political, not theological

Who made Roman law? Jurists

  • primarily jurisprudential law; statutes only supplement it
  • opinions to the praetor = responsa; to parties = consilia; praetors developed the law on them
  • the praetor was a politician, not a jurist
  • first jurists priests, then wealthy Romans, unpaid: respondere, cavere, agere, scribere; later the ius publice respondendi
  • Cicero = advocate, not a jurist
  • schools: Sabinians vs Proculians; later law schools of Berytus and Constantinople

"Did the Romans invent law?" — No, but…

Law is older (Ur-Nammu, Hammurabi). What Rome created is legal scholarship (iurisprudentia): experts who expound law systematically, build concepts, write literature. Elsewhere there were norms, in Rome a discipline.

Plus: a normative system of civil law, the basis of continental legal culture; first comprehensive rules on slavery.

Trap — Rome's extent: Britain was Roman yet has common law → reception decided, where graduates went. Long Roman rule left vulgar law, so reception fell on prepared ground (Italy, S France, Hispania).

Features of early Islamic law

  • religious origin: God's will, unalterable by humans
  • scholarly, not state: ulema make it; the caliph executes
  • casuistic and discursive: disagreement (ikhtilaf) recorded
  • law not separated from religion and ethics
  • took over pre-Islamic custom and Byzantine/Sasanian practice
  • systematised in the 8th–9th c. (schools, al-Shafiʿi)

Sunni and Shia — a dispute over succession

who succeeds the Prophet?
Sunni: caliphs — Umayyad, Abbasid, Ottoman. The caliph rules: spiritual and temporal head in one.
Shia: imams from ʿAli to the 12th, Muhammad al-Mahdi, the hidden imam — an imam who is awaited.

Contrast the West's two swords → Topic 3

Is the sharia "law" on our definition?

  • created by humans? some norms come directly from God
  • organized coercion? many norms are religious or moral duties

→ a legal and moral order ("the way to the watering place"); fiqh must separate legal from moral norms.

Sharia = ideal divine order · fiqh = human, fallible understanding of it. Knows people↔people vs man↔God rules (non-Muslims bound only by the first); knows no public/private divide.

Core thesis: the fiqh is a jurists' law (8th–19th c.) made by scholars, not by political lawgivers — a historical form of interpreting the shariʿa, so its rules can be replaced.

Johansen I — how a jurists' law works

jurists: our reasoning is for trained jurists only
➜
non-jurists, even theologians, excluded from ijmāʿ
➜
who counts in the consensus makes law
  • Abbasid empirewide judiciary (chief justice appoints qadis) needed universal norms → schools spread because appointments did; urban elites competed for the qadi's office
  • Dissent is structural: protected subjects — Hanafis admit all but Arab polytheists, Malikis even them, Shafiʿis/Hanbalis only "people of the book"; khilāf a genre from the 10th c.
  • Schacht: "the sum total of the personal privileges of all individuals"

Genres: how law changes without a legislator

  • mutūn: school texts, learnt by heart, almost unchanged = fixed core
  • shurūḥ, ḥawāshī: commentaries and glosses = moving edge (new rules, classical vs later jurists)
  • fatwa: mufti must pick one opinion; does not check facts (qadi's task); lenient guidance only orally
  • ḥiyal (legal devices) · shurūṭ (notaries' forms)
  • qawāʿid: principles by induction from norms, vs usul al-fiqh deducing from revelation; furūq explain differences

Shariʿa ≠ fiqh — and the modern payoff

815–820 Shafiʿi's Risala: first to separate legal method from material
late 10th c. genre usul al-fiqh ("roots"); fiqh = "branches"
late 19th c. Western codes replace fiqh rules; constitutions install the political lawgiver
since 1970s "principles of the shariʿa" a main source of legislation (Kuwait 1962, Egypt 1971/1980 …; Afghanistan 2004 Art. 3)

They cite principles, never fiqh rules.

Two forums, two kinds of claim

  • qadi decides only wājib / ḥarām; the rest directs the conscience; ibāḥa accepted last (legalised custom)
  • ẓāhir (judgment) vs bāṭin ("between the believer and his Lord"): a judgment does not make the illicit licit
  • Hanafi: farḍ (obey + believe) vs wājib (obey)
  • ḥuqūq Allāh vs ḥuqūq al-nās: the needy person's claim may prevail over God's — God does not need his claims fulfilled

Punishment

  • ḥudūd (fixed, claims of God) and qiṣāṣ (talio = family's private right: execute, settle, renounce)
  • al-ḥudūd tudraʾu bi-l-shubuhāt: doubt bars them — subjective, objective, procedural
  • taʿzīr: no fixed crime/penalty, outside the doubt rule → grew to death sentences by the 19th c.

Weiss — sources of knowledge

  • shariʿa is more than law: ethics + ritual; it also advises, recommends, disapproves; infractions are sin
  • only God is the source → the jurists' question is where to know his law
Qurʾan, Sunnamaterial sources
analogya method
ijmāʿepistemic: turns opinion into certainty ("my community will never concur on an error")

ijtihād: humans construct God's law → if jurists differ, all but one err; saved by "difference of opinion is a mercy from God".

Motzki — is the Sunna authentic?

  • Sunna ranks 2nd: content divine, wording human
  • authentic hadith: unbroken (muttaṣil) + recurrent (mutawātir) → certainty; most are solitary (āḥād) → probability: usable for practice, not belief
  • Western doubt: prophetic hadiths appear late in 8th-c. debates; the common link = fabricator, or just a collector?
  • an early date ≠ a saying of the Prophet — open question

Charlemagne and the two-emperors problem

The Pope crowns Charlemagne Emperor (800) → an emperor in the West and in Byzantium, each claiming to be the Roman emperor. A universal claim tolerates only one bearer, so the dispute is insoluble.

Society: status decides the law

  • under the king's special protection (Sachsenspiegel): monks, priests, women, Jews
  • 90 % peasants, subsistence; rule over land = rule over people (land and sovereignty not yet separated); the lord nearby counts more than the distant king

Legal circles — the narrower wins

Territorial rule replaces tribal association (Austria 1156). In one territory several laws apply at once:

imperial law
land law
municipal law
lex familiae (Hofrecht) — the manor

Rule: the narrower legal circle takes precedence over the wider. Result: fragmentation — each law valid in its circle, the closest one wins.

Towns — the driving force

  • towns free themselves from their lord: privileges or own statutes
  • municipal law is far more dynamic than the lex familiae: long-distance trade, contracts with craftsmen, credit
  • law families (from 10th c., German settlers): a new town adopts its mother town's law and appeals to it — Magdeburg and Lübeck
  • law spreads by adoption, not conquest or legislation

The many city statutes beside Roman law later force the statute theory → commentators

Law books: mirrors, not statutes

  • law book = private recording of customary law; some acquired law-like effect
  • Sachsenspiegel (c. 1225; 1220–35) by Eike von Repgow — educated at a monastery school, not a university → native law, not learned Roman law
  • oldest law book in German; Landrecht + Lehnrecht; hundreds of manuscripts, Netherlands to Ukraine, partly in use into the 20th c.
  • Schwabenspiegel

Trap: "Is the Sachsenspiegel a statute?" — No: Spiegel = mirror.

Registers of transactions = precursor of the land register, unknown in Roman law — a genuinely medieval invention. Domesday Book (1086, England) · Vienna's Eisenbuch (14th–18th c.).

Education: seven liberal arts

Taught first in monastic and cathedral schools. The trivium trains close reading and spotting contradictions — what scholasticism then does to legal texts.

The university = a corporation

universitas magistrorum et scholarium: a community of teachers and students, a legal person with privileges, own jurisdiction, autonomy (not under abbot or bishop) — until Maria Theresa made them state institutions (c. 1750). Latin everywhere → a European scholarly space.

Four faculties, two laws

Arts · theology · law · medicine. The law faculty teaches canonistics (church law) and legistics (secular, i.e. Roman law) → doctor iuris utriusque.

Canonistics — church law

A canon = a rule; applies partly to clerics only, partly to laymen. Sources: Bible, councils and synods, papal decretals.

What the reception was — and was not

Reception = transfer of the results of legistics from the universities into legal practice; pan-European, from the 13th c., peak 15th–16th c. Not a direct transfer of a text but the gradual academisation of practice: graduates of Pavia and Bologna fill the judiciary, administration and politics — nobody decreed it.

Theoretical (taught) vs practical (courts decide by it) · in complexu vs single institutions. Carriers: learned jurists, church courts, the idea that Roman law is the emperor's law.

Doubt about custom is the doorway. Conring (1643): doctors of Roman law consulted more and more since the 15th c.

Ius commune — rank on paper and in practice

  • pan-European learned law (gemeines Recht); universal: tied to no territory or group
  • formally subsidiary: statutum derogat iuri communi — applies where no conflicting local custom is proved (a burden of proof, not a gap rule)
  • in practice it displaced local law — the default position; it also gave local law its language and concepts

Legislation: law reforms

The ius commune is the cause, means and end of early modern legislation.

  • municipal: Nuremberg 1479, Worms 1498, Ptuj 1513, Freiburg 1520 · land: Bavaria 1518, Württemberg 1555
  • Austria the other way: Tyrol 1526 keeps domestic law; land-law drafts 1526–1654 never in force

Policey and the Carolina

  • Policey ≠ police, ≠ policy: precursor of administrative law — epidemics (Vienna infection measures from 1541), building and fire rules, begging, prostitution; ordinances often carry private law too
  • Constitutio Criminalis Carolina 1532: procedure for capital crimes; unifies and modernises, full of learned doctrine — but only subsidiary behind land law
