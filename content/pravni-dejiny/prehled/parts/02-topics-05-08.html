'Jewish law' = the modern English word for halakha: one system for ritual, property, family, commerce, crime, courts and taxes. Jurisdiction personal, not territorial. Key sentence: before emancipation, being Jewish meant living according to Jewish law.

The lecture asks three questions, each from the Jewish and the non-Jewish side. Jewish courts used non-Jewish law and Christian courts applied Jewish law: the mixed cells are the point. This guards against two mistakes: imagining full Jewish autonomy, or a neat separation of the two orders.

Why the method pays off

It shows Jewish law's historicity and 'secular' parts, and raises two questions for all legal history: the role of the state, and duties vs rights.

"…it is necessary to allow them to live by these laws and to be judged according to them."Dohm, On the Civil Improvement of the Jews, 1781 — 'ninth point'

What to say about Dohm

  • Equal rights require legal autonomy rather than ruling it out.
  • Jewish law (written and oral, plus authoritative commentary) = a particular law, like a town's statutes.
  • Evidence: Rome and recent states — autonomy did no harm.

Two sharp contrasts

  • The Talmud: rejected by Christian theology, valid in law.
  • Pütter: a real state may exist without its own private law — the source of autonomy and custom.

What Jewish law teaches legal theory

  1. non-state law (law without a state)
  2. duty-based, not rights-based — Beck: Jews were denied the officia humanitatis
  3. more than religious law: its 'secular' parts are private law
  4. a barometer: Takkanot ≈ police ordinances = the same bureaucratization as the states
  5. a part of a mixed jurisdiction in the Empire

How the king's peace grew

'From an occasional privilege into a common right.' The 19th-c. account is now contested as a legend, but worth telling.

Why? The crown is responsible for granting peace. Real aim: limit the lords, reach the vassals directly, master the land.

ROYAL COURTS — beside, not above · no supervisory powers · a very thin web: rarely more than 15 judges until the late 18th c.
  • Royal justice grew by being chosen, not imposed. The unfree could not plead before the king.
  • Why choose it? It could compel defendants and jurors; judgments were kept in rolls in the Tower; it looked impersonal and rule-bound.

Two terms to keep apart

Multinormativity: several normativities regulate one issue. Legal pluralism: overlapping norms in one territory, all felt to be binding. England has both.

The writ: from order to lawsuit

① Administrative order: Latin parchment under the king's seal, issued ex parte; repeats the plaintiff's story → easily abused.
⇣
② 12th c. — the revolution: the wrongdoer may choose: comply, or appear and explain why not → the writ now starts litigation.
⇣
③ Fixed formulas: the chancery records writs; a public list (praecipe quod reddat, novel disseisin). A favour becomes routine.
⇣
④ 13th c. pleading: formal, pre-set positions until it is clear what is agreed and what contested.
⇣
⑤ Trial of the facts by oath, ordeal or jury; the official now acts as a judge.

Denying a writ = no royal court (others stay open). Writs grew from private interests, not the public good.

England did have Roman law

Roman law taught at Canterbury (7th c.); the Quadripartitus (c. 1108–18) follows Roman structure; Lanfranc at court; Vacarius' Liber pauperum. Until the 11th c. England looked like the rest of Europe; canon law is 'foreign' only after the Reformation.

The legal professions

Procurators, barristers (who plead), attorneys (who counsel). Four Inns of Court + an Inn of Chancery: mock trials, moots, readings on statutes. Courts spoke French, wrote Latin. → Topic 12

Royal legislation

Assize of Clarendon 1166 (accusation by a jury of 12; crime = royal jurisdiction); Edward I: jury compulsory in criminal cases; Statute of Uses 1536, of Wills 1540, of Frauds 1677. Herzog: are 'marginal' statutes a 17th-c. distortion?

Remedies create rights (ubi remedium, ibi ius)

In theory the king gave only forum and procedure. In practice, every writ granted recognised a right; every writ denied suppressed one.

Mort d'ancestor
→ right to inherit land
Clarendon 1166
→ no charge without official indictment (jury of 12)
Habeas corpus
→ no imprisonment without cause
"…a vain thing to imagine a right without a remedy, for want of right and want of remedy are reciprocal."House of Lords, 1704 — reversing the Queen's Bench: a man denied his vote needs a remedy even though his candidate won

Procedure, not material justice

  • The question was not 'what is the right result?' but 'was the right procedure followed?' The judge polices the script of pleading.
  • Yearbooks (13th c., often by law students) record dialogues, not rules — how facts become legal arguments.
  • To compete, royal courts promised one procedure regardless of who the parties are → what we call due process.
  • Hence almost no appeals: writs of error only for procedural errors visible in the record.

Why did England drift from Roman law? Three answers

  • Lecture: 14th-c. appointment policy — the writ system needs practical skill, so judges come from the advocates at the king's court, not universities ('obedient servants').
  • Exercise class: the reception came too late — England already had royal courts, its own law and profession.
  • Herzog: divergence only gradual (14th–15th c.) + the jury. Roman law stayed: Vacarius, Glanvill, 'Bracton' (built like the Corpus Iuris), church courts, admiralty, equity.
Calvin's Case 1608. A Scot born after James's accession (1603) may inherit English land: allegiance is owed to the person of James, not to a crown. The case was fabricated: Parliament had refused, so a plaintiff needing a remedy had to be found.

Herzog: was England exceptional?

Traditional thesis: ius commune could not get in, because England already had a modern, centralised, efficient system.
  1. No such system existed in the Middle Ages: royal, feudal and local courts, rules of mixed origin, few judges, few cases.
  2. Built by a French dynasty with Roman and canon jurists and ius commune methods (Roman jurists still pleading in the 15th c.).
  3. Same aim everywhere — royal power over the lords, justified by peace. Only the means differed: own courts vs the ius commune.
  4. Real divergence: gradual, 14th–15th c., and the jury. Serious parting only in the 16th–17th c., when lawyers re-invented the common law against the king.

lecture: Foljanty · reading: Duchhardt (2012)
+ Olechowski paras 3101–08, 3201–06, 3208–09

The chain of the unit: religious war → sovereignty → absolutism → enlightened absolutism, with public law as its commentary. The religious conflicts of the 16th–17th centuries are the propeller of modern statehood.

1517

Luther's 95 theses (Wittenberg). A split the legal order cannot absorb.

➜

Protestant princes rule the regional church; church property taken over; conflicts between estates → solved by imperial legislation.

➜

cuius regio, eius religio — the ruler decides the territory's faith: first step to a system of sovereign states.

➜

Wars go on

French civil war; estates vs Emperor; 1618–1648 Thirty Years' War (1620 White Mountain); heavy population loss.

➜

1648 → 1654

Peace of Westphalia; 1654 adopted among the leges fundamentales = constitutional law of the Empire.

The Peace does two different things — keep them apart

PEACE OF WESTPHALIA 1648 · Osnabrück (Empire–Sweden) + Münster (Empire–France) — two towns, because Protestants and Catholics would not sit at one table
① Re-orders EUROPE
  • Switzerland and the Northern Netherlands leave the Empire
  • Spain weakened (Portugal and N. Netherlands independent of Spain since 1640)
  • France and Sweden rise — and are the guarantors of the peace
  • France overtakes Spain → the 'French era' begins
② Re-orders the EMPIRE
  • three confessions equal (Catholic, Lutheran, Calvinist); cuius regio confirmed, reference year 1624
  • Emperor's core powers — legislation, war and peace, alliances, supreme jurisdiction — need the Reichstag's consent
  • estates split into confessional corps
  • sovereignty (Landeshoheit) and ius foederis for the princes

Direction: power moves downwards. The Empire does not become a state; it becomes a frame inside which its members do. The Empire itself was never absolutist — the Emperor–estates dualism remained.

"…the free exercise of the sovereignty of the land…" · alliances allowed, "not directed against the Emperor and the Empire".Peace of Osnabrück 1648, Art. VIII §§ 1–2 ('die freie Ausübung der Landeshoheit')

What to say about Art. VIII

  • § 1: ancient rights + territorial sovereignty, never to be disturbed.
  • § 2: alliances, even with foreign powers — an attribute of sovereignty…
  • …but not against Emperor, Empire or treaty, and the oath to the Emperor stays. Sovereign and still a member.

Is 1648 the start of modern international law?

  • 'Widely seen' so: first attempt to codify international law; new standards for negotiations (actors, mediation, documents, diplomacy); territorial sovereignty.
  • Duchhardt's doubts: no European security system (as Richelieu wanted); did not end the Franco-Spanish war (only the Pyrenees 1659). A 'Westphalian system' remains open.
  • Still the key legal text for relations in central Europe and the reference point of all peace treaties until the 1790s.

Two points from the exercise class

  • Paradox: Catholic France fought on the Protestant side while persecuting Protestants at home → a war of power, not doctrine.
  • Westphalian sovereignty = a shield against outside influence, less power inwards.

'Constitution' before 1787 — a trap

Absolutism — definition and where it worked

The monarch rules without the estates: supreme legislator, judge and official in one person ('detached', absolutus). Legitimised by Bodin and Hobbes.

Ius publicum and Policey: from 'finding' to 'making' law

Public law becomes a discipline in the 17th c. out of three pressures: local Policey ordinances and law reformations (15th c.); imperial reforms (1495, 1519–1555, police ordinances 1530, 1548, 1577); conflicts of the schism. Born out of an argument nobody could settle; with sovereignty it turns into state law. Policey = good order of the community, not today's police.

Natural law III — the law of reason and the codes

from duties to rights;
from a system of reason to a statute book

The law of reason (Vernunftrecht)

  • Rationalism and the sciences (late 17th c.): law as a system derived from nature — the mos geometricus; a complete picture in which the general precedes the particular (the old rule 'the narrower circle wins' is overturned).
  • Kiel 1665: first chair of natural law (Rachel); then Pufendorf at Lund. De officio hominis et civis (1673): nearly 100 editions in a century, 7 languages.
  • Pufendorf's method from mathematics: 21 definitions, 2 axioms, 5 observations (teacher Weigel). Duties to God, neighbour, self.
  • Thomasius ('father of the German Enlightenment') cuts the bond with Roman law: natural law is only an ideal model, not binding. Wolff (Halle) 8 volumes.
  • Martini 1797: natural laws are 'like mathematical truths… mathematically certain'.
  • Why lawyers liked it: a rational structure, apparent objectivity — and Pufendorf's rules overlap with Roman law.

Common features, Grotius → Wolff

God gives natural law its binding force, but not its content; content = human reason from a few self-evident premises; moral truths true in themselves, like scientific ones; focus moves from the nature of natural law to its concrete content.

The shift at the end of the 18th c.

Grotius and Pufendorf wrote about the duties natural law creates; now attention turns to the individual rights it creates → the individualist spirit of 1789. The same doctrine had served absolutism — it could be shaped.

Ibbetson's test case: mandate. Pufendorf: do no harm + keep your agreements. How much care does an unpaid agent owe? Roman law: the highest (exactissima diligentia). Pufendorf: the care he shows in his own affairs — a mere assertion, derived from neither principle. Others concluded differently (Barbeyrac).

The verdict

The steps were plausible, not deductions; a reader could disagree at almost any point. 'The appearance of objectivity was wholly superficial.' Vaughan (1670) called the law of nature the pons stultorum — 'when fools know not their way, they go that way'.

Two ways round the problem

  • Domat, Les loix civiles (1693): Roman law as it stands, only re-arranged by its rational basis — natural law gives structure, not content (Pothier arranged it differently).
  • Montesquieu: no fixed law for all nations; law must fit the constitution, history, society, geography, climate — a variable natural law. That let it into the absolutist monarchies: Catherine II, Instructions 1767. Exercise class: from the 18th c. natural law may 'take different forms by time and place'.

England: atypical

No abstract framework, no codification → natural law laid over existing law. Coke equates the common law with the law of nature. Pufendorf in English translation shapes contract (Ballow 1737), negligence (Buller 1767) and the trust; Pothier from the 1780s. Blackstone (1765–69) is systematic, unlike Coke. Deepest effect: rules gain a moral justification. → Topic 6

Reading Arts 1 and 2

  • Art. 2: liberty, property, security, resistance to oppression (an ordinary natural right).

Constitutionalism (from 1789)

Ruler → people; subject → citizen; unlimited → divided power. Continues enlightened absolutism: for the people becomes through them — the bourgeoisie, not the workers.

"A society in which the guarantee of rights is not secured, nor the separation of powers determined, has no constitution."Declaration 1789, Art. 16

What to say about Art. 16

  • Locke in the first half (government secures natural rights; power as a trust), Montesquieu in the second (separation of powers).
  • Two conditions, not wishes: the descriptive 'constitution' → Topic 7 becomes normative.
  • Modern constitution: written, by the people, above statutes. → Topics 9–11
