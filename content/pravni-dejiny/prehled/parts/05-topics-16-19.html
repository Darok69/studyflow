For Aristotle democracy is the degenerate form of rule by many. The modern positive sense is a later reversal. → Topic 2

Who was actually included?

  • Greek polis: a small number of men who could gather in one place (the agora) and decide directly.
  • Excluded: slaves, foreigners (métoikoi), women.
  • Irony: Aristotle himself was a métoikos in Athens: the philosopher of the constitution had no vote in it.
  • Early modern cities: power with a small minority, mainly merchants.

The critique of representation

Rousseau: popular sovereignty; only direct democracy is conceivable. His target is the free mandate.
"The people of England … is free only during the election of members of parliament. As soon as they are elected, slavery overtakes it."Rousseau, Du contrat social (1762)
⇣
Lenin 1918: choosing every few years "which member of the ruling class should oppress and trample on the people in parliament" = the same argument, from the opposite end of the spectrum.
⇣
Soviet system = bound mandate: a stronger link to the voters, but deputies become mere messengers → debate in parliament is pointless, parliament loses significance → Topic 18
Free vs bound mandate: free = the deputy follows no instructions of the voters. Bound = he must vote as instructed, so nobody can be persuaded and there is nothing to debate.

Representation as a necessity

Institutes I.2.5: the Roman people had grown so large that it was difficult to assemble it → the senate was consulted instead of the people (senatusconsultum). Representation = a solution to scale, not a principle.

"In a democracy, the people meet and exercise the government in person; in a republic, they assemble and administer it by their representatives and agents."Federalist Papers (Hamilton, Madison, Jay)

For the Federalists "democracy" = direct (confined to a small spot); "republic" = what we call representative democracy (it can extend over a large region).

Kelsen's defence of democracy

tat tvam asi "that's you there"
➜
altruism, pacifism, relativism
➜
nobody knows better → the only option is to vote
  • The advantage is not finding the truth: power can change after every election, and government and opposition change peacefully.
  • Parliamentarism is "nothing more and nothing less than the result of a society based on the division of labour" → the vote must be representative.

Scale: isonomy → democracy → electoral autocracy. "Not everything that calls itself democracy deserves that name": elections are one component, not the whole.

Why it belongs to legal history

  • At Nuremberg international law still focused on relations between states; Nazi law had abolished the prohibition of retroactivity, and Nuremberg had to answer that objection → Topic 18
  • Hague law, Versailles Art 227 and the League as precursors → Topic 15
  • Amnesty clauses in peace treaties since Westphalia 1648 (Kant: part of making peace) → Topic 7

Tadić (ICTY): praised and criticised by Kreß

  • landmark (2 Oct 1995): customary war crimes exist in non-international conflicts (Common Art 3, Protocol II: no grave-breaches regime).
  • too hasty: derived individual responsibility from a treaty, against the Secretary-General's report (only rules "beyond any doubt part of customary law").

Ad hoc tribunals: the reproach

ICTY and ICTR were set up partly ex post facto by the Security Council, a political organ with selective membership, as its subsidiary organs. Still decisive: they made crimes against humanity a distinct category, inside or outside armed conflict, and cleared the way for the second generation.

International criminal law — Kreß's map of the field

the whole article is built on the four senses
of one ambiguous term
"INTERNATIONAL CRIMINAL LAW": four senses
① Jurisdiction of states
territoriality (uncontested), personality, protective, vicarious, universal jurisdiction
② Cooperation
extradition, transit, mutual assistance … horizontal (state–state) vs vertical (state–court)
③ Transnational criminal law
cross-border crimes by treaty: aut dedere aut iudicare
④ ICL stricto sensu
individual criminal responsibility directly under international law; the ius puniendi of the international community

The core crimes (Rome Statute)

crimeessence
genocide (Art 6)intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group (from Genocide Conv. 1948); in practice a collective campaign
crimes against humanity (Art 7)first codification; act as part of a widespread or systematic attack on a civilian population, with knowledge; inside or outside war; needs a state or organisational policy
war crimes (Art 8)the oldest, yet disputed until 1945; Geneva law (persons hors de combat) + Hague law (conduct of hostilities); since Tadić also in non-international conflict
aggression"supreme international crime", yet undefined, so the ICC cannot exercise jurisdiction (Kreß 2009): a historical irony; the Security Council's role = "the question of questions"

Features of ICL stricto sensu

  • only human beings are liable: no legal persons, no state
  • ius cogens; overrides national law and immunity ratione materiae, because it targets state-led macrocriminality
  • enforced directly (international courts) or indirectly (national courts as trustees)
  • in conflict, Kompetenz-Kompetenz stays with the international court
  • not a "law of leaders", except for aggression

The ICC

  • complementarity: acts only if no state is willing or able genuinely; decides conclusively
  • nationals or territory of a state party; universal on SC referral
  • outside: China, India, Russia, USA
  • cooperation = weakest link: without states "utterly powerless" (Cassese)

Legality and the general part

  • Nuremberg to ICTR: crimes only as titles of jurisdiction, details from custom
  • Rome: lex scripta (Arts 6–8, Art 22, Elements of Crimes)
  • Part 3: first general part; guilt over prevention: duress allowed, unlike McDonald/Vohrah in Erdemović (1997)

Objections

  • Hegel/Jakobs: criminal law in the state of nature, creating order by coercion
  • Kant (1795): a world coercive power; Köhler, Gierhake on its scope
  • Rawls/Hoyer: only leaders; Kreß: goes too far (individual between Scylla and Charybdis)

Exam: Topic 19 — ask

  • Austria 1933–38: legal basis of each step; authoritarian or fascist (both sides)?
  • How was the Anschluss made "legal"?
  • Nuremberg: charges, the two legal problems, why a breakthrough.
  • Kreß: four senses; generations; complementarity.

Procedure at the ICC

  • ICTY/ICTR adversarial; ICC: compromises of all legal families
  • prosecutor must investigate incriminating and exonerating facts: a seeker of truth
  • pre-trial chamber: between Ermittlungsrichter and juge d'instruction
  • victims: right to participate and to reparations

Amnesties

  • since the Westphalian treaties 1648 amnesty clauses in peace treaties; Kant: part of making peace
  • pardons after both world wars: trials tend to end as de facto amnesties
  • Rome preamble: duty of every state to prosecute; yet the Statute does not expressly forbid a national amnesty

Transnational ≠ international crimes

  • treaties up to 1970 protect parallel state interests (then the "Hague model", 1970)
  • real test: do states internationalise the norm? Only grave breaches of Geneva I–IV crossed it
  • Piracy: universal jurisdiction, though only as grave as robbery: no territorial sovereign
